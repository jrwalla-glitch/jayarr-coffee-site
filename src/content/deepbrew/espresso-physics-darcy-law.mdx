## Darcy's law in the portafilter

In 1856 Henry Darcy described how water flows through a bed of sand, and the same law is the starting point for any packed bed, including a coffee puck:

<p class="eq">Q = k·A·ΔP / (μ·L)</p>

In words: the flow Q (milliliters per second through the puck) rises with the bed's permeability k, the area A of the puck face and the pressure drop ΔP across it, and falls with the liquid's viscosity μ and the depth of the bed L. Permeability is the property of the bed itself: how easily its pores let liquid through, which depends on the sizes, shapes and roughness of the particles and the gaps between them.

Two of the terms are easy to pin down. A 58 mm basket has a face of about 2,640 mm² (calculated: π × 29² mm²). The pressure drop is what the machine applies above the puck, minus atmospheric pressure below it. The other three are the hard part, because in a real shot none of them holds still.

## Why flow rises with the square root of pressure

Read literally, Darcy's law says that doubling the pressure doubles the flow. Gagné explains why espresso doesn't behave that way: the law assumes a fixed, immovable bed, and an espresso puck is neither. As pressure rises, the puck compresses and gets shallower, which on its own would speed the flow. But the particles are also pushed closer together, so the pores between them shrink, and that slows the flow more. Some fine particles also break loose and move; Gagné notes they can raise the flow when they come free and lower it again if they lodge farther down.

Users of the Decent machine noticed the combined effect in their logged shots, and Gagné summarizes it as a rule of thumb:

<p class="eq">Q ∝ √ΔP</p>

That reads: below about 10 bar, the drip rate grows roughly with the square root of the pressure. Doubling the pressure therefore raises the flow by about 41%, not 100% (calculated: √2 ≈ 1.41). Turned around, a bed twice as resistant needs about four times the pressure to hold the same flow, which is why a flow-controlled shot's pressure reacts so sharply to small changes in grind or puck prep.

Above about 10 bar the rule breaks down: the drip rate becomes very small even at high pressure and shots can stall. John Buckman at Decent calls this "secondary puck compression", and Gagné suggests it *may* come from the cellulose of the particles deforming and closing the remaining pores. Neither presents it as measured.

None of this comes from the water. Water barely compresses at espresso pressures: Gagné notes it takes about 220 bar to compress it by 1%. So when more water goes in before the first drops appear, something else is giving way, and it's the puck. Transparent-portafilter videos he cites show the puck squeezing like a spring under pump pressure and relaxing when the pressure comes off.

Rao reaches the same physics from the barista's side. In *Espresso Extraction* (chapter 3) he writes that as pump pressure rises, flow through the bed rises too, until somewhere between 7 and 9 bar at the group, beyond which "additional compression inhibits flow more than the additional pressure promotes flow." He recommends running a non-profiling machine at whichever pressure gives the most flow, because that allows the finest grind, and he notes that most machine gauges read pump pressure, which is a little higher than the pressure at the group. His worked example of the test is labeled hypothetical, so it isn't data about any one machine.

## Puck resistance, the number Gagné tracks

To compare shots despite the square-root behavior, Gagné characterizes the puck by its hydraulic resistance:

<p class="eq">R = √ΔP / Q</p>

That is, resistance is the square root of the pressure drop divided by the flow. A finer grind, a denser bed or a clog raises R; a channel lowers it. He corrected this definition himself: his January 2021 post first gave it upside down, and a February edit fixed it. His posts also describe the DE1's own resistance curve in two different ways (flow squared over pressure in January, pressure over flow squared in April). Only the second is the square of R as defined here; the first is its inverse. The numbers quoted in this note are for R itself, as he reports them.

## What happens to resistance during a shot

In his January 2021 study, Gagné pulled 24 shots of one washed Honduran coffee: 18 g doses (all between 17.8 and 18.0 g) ground on a Niche Zero at setting 14.0, with three dial-in shots before he started. He used the DE1's "Best Pressure Profile": a preinfusion at about 3.5 ml per second until the pressure reaches 4 bar, then a jump to about 8.4 bar, easing to about 5 bar by 35 seconds. He alternated between puck prep methods so that warm-up or fatigue wouldn't favor any one of them.

The logged shots followed a consistent pattern:

- **The first drops appeared at 11–12 seconds.** The flow measured into the cup caught up with the flow entering the puck at about 17 seconds, which he reads as the moment the puck is mostly saturated.
- **Resistance climbed at the end of preinfusion,** which he attributes to the puck compressing, then fell by a factor of about 2 to 3.5 and settled.
- **Thinner liquid can't be the whole explanation for that fall.** Viscosity data from Sobolík and colleagues (2002), which he plots, suggest the change from strong to weak extract should shift resistance by about 50%, not a factor of 2 or more.
- **A 10-second pause after preinfusion "completely removes" the peak.** Nothing should thin out or migrate during a pause without flow, so he concluded the likeliest cause was a puck still partly dry when full pressure arrived.

Later work shifted the picture. In his May 2021 comparison he used a 30-second bloom, so the puck was fully wet before extraction, and the resistance still fell during the shot. This time he judged a drop in viscosity, as the liquid leaving the puck gets weaker, the most likely cause. He also said he was "very skeptical" of the common idea that the puck falls apart or develops many channels late in the shot, because longer shots pulled out to 1:11 showed no further loss of resistance. Rao, in chapter 11 of his book, describes the same late decline at the group as the bed eroding.

Independent imaging supports the wetting part of the story. Foster and colleagues (2025) followed water moving into an initially dry espresso bed with time-resolved X-ray micro-CT, tracking the wetting front as it advanced, and matched it with a model that splits the bed into wet and dry regions.

## Laminar flow, with one exception

Is the flow inside the puck turbulent? Gagné says no. Citing rule-of-thumb calculations and the simulations of Ellero and Navarini (2019), he puts the microscopic Reynolds number inside the puck at 2–12, far from the thousands needed for turbulence. That rules out the Darcy–Weisbach equation, which is built for turbulent friction, as a model for espresso, "perhaps unless a gigantic channel happens", as he puts it. In other words, laminar flow is the rule inside a sound puck, and a large channel is the possible exception.

Laminar flow doesn't mean the puck is easy to model, though. In geophysics, an empirical rule for soils says a bed's permeability goes with the square of D10, the particle size below which the finest 10% of the material falls. Gagné tested that rule in September 2023 against about 300 particle size distributions from 24 espresso grinders, gathered by the Kaffeemacher team with Marco Wellinger's lab at ZHAW (Zurich University of Applied Sciences). If the rule held, every grinder's dialed-in espresso grind should have landed at about the same predicted resistance. They didn't. He offered three possible reasons: different grinds release solids, oils and CO2 at different rates and so change the liquid's viscosity; espresso pressures compress and rearrange the bed in ways soils rarely see; and coffee particles may simply have unusual shapes or surfaces, which he thought least likely.

What the same data did show is that fines dominate dialing in. In his words, dialing in espresso means "dialling in the amount fines" more than the size of the coarser particles. Grinders that make fewer fines must be set much finer to reach the same flow, and, oddly, they end up with slightly more fines per gram at dial-in than grinders that make more, an effect he calls "over-shooting" and couldn't fully explain. His 2021 low-fines comparison fits: the EG-1 with ultra-low-fines burrs had to grind far finer than the Niche Zero for the same resistance, and the shots extracted more. So fines largely set the resistance, but a formula built on D10 alone doesn't predict it.

## Channeling, distribution and the paper filter

Water takes the path of least resistance. Wherever the bed is looser, through a gap at the wall or a low-density patch, flow speeds up, and the coffee along that path gives up more while the rest gives up less. Our [channeling guide](/blog/fix-channeling-uneven-extraction/) covers the symptoms. Three measured interventions stand out.

**Deep WDT narrowed the spread, but didn't fix it.** With "surface WDT" (stirring only the top third of the dose), Gagné's peak resistance varied by about 40% from shot to shot. Stirring the full depth ("deep WDT") brought most shots closer together, but about 15% to 25% of the deep-WDT shots were still outliers, and all of them appeared to be chokers, not gushers. His "slightly frightening suspicion" was that his distribution was still inconsistent and that the best-prepared pucks were the high-resistance ones. All 24 shots looked fine to the eye. The settled resistance near the end of the shot was far steadier, varying about 5%, which he compares with adding or removing 1 g from an 18 g dose. Our [WDT guide](/blog/best-wdt-tool-espresso-distribution/) covers the tools.

**A paper filter on top didn't help; one underneath did.** A dry paper filter above the puck raised the resistance by about 6 to 7%, did nothing clear for repeatability, and left the hollow that formed in the middle of every spent puck. In April 2021 Gagné moved the filter underneath and pulled 10 shots, alternating with and without it. The bottom filter lowered the peak resistance by a factor of 1.9 ± 0.2 and the settled resistance by a factor of 1.43 ± 0.04. He writes the second as a reduction of "about 43 ± 4%"; expressed as a cut, a factor of 1.43 takes the resistance to about 70% of its former value, about a 30% reduction (calculated). The shot-to-shot spread barely changed (4 ± 1% against 5 ± 1%). The spent pucks changed completely: all five without the filter had the central hollow, and none of the five with it did.

His explanation is that the bottom filter lets water use the whole underside of the puck. The holes in his Decent basket only cover a circle about 50 mm across inside the 58 mm basket, so without a filter the outer ring has nowhere to drain. Stéphane Ribes's experiments, which Gagné cites, found the edges of unfiltered pucks under-extracted. Gagné expects slightly more even flow and a slightly finer grind as the benefits, and wanted blind tastings to confirm them.

**Tamper fit matters more than it looks.** Rao (chapter 6) recommends a tamper 0.25 to 0.30 mm smaller than the basket's inner diameter, which leaves a gap of 0.125 to 0.15 mm all around: a 58.4 mm tamper in his 58.7 mm basket, for example. He writes that such channels "may not always be visible," but a refractometer shows the lower extraction, and that subtle channeling from a poor fit "can easily decrease extraction by 0.5 percentage point." Our [tamping guide](/blog/tamping-pressure-doesnt-matter-myth/) covers the rest of the tamp.

## What it means at the machine

- **Dial in with the grinder, and expect it to move the fines.** That's what the 24-grinder data say you're mostly adjusting. A grinder that makes fewer fines needs a much finer setting for the same shot time, and on Gagné's evidence it extracts more at the same ratio. See our guide to [dialing in espresso](/blog/how-to-dial-in-espresso/).
- **Judge puck prep by the settled part of the shot.** The early peak in resistance is dominated by how the puck wets; the settled resistance repeated to within about 5% in Gagné's shots.
- **Wet the puck fully before full pressure.** A pause after preinfusion, or a bloom, removed the early peak in Gagné's shots. Our [pressure profiling guide](/blog/pressure-profiling-espresso-guide/) covers profiles, including Rao's.
- **Try a paper filter under the puck if your spent pucks are hollow in the middle.** It's the change with the clearest measured effect here, at the cost of a disposable filter per shot.
- **Measure, because the differences are small.** Rao's half-point from a loose tamper, or a point or two between grinders, is the kind of change his book argues you can't track by taste alone: taste-led dialing in makes it "nearly impossible" to know you've found the best settings (chapter 1). A refractometer reading turns it into a number.
