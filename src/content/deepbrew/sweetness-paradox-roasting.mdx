Green arabica carries a good share of sugar, mostly sucrose. By the end of first crack, James Hoffmann writes, there are "few or no simple sugars left." Yet cuppers describe good roasted coffee as sweet, and Hoffmann draws sweetness across roast degree as a bell curve, "peaking in between the highs of acidity and bitterness": highest somewhere between a sharp light roast and a bitter dark one. A good roaster, he adds, can move where a coffee is sweetest. Our guide to [roast levels](/blog/coffee-roast-levels-explained/) covers the practical side.

This note asks where that sweetness comes from, if not from sugar. How sugar breaks down in the roaster, through caramelization and the Maillard reaction, is covered in [Maillard, caramelization and Strecker](/deep-brew/research/maillard-caramelization-strecker/); this note picks up at the cup. This note summarizes published research; the sources are at the end.

## How much sugar survives

Green arabica is a few percent sucrose by weight. In a table of published analyses compiled by Bastian and colleagues (2021), arabica samples run from about 4.4% to 9.3% sucrose, with one semi-dry Brazilian sample at 12.3%, and robusta samples from about 3.2% to 4.9%. Arabica usually starts with more.

Roasting takes most of it. Hoffmann explains that once the water has gone, some sugars caramelize and others react with proteins in the Maillard reaction, and by the end of first crack few or none are left. Rob Hoos, a production roaster, puts it in flavor terms: caramelization "replaces the sweetness of the sugar with a bitter and complex flavor."

What reaches the cup is what matters for taste, and Rune and colleagues measured it. They roasted five specialty arabicas from Brazil, Bolivia and Kenya to three levels, brewed each in a French press to about the same strength, and measured the sugars. Their "darker" roasts, they note, fall in the range older studies call medium. Those brews held 0.17 mg of sugar per mL on average, and their middle roasts 0.25 mg/mL. The lowest taste thresholds they cite for these sugars in water start at 1 mg/mL.

<p class="eq">1 mg/mL ÷ 0.25 mg/mL ≈ 4</p>
<p class="eq">1 mg/mL ÷ 0.17 mg/mL ≈ 6</p>

That is, the sugar in those brews was a quarter to a sixth of the lowest threshold (calculated). The authors add that thresholds are likely higher in coffee than in pure water, because of everything else in the cup, so they don't expect these sugars to be tasted directly.

Their lightest roasts were different. Dropped at 336°F&nbsp;(169°C), with no more than 28 seconds of development after first crack, those brews held 0.97 mg/mL, close to the threshold. The authors suggest sugar could affect taste directly for some people in roasts that light. So the claim that "all the sugar is gone" is true only roughly: very light roasts keep a little that may matter, and medium roasts keep well below the lowest published threshold. An earlier version of this note said roasting destroys "100%" of the sugar; that was never what its own figures showed.

A UC Davis study came at this from the other side. Batali and colleagues (2020) split drip brews into fractions, swapping in an empty carafe every 30 seconds, and had a trained panel describe each one. They measured the simple sugars too, and none of the fractions reached a perceptible level. Yet the panel found the later fractions systematically sweeter than the earlier ones. The authors conclude that coffee's perceptible sweetness is "a consequence of masking effects and/or the presence of sweet-associated aromas and flavors." Those two explanations are the rest of this note.

## Sweetness you smell

Smell can change how sweet something tastes. Stevenson, Prescott and Boakes (1999) added 20 odors, one at a time, to sugar solutions. Odors that smelled sweet made the solutions taste sweeter, and odors that didn't smell sweet made them taste less sweet. How sweet an odor smelled was the best predictor of its effect. In a second experiment, caramel, the sweetest-smelling of the odors they used, also made a citric acid solution taste less sour. The authors' explanation is learned association: we have met these smells alongside sweet tastes so often that they come to carry sweetness themselves.

Roasted coffee is full of such smells. Furaneol and related furanones have a caramel-like smell (Schwab, 2013), and furanones were among the odorants with the greatest effect on coffee's flavor in Czerny, Mayer and Grosch's 1999 recombination study. The [Maillard note](/deep-brew/research/maillard-caramelization-strecker/) covers how they form; it's not by caramelization alone.

Two cautions keep this from being a complete answer. Stevenson's experiments added odors to solutions that already contained sugar; they show odor boosting sweetness that's there, not creating it in a cup with almost none. And no study here measured how much of coffee's sweetness a given compound supplies. Rune and colleagues do cite related work in which changing the roast changed coffee's perceived sweetness without changing its sugar content, which fits the aroma account.

## Sweetness you stop masking

The other half of Batali's explanation is masking: other tastes hiding sweetness, and sweetness showing when they drop.

**Sourness.** Rune and colleagues measured each acid in their brews and tested how much of it people could detect. Only citric acid was clearly above its detection threshold: 0.23–0.60 g/L in the coffees, against a threshold below 0.16 g/L. Citric and malic acid fell as the roast went darker, while acetic, lactic, phosphoric, quinic and glycolic acid rose. Across the light roasts they studied, the one acid people could clearly detect on its own was falling.

Acids do suppress sweetness in mixtures. In water, ethanol and white wine at wine-like acid levels, Zamora, Goldner and Galmarini (2006) found that tartaric acid suppressed the sweetness of fructose more strongly than fructose suppressed the acid's sourness. They cite earlier work showing that citric acid above about 0.01 molar suppresses the sweetness of sucrose. That's a far higher level than coffee holds:

<p class="eq">0.01 mol/L × 192 g/mol ≈ 1.9 g/L of citric acid</p>

That is about three to eight times the citric acid Rune and colleagues measured in their brews (calculated, using citric acid's molar mass of 192 g/mol). So less sourness plausibly lets more sweetness through as a roast develops, but the size of the effect at coffee's acid levels hasn't been measured.

**Bitterness.** Bitterness rises as roasting goes on. Hoffmann describes it increasing slowly with roast time and "definitely" with roast darkness. The bitter compounds come mostly from the chlorogenic acids, and the [chlorogenic acids note](/deep-brew/research/chlorogenic-acid-cascades/) follows them. On Hoffmann's curve, sweetness peaks before bitterness takes over; the sources here don't separate how much of the fall in dark roasts is masking and how much is lost aroma.

**Strength.** Concentration matters as well. Frost, Ristenpart and Guinard (2020) roasted one washed arabica light, medium and dark, brewed each to nine combinations of strength and extraction, and had a trained panel describe them. At every roast level, perceived sweetness went down as strength (total dissolved solids) went up. Batali's sweeter late fractions were also the weaker ones. Neither study pinned down why, but more of everything else in the cup is consistent with masking.

## Hoos's end-temperature test

Hoos treats the roast's end temperature as the main control on how far caramelization goes. In his model, a lower end temperature leaves more residual sugar and a sweeter cup, with a risk of vegetal flavors, and a higher one adds caramel bitterness and complexity. He hedges throughout. He calls the book's chemistry "to a certain extent, my opinions" and says he isn't a scientist by trade, and he describes the results in terms of what they "seem to indicate."

His test used one coffee, a Guatemalan. He roasted it three times on the same profile, keeping the other phases within 3 to 10 seconds of the baseline, and dropped the batches at the baseline temperature and at 6°F&nbsp;(3.3°C) below and above it. The lower drop "did tend to remain sweeter," in his words, but tasted vegetal and less complex. The higher drop was more savory, with more bitterness from the caramels. The baseline scored best overall. On his cupping form, all three got the same sweetness score; the difference showed in his descriptors and the other scores. For the full scores, see the book.

Rune's measurements fit the direction of Hoos's claim: their three roasts were dropped at 336, 345 and 356°F&nbsp;(169, 174 and 180°C), and the sugar in the brew fell as the drop temperature rose. But their roasts also differed in development time, so the two can't be separated there. And only their lightest roasts came near a tasting threshold. At ordinary roast levels, a lower drop may taste sweeter because of different aromas and acids as much as because of leftover sugar; the sources here can't settle which.

An earlier version of this note gave a "sweetness score" for each roast level, from 3–4 for light to 8–9 for medium. No source contains those scores, so they're gone.

## What it means when you brew

If sweetness in coffee comes mostly from aroma and from what isn't masking it, a few practical points follow. The last two are readings of the studies above, not results anyone has tested directly.

- **Bright isn't the same as sour.** A light roast can taste lively and still sweet. Our Fix My Coffee tool treats sourness that hits sharply and then disappears as a sign of a brew that stopped early, and acidity with a sweet, lingering finish as the coffee itself.
- **Strength can hide sweetness.** In the UC Davis panels, sweetness rose as strength fell. If a cup tastes heavy and harsh rather than sour or bitter, a slightly weaker ratio is worth a try.
- **Aroma is perishable.** If much of the sweetness is smelled, anything that strips aroma should dull it. That's an inference, not a measured result; the [aroma staling note](/deep-brew/research/aroma-staling-kinetics/) covers how fast coffee's aromas fade.
