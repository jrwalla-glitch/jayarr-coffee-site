This note summarizes published research; the sources are at the end. Its subject is one book: Rob Hoos's *Cultivar: A Practical Guide for Coffee Roasters* (2025), which gives roasters a starting point for each cultivar, the genetic variety of the coffee plant. Hoos is a roaster and educator, author of *Modulating the Flavor Profile of Coffee* (2015), and the book records his own experience. It isn't a controlled study, and he says so throughout. We summarize the framework and a few of his examples here; the cultivar-by-cultivar profiles are in the book itself.

For the basics of home roasting, including the stages of a roast, see [how to roast coffee at home](/blog/how-to-roast-coffee-at-home/). For what cultivars taste like in the cup, see [coffee varieties explained](/blog/coffee-varieties-explained/).

## How Hoos built the framework

Over more than a year, Hoos bought as many cultivars as he could from single farms, all processed the same way, and compared them in a series of boxed sets, later repeating the exercise on farms in other countries. For each coffee he roasted a standardized baseline and then spent the rest of the green on finding the profile he liked best. He also went back through his old roasting notebooks for patterns between cultivars and his favorite approaches (p. 7).

He is explicit about the context (p. 20). He tasted everything at 16 parts water to 1 part coffee, using distilled water remineralized with Third Wave Water's classic mix, poured just off the boil, about 210 °F (99 °C) at his altitude. He roasted on a traditional drum roaster; fluid-bed roasters weren't heavily featured. And he roasted light, at what he calls "effectively, a sample roast level," aiming for fruity, floral and sweet character. For espresso or darker styles, his general advice is to push first crack back to 9:00 or later, extend development and roast darker.

So the timings in this note are starting points for light roasts on a drum. They aren't laws, and he doesn't present them as laws.

## The four groups

Hoos groups cultivars for two reasons (p. 21). The practical one is to give a starting point for any coffee, including cultivars he never roasted: someone buying SL14, for example, could look it up on a coffee family tree, see that it sits on the Typica side, and start with the Typica group's faster roast. The second is a hypothesis, and he hedges it carefully. He suspects that seed chemistry and shape, influenced by genetics, affect how a coffee tastes and responds to roasting, adding: "That suspicion may be completely unfounded." He also warns that he is "intentionally oversimplifying," that cultivars within a group don't necessarily taste alike or need identical roasts, and that some cultivars, such as Catuai, have a "massive sweet spot" while others, such as Gesha, only present well within a very narrow range.

His summary chart places the groups on a line from fast to slow roasting: Typica and the Ethiopian group toward the fast end, Bourbon toward the slow end, and the Timor Hybrid group spanning the middle.

**Typica group (p. 22).** He found these did best reaching first crack before 8:00, which brought out their floral character and kept savory notes down, with 1:20 to 1:30 of development on a light roast. The flavors he found in common were florals, pleasant acidity, a creamy, full body and caramel tones. Pacamara was the exception: more aggressively acidic, and needing extra development to bring the acidity into check.

**Bourbon group (p. 32).** These did well with a longer roast to first crack, landing it somewhere between 8:30 and 9:30 for light roasts. Roasted too quickly, they turned starchy, papery and thin. He noticed a red-fruit note in almost all of them, from cherry to strawberry or currant.

**Ethiopian group (p. 37).** The cultivars here have Ethiopian landrace genetics but are mostly grown elsewhere, from Gesha to Sidra. He finds them hard to generalize, but says intense, perfumed florals, sweetness and sparkling, citrus-like acidity tie them together. He has better luck roasting them quickly, aiming for first crack around 7:30 and, depending on processing, about 1:30 of development. With Gesha, he finds that a slow roast lets savory, caramel notes take over the delicate florals, and he finds it more prone to tipping (scorched bean tips), so he suggests a lower inlet temperature with more airflow to keep the roast quick (p. 40).

**Timor Hybrid group (p. 44).** These cultivars were bred with the Timor Hybrid, usually for disease resistance, vigor or yield. The group is too diverse for common timings, so his advice is to look at the cultivars in the cross and expect the best roast to follow one of the parents. Castillo, in his experience, roasts like Caturra; H1 follows Rume Sudan. In washed coffees from this group he often tastes a herbal or woody note in the aftertaste, faint in great examples and sometimes heading toward green bell pepper in weaker ones.

## Two examples where the name misleads

**SL28 and SL34.** Both were selected at Scott Agricultural Laboratories in Kenya, so a roaster might expect them to behave alike. World Coffee Research's catalog describes SL28 as Bourbon-related, a selection from "Tanganika Drought Resistant" (the catalog's spelling) with a Bourbon-like genetic background, and SL34 as Typica-related. Hoos's groups match: he puts SL34 in the Typica group, with first crack before 8:00, and SL28 in the Bourbon group, with first crack between 8:30 and 9:30. He also notes that SL cultivars tend to come out a little darker in color than he expects, which is a reason to check weight loss and color rather than trust your eye alone (p. 54).

**Pink Bourbon.** Despite the name, genetic testing commissioned by the importer Café Imports, reported by Ian Fretheim with Christophe Montagnon of the RD2 Vision lab (2023), found it unrelated to Bourbon: the lab identified Pink Bourbon, an Ethiopian landrace, in all five samples sent, two of them mixed with Bourbon and two with Catimor. Hoos, citing their work, calls it "likely an Ethiopian Landrace Variety" (p. 8) and roasts it with the Ethiopian group. His best-fit profile for it was 4:15 to yellow, 3:15 from yellow to first crack and 1:20 of development, and pushing first crack back to about 8:00 made it noticeably more savory (p. 41).

## Processing adjusts development and end temperature

In Hoos's method, cultivar mostly sets how long to take to first crack, and processing mostly sets what happens after it (pp. 18 and 51). For his lightest roasts (p. 18):

- **Natural:** shorter development, usually somewhere between 1:00 and 1:30, often ending at a slightly lower temperature for the same color.
- **Washed:** more development, generally 1:20 to 1:45, sometimes ending hotter.
- **Honey:** black and red honeys behave more like naturals, yellow and white more like washed coffees.
- **Anaerobic and similar processes:** slightly shorter development and a slightly lower end temperature than the base process. His example is an anaerobic washed coffee that "may tend to favor" 1:15 to 1:20, with a lower end temperature than a classic washed coffee.

Elsewhere he puts the difference for naturals and honeys at 15 to 30 seconds less development than washed coffees (p. 54). He prefaces the whole section with "there will always be exceptions to almost any rule." He doesn't give a margin of error for anaerobic coffees.

## Planning a first roast

Hoos's own walk-through (pp. 50–56) goes in four steps, and his first worked example shows them together. In it, a roaster with an anaerobic natural Bourbon from Costa Rica reasons that the Bourbon group wants a slower roast to first crack and an anaerobic natural wants shorter development than a usual natural, and so starts with yellow at 4:40, first crack at 8:45 and about 1:00 of development (p. 50). The steps:

1. **Decide on the roast style.** His group timings assume light to medium roasts.
2. **Find the cultivar's group** and set a target time for first crack. For a blend of cultivars, he favors the approach of whichever makes up the largest share.
3. **Use the processing method** to set the development time.
4. **Choose the end point** by temperature, color and weight loss together. He reads roast degree from weight loss as well: under about 11% worries him, and he calls 11–13% light and 14–16% medium.

For the drying stage, he gives a rule of thumb: multiply the target first-crack time by 0.56 to get "roughly" when to aim for yellowing. His example is a Villa Sarchi, a Bourbon mutation, where an 8:45 first crack gives about 4:54 to yellow (p. 56). He presents his planning chart as "just a recommendation" (p. 55). He also reports that his own drying phase tends to run 43–51% of the whole roast, the stretch from yellow to first crack 32–44% and development 12–20%, adding: "That doesn't mean yours has to fall within these ranges" (p. 53). In his earlier book, he names the stretch from color change to first crack the Maillard-to-first-crack phase, or MAI, and finds that lengthening it adds body and complexity at some cost to clarity (*Modulating*, pp. 21–22). Our note on [Maillard, caramelization and Strecker reactions](/deep-brew/research/maillard-caramelization-strecker/) covers the chemistry.

After that, he adjusts for moisture, density and altitude. Wetter coffees heat faster early on, then slow as the beans approach 100 °C and the water absorbs energy to evaporate, so he may charge slightly hotter or add more heat; denser, higher-grown coffees take more heat without defects, while less dense ones can tip if pushed (p. 56).

## What the framework doesn't settle

- **The label may be wrong.** Hoos stresses that cultivar identities are "fuzzy" (p. 8). Farmers rely on whoever supplied the seed, genetic testing is new and costly, and lines can drift toward one parent.
- **Arabica isn't purely self-pollinating.** Montagnon told Hoos he suspected about 25% of seeds from a coffee plant were cross-pollinated. Kenneth Davids, as Hoos quotes him, says about 20% of the flowers are pollinated by bees and other insects, which isn't the same as the share of seeds that are genetic crosses (p. 8). Both are estimates, not measurements. A single-cultivar lot may still be a mix.
- **Processing may matter more.** Hoos writes that processing "may have the most dramatic impact on a coffee's flavor profile," with terroir interacting with the cultivar too (p. 9).
- **Dark roasts are an open question.** He's confident in his approach for light and medium roasts, but still investigating whether his first-crack timings help dark roasts (p. 54).
- **Other roasters will differ.** His timings come from drum roasting; fluid-bed roasters, he says, weren't heavily featured outside sample roasting, so the book doesn't cover how the timings translate to them.

The framework's strength is that it gives a reasoned first roast for an unfamiliar cultivar, which you then cup and adjust. If you cup with Hoos's numbers, your water matters too.
