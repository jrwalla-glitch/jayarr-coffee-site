Roasted coffee contains about a thousand volatile compounds. Otto Vitzthum put the count at more than a thousand in 1999; Adriana Farah's 2012 review gives more than 950, depending on the coffee, the roast and the method. Most are present at levels nobody can smell. Since the late 1980s, flavor chemists have asked a narrower question: which few compounds make coffee smell like coffee?

This note summarizes published research; the sources are at the end. It covers which compounds matter and how researchers decided. How fast they fade is in our note on [aroma staling](/deep-brew/research/aroma-staling-kinetics/).

## What an odor activity value is

An odor activity value compares how much of a compound is present with how much it takes to smell it:

<p class="eq">odor activity value = concentration ÷ odor threshold</p>

In words, it says how many times over its own detection threshold a compound is present. A value below 1 means the compound is below the level a person can detect on its own; a value of 1,000 means it is present at a thousand times that level. To take made-up numbers as an illustration: a compound at 500 parts per billion with a threshold of 1,000 parts per billion has a value of 0.5, while one at 0.05 parts per billion with a threshold of 0.01 parts per billion has a value of 5. The rare compound outranks the abundant one.

The threshold has to be measured in the same medium as the concentration, because a compound's threshold in water, in oil and in air can differ widely. That is one reason values for ground coffee and for brewed coffee aren't interchangeable.

Werner Grosch's laboratory in Garching built the method most of this note relies on, and his 1998 review sets out its steps:

1. **Find the odorants by smell.** In aroma extract dilution analysis, an aroma extract is run through a gas chromatograph while a person sniffs the outlet. The extract is diluted again and again, by 1:5 or 1:10 each time, as Vitzthum describes; the odors that are still detectable at the highest dilution are the most potent.
2. **Measure how much of each is there,** using stable isotope dilution assays: a labeled copy of each compound is added as an internal standard.
3. **Calculate odor activity values** from those amounts and the compounds' odor thresholds.
4. **Test the result by smell.** Mix the high-value odorants into a model, compare it with the real coffee, then leave compounds out and see whether a panel notices.

The last step matters because an odor activity value describes one compound in isolation. Odors mask and blend in a mixture, so a high value says nothing certain about what a compound adds to the whole. The only check is to smell the mixture with and without it.

## What the omission tests showed

The Grosch group ran that check on ground coffee and then on the brew.

**Ground coffee.** Michael Czerny, Florian Mayer and Grosch quantified the potent odorants in a roasted Arabica, dissolved 27 of them in an oil-and-water mixture, and found the model's flavor profile "very close" to the real sample. In duo and triangle tests against models missing one or more odorants, 2-furfurylthiol, 4-vinylguaiacol, several alkylpyrazines, furanones, acetaldehyde, propanal, methylpropanal, and 2- and 3-methylbutanal had the greatest impact. According to Roberto Buffo and Claudio Cardelli-Freire's review of the study, assessors didn't notice when 2,3-butanedione, 2,3-pentanedione, β-damascenone or vanillin was left out.

**The brew.** Mayer, Czerny and Grosch then quantified the odorants in a medium-roasted Arabica and in the brew made from it, and built a 24-odorant model of the brew. In triangle tests, its aroma was "mainly caused by" some alkylpyrazines, furanones and phenols, and by 2-furfurylthiol, methional and 3-mercapto-3-methylbutyl formate. Methional and the formate mattered more in the brew than in ground coffee of the same origin and roast, and 4-vinylguaiacol mattered less.

**The smell of the bag.** Mayer and Grosch also measured 22 potent odorants in the air above roasted coffee powder. Buffo and Cardelli-Freire report that the evaporated model scored 2.6 for similarity to the real headspace, on a scale from 0 (no similarity) to 3 (identical).

Two lessons follow. First, a few dozen compounds are enough: models of 27 and 22 odorants came close to the real ground coffee and its headspace. Second, the ranking by odor activity and the ranking by smell disagree in places. β-Damascenone had the highest odor activity value in Semmelroch and colleagues' study of ground Arabica, yet leaving it out of Czerny's model went unnoticed. Buffo and Cardelli-Freire conclude that sensory studies did not confirm the important role once suggested for it in coffee.

## The cup isn't the bag

Brewing doesn't carry every odorant into the cup equally. Mayer, Czerny and Grosch found that more than 75% of the acetaldehyde, 2,3-butanedione, 2,3-pentanedione, vanillin and some furanones were extracted into the brew, but less than 25% of the less polar compounds: 3-isobutyl-2-methoxypyrazine, β-damascenone and 2-furfurylthiol, which they describe as unstable.

So a brew's aroma leans toward the water-loving odorants, the buttery diketones, vanillin and the caramel-like furanones, compared with the smell of the beans. Buffo and Cardelli-Freire report an earlier study by Semmelroch and Grosch that found the same shift for Arabica and Robusta brews, and whose aroma models of the two brews "smelled clearly coffee-like" and reproduced the difference between them.

The species difference is real in the chemistry too. Farah's review reports Blank and colleagues' finding that caramel-like, "sweet-roasty" notes predominate in Arabica and spicy, "earthy-roasty" notes in Robusta. Arabica had more 3-mercapto-3-methylbutyl formate, sotolon, abhexon, 2-methyl-3-furanthiol, phenylacetaldehyde and linalool, among others; Robusta had more 2,3-diethyl-5-methylpyrazine, 4-ethylguaiacol and 3-methyl-2-buten-1-thiol.

## The sulfur odorants

Several of the compounds that pass the omission test contain sulfur, and they behave differently from the rest.

**2-Furfurylthiol** is the odorant most often linked to the smell of roasted coffee, and its character depends on how much is there. Stefan Schenker's literature review reports Tressl and Silwar's 1981 finding that it smelled like freshly roasted coffee at 0.01 to 0.5 parts per billion, and like stale coffee with a sulfury note at 1 to 10 parts per billion. The same review reports that it forms in model systems of the amino acid cysteine and the sugar ribose, increasing with temperature and roasting time (Stahl and Parliment, 1993), and that several formation pathways may run in parallel (Hofmann and Schieberle, 1998). Our note on [Maillard, Strecker and caramelization chemistry](/deep-brew/research/maillard-caramelization-strecker/) covers those reactions.

**3-Mercapto-3-methylbutyl formate** shows how concentration changes character. Vitzthum writes that the pure compound doesn't smell like coffee: it has a "blackcurrant like, catty note". Only in great dilution in a coffee beverage, especially one made from soluble coffee, does it earn the description "roast coffee likeness".

**Methanethiol** is the smell of freshness. In Wilhardi Holscher and Hans Steinhart's sniffing study of freshly roasted coffee, it and methylpropanal gave the most intense notes. It is also the first to go, which the [aroma staling note](/deep-brew/research/aroma-staling-kinetics/) covers with the other losses.

Some sulfur odorants are hard to measure at all. Holscher and Steinhart note that 2-furfurylthiol and 3-methyl-2-buten-1-thiol contributed to the pleasant smell of freshly roasted coffee but gave no response on their detector. A person could smell them below the level the instrument could measure.

## How origin and roast change the odorants

The same potent odorants turn up across coffees, in different amounts. Mayer, Czerny and Grosch measured 28 potent odorants in medium-roasted Arabica blends from Colombia, Brazil, El Salvador and Kenya and in Typica and Caturra coffees from Ecuador:

- The Kenya blend had more 3-mercapto-3-methylbutyl formate than the Colombia and Brazil blends, and less 2-furfurylthiol, guaiacol and 4-ethylguaiacol.
- The El Salvador blend had less 4-vinylguaiacol and 2-furfurylthiol but more 3-methyl-2-buten-1-thiol.
- The Typica and the two Caturras had less 2,3-butanedione, 2,3-pentanedione and furaneol than the three blends, and more guaiacol, 3-mercapto-3-methylbutyl formate and 3-methyl-2-buten-1-thiol. Typica and one of the Caturras had the most 2-furfurylthiol.

Roasting darker didn't move every coffee the same way. Guaiacol, 4-ethylguaiacol and 3-methyl-2-buten-1-thiol rose with roast degree in the Colombia and Kenya blends and in the Typica. 2-Furfurylthiol rose only in the Colombia and Kenya blends. In the Typica, the carbonyl compounds, furaneol, methional and β-damascenone fell from medium to dark. So there is no single roast level at which "the" coffee aroma peaks; it depends on the coffee.

The smoky and spicy phenols have their own route. Ralph Dorfner and colleagues followed 4-vinylguaiacol, guaiacol and phenol in real time during roasting and proposed two channels. At lower temperatures, a feruloylquinic acid (one of the chlorogenic acids) breaks down to ferulic acid, which loses carbon dioxide to form 4-vinylguaiacol. At higher temperatures, 4-vinylguaiacol oxidizes to guaiacol and then to phenol and other phenolic compounds. Our [chlorogenic acid note](/deep-brew/research/chlorogenic-acid-cascades/) follows the parent compounds.

## Where the numbers disagree

Odor activity values depend on the coffee, the medium and the method, so lists from different studies don't line up. A 2025 study by Xiaomei Chen and colleagues analyzed four commercial medium-roast Arabicas, from Colombia, Bench Maji and Yirgacheffe in Ethiopia, and Baoshan in China, as ground powders, using solvent-assisted flavor evaporation and gas chromatography with sniffing. They found 25 compounds with values above 1. The highest were 4-vinylguaiacol, at up to 761, and furaneol, at 196 to 250, depending on the coffee. 2-Furfurylthiol doesn't appear among the compounds they report.

That doesn't mean their coffees lacked it. Sulfur odorants are present in tiny amounts, as Holscher and Steinhart found, and a method that isn't aimed at them can miss them. It does mean that a single list of "coffee's top odorants by odor activity value" mixes numbers that can't be compared. The values in the table above come from one study of ground Arabica, and their real use is the one the Grosch group gave them: a shortlist to test by smell.

## What it means in practice

- **A flat-smelling cup usually means lost odorants, not missing ones.** The key odorants turned up in every coffee these studies measured; the lightest sulfur ones are the first to fade. Freshness is the first thing to check, then water. The [aroma staling note](/deep-brew/research/aroma-staling-kinetics/) explains why.
- **The bag isn't the cup.** The beans' aroma is richer in the less soluble odorants than the brew is, so judge a coffee by the cup.
- **Tasting notes are hypotheses.** Origin and roast shift the amounts of the same odorants, in different directions for different coffees. Our guides to [cupping at home](/blog/how-to-cup-coffee-at-home/) and [training your palate](/blog/train-your-coffee-palate/) cover the tasting side, and [roast levels explained](/blog/coffee-roast-levels-explained/) covers the practical effect of roast.
