Melanoidins are the brown polymers that form at the end of the Maillard reaction. Vincenzo Fogliano and Francisco Morales define them as high-molecular-weight, brown-colored, nitrogen-containing end products of that reaction. In coffee they are a large share of what dissolves into the cup, they give it much of its color, and they take part in espresso foam, in aroma staling and, in laboratory tests, in gut fermentation.

This note summarizes published research; the sources are at the end. It covers what melanoidins are, how much coffee holds, and what they do for color, crema, body and staling, plus what the health research does and doesn't show. How roasting makes them is in our note on [Maillard, Strecker and caramelization chemistry](/deep-brew/research/maillard-caramelization-strecker/).

## What melanoidins are

Melanoidins aren't one molecule. They are a family of large, varied structures assembled during roasting, and their makeup is still being worked out. The studies agree on the main ingredients, less on the details.

- **Protein and amino acids.** Fogliano and Morales's definition requires nitrogen. Eva Bekedam and colleagues, working with brew fractions from green, light, medium and dark roasts, found that the melanoidin level correlated with the nitrogen and protein content in every fraction.
- **Polysaccharides.** Fernando Nunes and Manuel Coimbra found that in the large molecules extracted from roasted coffee with hot water, phenolic and brown compounds were linked by covalent bonds to the polymeric material, the polysaccharides and proteins. Bekedam's group found arabinogalactans more involved than galactomannans, with galactomannans incorporated continuously as roasting goes on.
- **Chlorogenic acids.** Farah's review lists chlorogenic acids among the components melanoidins incorporate, and Bekedam's group concluded that proteins and chlorogenic acids should be primarily involved in their formation. Ralph Dorfner and colleagues proposed that 4-vinylguaiacol, which forms from a chlorogenic acid during roasting, polymerizes into partly insoluble coffee melanoidins. Not every study agrees on the details: Diana Gniechwitz and colleagues, analyzing one high-molecular-weight brew fraction by NMR, concluded that intact caffeic and ferulic acid derivatives were not incorporated to a significant extent.

Size varies just as much. Gniechwitz's group found that brews from differently roasted Arabicas contained 8–12% ethanol-soluble substances larger than 2 kilodaltons, with a large spread of sizes within them. The smaller melanoidins matter too: in Thomas Hofmann's model study, those of about 1,500 to 3,000 daltons cut the roasty odorant 2-furfurylthiol the most.

## How much coffee holds, and how roast changes it

The often-quoted figure is about a quarter, but sources differ on a quarter of what. Rosa Borrelli and colleagues write that melanoidins constitute up to 25% of the dry matter of coffee beverages. Adriana Farah's review gives about 25% of the dry matter of roasted coffee. Both give it as a round figure rather than a measurement of their own, and measuring melanoidins is itself hard: Fogliano and Morales note the lack of a reference material for them.

Roast degree changes both the amount and the size.

- **Amount.** Borrelli's group roasted the same green coffee light, medium and dark and isolated the melanoidins from each brew. The amount rose with the intensity of roasting.
- **Size.** Here the studies disagree. Borrelli's group found that molecular weight decreased as roasting intensified. Bekedam's group found that the first stage of roasting, from green to light, mainly formed intermediate-size melanoidins, and that longer roasting mainly formed large ones. The two groups used different coffees and different methods, so the conflict isn't resolved.
- **Share.** Nunes and Coimbra isolated six charged melanoidin fractions from light, medium and dark roasts and found that they made up 30–33% of the large, cold-water-soluble material at every roast degree.

These are a handful of coffees, each roasted to three or four levels. They show the direction, not a percentage you could attach to a given roast level.

## Color

Brown color is part of the definition of melanoidins, and Farah's review calls them responsible for the brown color of roasted coffee. Labs can track them by how much light a sample absorbs; Gniechwitz's group, for example, measured absorbance at 405 nanometers. More melanoidin in a darker roast, as Borrelli found, goes with a browner brew.

## Crema and body

**Crema.** Alessandra D'Agostina and colleagues separated the foaming material of freshly made espresso into two fractions. One was almost colorless, larger, and mostly polysaccharide; the other was smaller, darker and richer in protein and melanoidins. The darker fraction foamed more readily, but the pale polysaccharide fraction kept the foam stable. So melanoidins help make crema, and polysaccharides help it last.

**Body.** Nunes and Coimbra open their study by noting that the polysaccharides in coffee are known to contribute to body, the creamy sensation in the mouth, as well as to aroma release and espresso foam stability. In roasted coffee, those polysaccharides carry brown compounds bound to them. That makes it hard to say where polysaccharides end and melanoidins begin, and none of the studies here measures how much of the body a person feels comes from melanoidins themselves. Statements that melanoidins are "the" source of body, or that a darker roast's extra melanoidins give a set amount of extra body, go beyond the evidence.

Oils are a separate part of the picture. Urgert and Katan explain that the diterpene lipids cafestol and kahweol, about 1% of coffee beans by weight, are extracted by hot water but retained by a paper filter. Paper filters retain much of that diterpene-rich oil fraction; these sources don't measure what a paper filter does to melanoidins, which are largely dissolved in the brew.

## Melanoidins and staling

Melanoidins also take part in aroma staling. Hofmann and Peter Schieberle mixed the volatiles from a fresh brew with melanoidins isolated from coffee and found a drastic drop in the headspace of five odorous thiols; 2-furfurylthiol fell by a factor of 16, and the roasty, sulfury aroma weakened. They traced the binding to pyrazinium structures formed in the Maillard reaction. Later work by Christoph Müller and Hofmann found that the loss of 2-furfurylthiol in stored brew is mainly due to its coupling with a small phenol, hydroxyhydroquinone. So melanoidins are one thiol trap among several. The [aroma staling note](/deep-brew/research/aroma-staling-kinetics/) has the measured losses and time scales.

## What the health research shows

Most claims about melanoidins and health rest on laboratory work. Farah's review puts it plainly: studies suggest melanoidins are partly responsible for the antioxidant, antibacterial and metal-chelating properties of coffee beverages, but "their physiologic relevance in humans is unknown."

**Antioxidant activity depends on the test.** Borrelli's group tested the melanoidins they isolated from light, medium and dark brews of one coffee. Their activity against two test radicals decreased as roasting intensified, but their ability to prevent the oxidation of linoleic acid was higher in the dark roast. Bekedam's group, using a different method that picks up only antioxidants formed during roasting, found that this activity rose with roast degree, mostly in the large molecules, and correlated with melanoidin content. Their results also suggest that chlorogenic acids keep their antioxidant activity once built into melanoidins. These are test-tube measurements of isolated fractions; they don't show that anything happens in the body.

**Fermentation in the gut is partial.** Fogliano and Morales note that melanoidins escape digestion in the upper gut and can be fermented by bacteria in the colon, sharing some properties of dietary fiber. Gniechwitz's group put that to a test in the lab: they fermented one large, ethanol-soluble fraction from a medium-roast brew with human fecal bacteria. It was less fermentable than coffee's soluble fiber and produced less short-chain fatty acid, because it held less carbohydrate. The bacteria preferred the carbohydrates; the other compounds were used only "to some extent". That is one fraction in a lab vessel, not a measured effect in people.

**Metal binding has been shown in the lab.** Nunes and Coimbra separated melanoidin fractions by their ability to bind copper, and their results suggest that how well the fractions bind depends on their carbohydrates.

**Intake.** Fogliano and Morales estimated that moderate and heavy coffee drinkers take in about 0.5 to 2.0 g of coffee melanoidins a day. They call it "an educated guess", limited by the lack of a reference material, and estimate that bread and biscuits supply more.

None of this is a reason to pick a roast for your health. If you have a question about coffee and your health, ask your clinician.

## What it means in practice

- **Darker roasts hold more melanoidins.** In Borrelli's coffee the amount rose at each roast step, which fits the browner color of darker brews. How that translates into body depends on more than melanoidins.
- **Thin and weak go together.** Melanoidins are part of what dissolves, so a brew with fewer dissolved solids carries fewer of them. Our [strength and extraction guide](/blog/coffee-strength-vs-extraction/) describes a weak cup as thin and watery, and explains how strength differs from extraction.
- **The filter changes the oils.** Paper holds back the diterpene oils, as Urgert and Katan describe. How much it changes the melanoidins hasn't been measured in these sources.
- **Brew close to drinking.** Melanoidins are among the compounds that bind coffee's sulfur odorants in the cup.
