It brings together three kinds of evidence that are easy to mix up: measurements of recent heat, models of where coffee will grow in future, and the harvest numbers for the latest seasons. ## What "coffee-harming heat" means

Climate Central counted, for each day from 2021 to 2025, whether the maximum temperature in a coffee-growing region passed 30 °C (86 °F), and whether it would have stayed below 30 °C in a modeled world without carbon pollution. Days that crossed the line only because of climate change are the "added" days. The observed temperatures come from the ERA5 reanalysis, the counterfactual from Climate Central's Climate Shift Index, and the 30 °C threshold from published crop research (Naik and colleagues, 2021; Cheserek and Gichimu, 2012). Above it, they write, heat is "extremely harmful" for arabica and suboptimal for robusta.

Three findings stand out:

- **Every country studied got more of it.** All 25 countries, which together grow about 97% of the world's coffee, had more coffee-harming heat, about 47 extra days a year on average.
- **The top five were hit harder.** Brazil, Vietnam, Colombia, Indonesia and Ethiopia (about 75% of supply) now see more than 144 such days a year on average, about 57 more than they would without climate change. The countries with the most added days were El Salvador (99), Nicaragua (77) and Thailand (75).
- **The count may understate arabica's exposure, or overstate it.** Climate Central notes that 25–30 °C is already suboptimal for arabica, below the threshold used, so it calls the count conservative. But arabica is also usually grown higher, and cooler, than the regional averages in the analysis.

This is an attribution study of temperature. It doesn't measure yields, bean quality or rainfall, though Climate Central writes that the added heat was "potentially affecting the quality and quantity of recent harvests."

## Robusta isn't the heat-proof backup it seemed

Robusta has long been considered the heat-tolerant species, more resistant to climate change than arabica, with an optimal mean temperature usually given as 22–30 °C, an estimate based only on the climate of its native range in the Congo basin. Kath and colleagues (2020) tested that with 10 years of yields from 798 farms across Southeast Asia. Their models put robusta's optimum below 20.5 °C, 1.5 to 9 °C lower than the usual estimate. In the middle of the assumed range (mean annual temperatures over 25.1 °C), yields were 50% lower than at the optimum. During the growing season, every 1 °C rise in mean minimum and maximum temperature above 16.2 and 24.1 °C went with a yield drop of about 14%, or 350 to 460 kg per hectare.

Robusta supplies about 40% of the world's coffee, by their figure, and two of the three largest robusta growers are among the most exposed: Climate Central counts 59 added hot days a year in Vietnam and 73 in Indonesia. Global models point the same way, with a twist. Bunn and colleagues (2015) found that higher temperatures may reduce arabica yields, while robusta "could suffer from increasing variability of intra-seasonal temperatures": swings within the season, not only a warmer average.

## Region by region

The heat figures are from Climate Central (February 2026). The harvest figures are USDA forecasts for the 2026/27 season from July 2026, in 60 kg bags.

### Brazil

Brazil grows nearly 40% of the world's coffee, and Climate Central counts 70 added hot days a year there, 67 in Minas Gerais, its largest coffee state. USDA's account of the last five arabica harvests reads like a weather log: in 2021/22, drought and high temperatures cut the off-year crop by 13.3 million bags; the next year recovered only 3.4 million because of severe frost, heat and low rainfall; 2023/24 yields lagged because of excessive rain during fruit development; and two more years of decline followed as drought and heat returned.

Then came a rebound. USDA forecasts Brazil's 2026/27 crop at a record 71.9 million bags, with arabica up 9.5 million to 47.5 million after timely rain during flowering, "ending a 5-year period of underperformance due to adverse weather conditions." Robusta (conilon) is forecast at 24.4 million bags, mostly from Espírito Santo. Brazil's arabica swings with a two-year cycle of heavy and light crops, so one good year doesn't settle the trend either way. Brazil's rebound is the main reason USDA expects world production to rise from 178.8 million bags in 2025/26 to a record 189.7 million in 2026/27. Looking further ahead, Bunn and colleagues (2015) projected that Brazil and Vietnam may see substantial losses of land suitable for coffee.

### Vietnam

Vietnam is the largest robusta producer: USDA forecasts a record 32.5 million bags for 2026/27, helped by more planted area and, after recent high prices, more spending on fertilizer. It also has 59 added hot days a year. Kath and colleagues' farms were in Southeast Asia, so their finding on robusta's lower optimum applies directly here.

### Colombia

Colombia's 2025/26 crop was cut by 1.3 million bags to 12.5 million because excessive rain and cloud cover lowered yields; USDA forecasts 13.4 million for 2026/27. Climate Central counts 119 hot days a year, 48 of them added by climate change. Too much rain, not only heat, is the recent story here.

### Ethiopia

Ethiopia is on course for a record 12.1 million bags in 2026/27, which USDA attributes to more area and higher yields after more than half of the cultivated area was replanted with higher-yielding varieties over four years. It has the fewest added hot days of the top five (34).

The long-range picture is less comfortable, and the stakes are high: by Moat and colleagues' count, coffee supports around 15 million Ethiopian farmers and earns a quarter of the country's export income. Their 2017 study projected that 39–59% of Ethiopia's current growing area could become unsuitable for coffee under climate change, "in the absence of significant interventions," while moving coffee areas, combined with forest conservation or replanting, could increase the suitable area at least fourfold. For wild arabica in its native forests, Davis and colleagues (2012) projected losses by 2080 ranging from a 65% to an almost 100% reduction in the number of suitable localities, or 38% to about 90% of suitable area, depending on the scenario. Those forests hold the species' wild genetic diversity, which breeders draw on. For more on Ethiopia's coffees, see [Ethiopian heirloom vs named varieties](/blog/ethiopian-heirloom-vs-named-varietals/).

### Indonesia

Indonesia has the most added hot days of the top five, 73 a year. It is the third-largest robusta producer, after Vietnam and Brazil, on USDA's numbers (calculated from its table: 10.0 million bags of robusta against Brazil's 24.4 million). Its 2026/27 crop is forecast to fall 1.0 million bags to 11.4 million because excessive rain disrupted flowering and cherry formation in the lowlands of southern Sumatra and Java, where about 75% of its robusta is grown.

### Central America and Mexico

USDA forecasts 17.7 million bags for the region in 2026/27, with Honduras accounting for nearly all the growth. Guatemala's 2025/26 crop was cut to 3.2 million bags by coffee berry borer and leaf rust. El Salvador (99 added hot days) and Nicaragua (77) top Climate Central's list.

The region's leaf rust epidemic of 2012–13 is a well-documented example of weather and disease combining. Avelino and colleagues (2015) found Central American production fell 16% in 2013 compared with 2011–12, and another 10% in 2013–14. They identify economic and weather causes. Low profitability led to poorer farm management, which left plants more vulnerable, and a narrower daily temperature range likely shortened the disease's latency: higher minimums and lower maximums, by 0.9 °C and 1.2 °C on average in 2012 across 1,224 Guatemalan farms. They call the epidemics a warning, "enhanced by weather conditions consistent with climate change."

## Projections: how much land stays suitable

Models of future suitability give large numbers, and they vary.

- **Global.** Bunn and colleagues (2015) projected that climate change will reduce the global area suitable for coffee by about 50% across emission scenarios, with the biggest losses at low latitudes and low altitudes. Some areas in East Africa and Asia may become more suitable, but partly in forest. Climate Central's summary, that suitable land "may decrease by 50% by 2050 without adequate adaptation," is in the same range.
- **Latin America.** Imbach and colleagues (2017) projected that coffee-suitable areas will shrink by 73–88% by 2050 across warming scenarios, a decline they describe as 46–76% greater than global assessments. They also modeled bees, which pollinate coffee, and found bee richness falling by 8–18% within the areas that remain suitable.
- **Ethiopia.** Moat and colleagues' 39–59% (above), with a large potential gain if coffee can move.

These models map climate suitability, not yields, and they mostly assume no adaptation. Suitable land moving uphill also means pressure on forest: both Bunn's team and Climate Central warn that new coffee land may come from clearing it. For how altitude shapes coffee, see our [altitude guide](/blog/coffee-altitude-guide/), and for one of the newer regions, [Yunnan](/blog/china-yunnan-coffee-emerging-origin/).

## Adaptation: what the evidence says about shade

Growing coffee under shade trees, or agroforestry, is a common adaptation, and the evidence for it has been pooled. Patil and colleagues (2025) pooled 68 studies from 2000 to 2024 in a meta-analysis. Agroforestry was associated with lower odds of climate-related yield loss (odds ratio 0.67, 95% interval 0.56–0.80), of pests and disease (0.79, 0.67–0.95), and of quality loss (0.74, 0.63–0.86), with consistent results across regions. An odds ratio below 1 means the bad outcome was less likely with shade. The review also lists trade-offs, lower yields under dense shade and more humidity-related disease, and notes that most of the studies weren't pre-registered.

New varieties are the other main route, from rust-resistant cultivars to F1 hybrids; our [F1 hybrids post](/blog/coffee-f1-hybrids-future-of-coffee/) covers them.

## What it means for the coffee you buy

- **Expect volatility, not a straight line.** Climate Central notes coffee prices hit records in December 2024 and again in February 2025. By July 2026, USDA reported prices down 25% over seven months, as larger supplies arrived. Both are true at once: warming adds risk, and harvests still swing with each season's weather.
- **Origins will shift slowly.** Projections point to losses at lower, hotter elevations and possible gains at higher ones, but Bunn's team notes that plantations last about 30 years, so moving coffee takes a long time.
- **Stocks are still thin.** USDA counts global ending stocks down 15.5 million bags between 2020/21 and 2024/25, recovering to 26.3 million in 2026/27 but still below the long-term average. With stocks this low, a short crop has less of a cushion.
- **Small farms are the most exposed.** Climate Central notes that most coffee growers farm less than about 12 acres and may rely on this one crop, which makes them particularly vulnerable.
- **Robusta isn't a safe hedge.** If you drink blends with robusta, they depend on some of the most heat-exposed regions in the data.
