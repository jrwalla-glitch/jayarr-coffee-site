Green coffee is one of the richest foods there is in chlorogenic acids (CGAs). Roasting breaks most of them down, and what they break into accounts for much of the bitterness in your cup. They're also a large part of why coffee is called an antioxidant drink. This note covers what CGAs are, how roasting takes them apart, where the bitterness comes from, and how far the antioxidant claims go.

This note summarizes published research; the sources are at the end. The browning reactions that run alongside, and that also pull in some CGAs, are covered in [Maillard, caramelization and Strecker](/deep-brew/research/maillard-caramelization-strecker/).

## What chlorogenic acids are

Chlorogenic acids are a family, not one molecule. Each is an ester that joins quinic acid to one or two hydroxycinnamic acids: most often caffeic acid, then ferulic acid and p-coumaric acid. In their review, Adriana Farah and Carmen Donangelo group the ones in green coffee into caffeoylquinic acids (CQAs), dicaffeoylquinic acids (diCQAs), feruloylquinic acids (FQAs), p-coumaroylquinic acids, and mixed caffeoyl-feruloyl esters, each with at least three isomers.

One isomer dominates. 5-CQA, the compound usually meant by "chlorogenic acid" in the singular, makes up about 56–62% of the total in green coffee. 3-CQA and 4-CQA each reach up to about 10%, the diCQAs 15–20%, and the FQAs 5–13%.

How much a green bean holds depends mostly on species and ripeness. Farah and Donangelo give 4–8.4% of dry weight for arabica and 7–14.4% for canephora (robusta). The ranges overlap; at their midpoints, robusta carries about 1.7 times as much (calculated: 10.7% against 6.2%). An earlier version of this note said 1.5 to 2 times, using figures its own table didn't support.

Reported amounts also depend on how they were measured. Farah and Donangelo note that simpler methods and high-resolution chromatography can disagree. Moon, Yoo and Shibamoto measured 34–42 mg per gram in seven commercial green coffees, and 61–86 mg per gram in methanol–water extracts of four others. Treat any single figure as approximate.

## How roasting takes them apart

Roasting does three things to CGAs, according to Farah and Donangelo's review:

- **It rearranges them.** Early in the roast, the acid groups migrate around the quinic ring. Esters at the 5-position fall, and those at the 3- and 4-positions rise, in some cases almost doubling.
- **It splits them.** Some diCQAs lose one caffeic acid to become mono-esters. Some CGAs split into free caffeic and quinic acids, and the caffeic acid can break down further into simple phenols.
- **It closes some into lactones.** A CGA can lose water and form a ring inside itself, a 1,5-lactone or quinide. Only isomers without a group at the 5-position can do this, which is why 3-CQL and 4-CQL are the main lactones.

Lactones start forming after about 6–7% weight loss. In Farah and colleagues' 2005 roasting study of two arabicas and a robusta, the main lactone, 3-CQL, peaked at a light-medium roast of about 14% weight loss: 230 mg per 100 g of dry coffee in arabica and 254 mg in robusta. After that, the lactones decline. The same study found that the balance between 3-CQL and 4-CQL was the reverse of their parent acids in the green bean, which suggests the acids rearrange before they close into rings.

Overall, the losses are steep. Farah and Donangelo summarize earlier work: about 8–10% of the CGAs are lost for every 1% of dry matter lost, and drastic roasting can destroy up to 95%. Moon and colleagues roasted green coffee under set conditions and found the total fell to nearly half at 446°F&nbsp;(230°C) for 12 minutes, and to almost trace levels at 482°F&nbsp;(250°C) for 21 minutes. A meta-analysis of 129 publications by Yeager and colleagues found that roast level mattered more than species for how much CGA a roasted coffee holds.

Not all of it disappears into simple fragments. Part of the CGA pool is bound into melanoidins, the brown polymers of roasted coffee (Farah and Donangelo; Moreira and colleagues). The [melanoidins note](/deep-brew/research/melanoidins-body-color-health/) covers those.

The roaster Rob Hoos describes the same chemistry from the cupping table. In his experience, a development time after first crack that's too short leaves excess CGAs, which he tastes as "bitter and metallic" acidity, and he conjectures that vegetal notes in under-developed roasts may come from leftover CGAs. These are his cupping observations and his own hedged explanation, not measurements of CGAs. An earlier version of this note credited Hoos with measuring lactone distributions and said fast development preserves CGAs as a goal; he did neither.

## Where coffee's bitterness comes from

Thomas Hofmann's group at the Technical University of Munich set out to find which molecules make coffee bitter, using chromatography and a tasting panel trained to detect coffee bitterness. Their findings, as the American Chemical Society reported them in 2007:

- **The bitterness is mostly made in the roaster.** Hofmann's group reported in 2007 that chlorogenic acid itself isn't the bitter compound (other reviews still credit chlorogenic acids with some bitterness and astringency); the lactones and phenylindanes form during roasting, and neither class is present in green beans.
- **Lactones dominate in light to medium roasts.** The CGA lactones, about ten compounds, are "the dominant source of bitterness" in light-to-medium roast brews.
- **Phenylindanes dominate in darker roasts.** These breakdown products of the lactones are found at higher levels in dark roasts, including espresso, and have "a more lingering, harsh taste."
- **Caffeine is a small part.** Hofmann estimated that about 15% of coffee's perceived bitterness comes from caffeine, noting that caffeinated and decaffeinated coffees have similar bitterness.

The papers behind that summary fill in the chemistry. Frank and colleagues (2007) heated caffeic acid and got a bitterness reminiscent of strongly roasted espresso. From it they isolated ten bitter compounds, eight of them phenylindanes, with recognition thresholds of 23–178 micromoles per liter, and showed that all ten occur in coffee brew. Their structures point to one origin: 4-vinylcatechol, released from caffeic acid parts during roasting, linking up into oligomers.

Blumberg, Frank and Hofmann (2010) then roasted coffee in the lab, either at 500°F&nbsp;(260°C) for 1 to 10 minutes or for 4 minutes at 374–536°F&nbsp;(190–280°C). The lactones, which have a coffee-typical bitterness, formed mostly at slight to medium roasting. Under harsher roasting they broke down again into the harsh-tasting 4-vinylcatechol oligomers. The chemical shift matched the change in bitter quality their panel tasted.

So roasting changes what kind of bitterness a coffee has, not only how much. An earlier version of this note gave a table of percentage shares (lactones 60–70%, phenylindanes 10–15%, and so on). We couldn't find those numbers in Hofmann's papers or the ACS report, so the table is gone. Apart from Hofmann's caffeine estimate, the sources here don't give each compound's share.

**Quinic acid.** Quinic acid is released as CGAs split, and Rune and colleagues (2023) measured it rising with roast degree across their light roasts. James Hoffmann describes it as stable through roasting, adding that it "can add a pleasing, clean finish." It isn't one of the two bitter classes Hofmann's group identified.

**Robusta.** Robusta usually starts with more CGAs, and in Farah's 2005 study its main lactone peaked higher (254 against 230 mg per 100 g). More precursor gives more raw material for bitter compounds. How much of robusta's reputation for bitterness this explains, as against its caffeine or its roast, the sources here don't say.

## From bean to cup

Brewing pulls most of what's left into the cup. Farah and Donangelo cite Clifford's figures: home brewing extracts 80–100% of the CGAs, and much of the lactones, giving 70–200 mg of CGAs in a 200&nbsp;ml (about 6.8 US fl oz) cup of arabica and 70–350 mg for robusta. Hotter water below the boil extracts more, and most comes out early: the fastest extraction is in the first 2 minutes at 199°F&nbsp;(93°C). Keeping brewed coffee hot afterwards lowers its CQA and lactone content.

The bitter compounds don't all leave the grounds at the same speed. Blumberg and colleagues percolated hot water through ground coffee and found that the CQAs and the mono-caffeoyl lactones came out quickly, the dicaffeoyl lactones slowly, and most of the 4-vinylcatechol oligomers clung strongly to the grounds. They concluded that controlling roasting and extraction "might be helpful in tailoring the bitter taste signature" of a cup. A reasonable reading, though not one they tested directly, is that a longer or more forceful extraction pulls more of the harsh dark-roast compounds out of the grounds. The ACS report adds that espresso-type brewing, with high pressure and high temperature, tended to produce the highest levels of bitter compounds.

In practice, that splits bitterness into two questions:

- **Is it the roast?** The lactones and phenylindanes are made in the roaster. If a dark roast tastes harsh and lingering at a normal brew time, much of that bitterness probably came from the roaster.
- **Is it the brew?** A grind that's too fine, water that's too hot or a brew that runs long extracts more of everything, including the slow-releasing harsh compounds.

A lighter roast is the bigger change if you want less of the harsh, lingering kind. Brewing a dark roast a touch coarser or cooler can take the edge off, but it can't turn phenylindanes back into lactones. ## Antioxidants: what the evidence supports

CGAs are strong antioxidants in the test tube. Farah and Donangelo describe them as the main phenolic compounds in green coffee, with potent antioxidant activity. Hofmann's group noted that the bitter lactones and phenylindanes are antioxidants too. So roasting doesn't simply swap antioxidants for bitterness: it changes which compounds are present.

Coffee looms large in antioxidant intake because people drink so much of it. Svilaas and colleagues (2004) estimated antioxidant intake from food records of 61 Norwegian adults and a national diet survey of 2,672. Total intake came to about 17 mmol a day, of which coffee supplied about 11.1 mmol, about two-thirds (calculated). It was the single largest source. Antioxidant intake here is a laboratory measure of what's in the food; it doesn't show what those compounds do once swallowed.

Studies in people are less clear-cut. Martini and colleagues (2016) reviewed 26 intervention studies in which people drank coffee, either once or regularly. Coffee could raise glutathione and improve protection against DNA damage, especially with regular drinking. Its effects on blood antioxidant capacity, antioxidant enzymes, and damage to proteins and fats were unclear. The authors put much of the inconsistency down to differences in coffees, doses, study length and the markers measured. These studies tested coffee, not CGAs on their own.

Lighter roasts keep more CGAs, so they deliver more of them per cup. Whether that makes them better for you, these sources don't show. An earlier version of this note made claims about diabetes, liver cancer and "five servings of fruit and vegetables" with no studies cited; they've been removed.
