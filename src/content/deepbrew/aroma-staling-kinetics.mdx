Roasted coffee starts losing aroma as soon as it leaves the roaster. The losses are uneven: the lightest, most sulfurous odorants go first, oils oxidize much later, and the pace depends on whether the coffee is whole, ground or already brewed. Each runs on its own clock.

This note summarizes published research; the sources are at the end. It covers the chemistry of aroma loss. For the practical side, such as when beans usually taste best, how to store them and how to freeze them, see our [coffee freshness timeline](/blog/coffee-freshness-roast-to-stale/), the [storage guide](/blog/how-to-store-coffee-beans/) and the [freezing guide](/blog/how-to-freeze-coffee-beans/).

## Which odorants go first

Roasted coffee holds more than a thousand volatile compounds, but only a few dozen carry its aroma; our note on [coffee's flavor volatiles](/deep-brew/research/flavor-volatiles-oav-guide/) covers which ones and how they were ranked. Staling is a story about the order in which those few dozen leave or change.

The first to go are the smallest and most volatile, and many of them contain sulfur. Wilhardi Holscher and Hans Steinhart sniffed the gas above freshly roasted, freshly ground Colombian coffee as it came off a gas chromatograph, diluting the sample step by step. Methanethiol and 2-methylpropanal were the last odors still detectable, which makes them the most intense; 2,3-butanedione and 2,3-pentanedione gave strong buttery notes. In their storage test, methanethiol showed the largest drop of anything they measured, recognizable one day after roasting, and it was also the compound that best separated fresh coffee from 10-day-old coffee in their statistics.

Otto Vitzthum, reviewing thirty years of industry research, describes the same sequence. Methanethiol gives fresh coffee its "sense" of aroma freshness. At room temperature it and other low-boiling sulfur compounds evaporate, while heavier ones such as 2-furfurylthiol stay behind and, in his words, "cause an 'aging' note". Oxygen turns the light thiols into disulfides, and dimethyl sulfide, an oxidation product of methanethiol, rises. He reports that no true "staling" compounds were found; hexanal, a breakdown product of linoleic acid in the oil, increases only after a longer time.

Concentration matters as well as presence. Stefan Schenker's literature review reports an older finding by Tressl and Silwar (1981): 2-furfurylthiol smelled like freshly roasted coffee at 0.01 to 0.5 parts per billion, and like stale coffee with a sulfury note at 1 to 10 parts per billion. So staling is partly a change in the balance between odorants, not only a loss of them.

Jonathan Gagné, in *The Physics of Filter Coffee*, groups the processes into three. Carbon dioxide leaves the beans. Aromas degas too, each at its own rate, faster with heat, with a bigger pressure difference between the inside of the bean and the air, and with moisture. And oils migrate to the surface, where they can oxidize. Because aromas leave at different rates, he writes, the coffee's smell and taste evolve, and those changes "will not necessarily be negative in the short term".

## Whole beans: days to weeks

Holscher and Steinhart's storage test is the clearest measurement of the early losses. They roasted Colombian coffee to a medium degree, stored 500 g lots in covered glass containers at 68°F&nbsp;(20°C) with light kept out but no protection from oxygen, and ground a sample for each analysis. The biggest changes in the light volatiles came within three weeks. Methanethiol fell to about 30% of its starting level after 8 days and to 10–20% after 3 weeks. Reference samples kept under nitrogen at −40°F&nbsp;(−40°C) barely changed over 100 days.

Schenker's own storage tests, on beans left exposed to air at room temperature, found that each compound behaved individually. Acetic acid and 2,3-butanedione lost little; all the other compounds he listed lost more or less heavily. He also found the oil fraction fairly resistant to oxidation, with only small amounts of the alkanes that mark lipid oxidation, and his results suggest that oxidation in stored beans mainly affects compounds other than the oils. He proposes that staling comes from three things working together: the bean's pore structure, the escape of gas, and oxidation.

Roast degree changes the pace. Gagné, citing Schenker, writes that darker roasts tend to lose aroma faster because of their larger internal pores.

Months are a different scale. Michaela Kreuml and colleagues had 10 trained assessors score 30 attributes of paper-filtered coffee brewed from vacuum-packed beans, soon after roasting and again after 9 and 18 months. By 9 months the pleasant odor and flavor attributes had weakened and the unpleasant ones had increased significantly; by 18 months rancid notes, a sign of oxidation, were clearly perceptible.

## Ground coffee: gas in seconds, aroma over days

Grinding breaks open the pores that hold the gas. Samo Smrke and colleagues weighed coffee as it degassed and found that, for freshly roasted coffee, up to 75% of the trapped gas was released during and immediately after grinding, within about 90 seconds. They attribute the jump to the destruction of the larger pores. Gagné explains the aroma side the same way: aromas degas much like carbon dioxide, and a ground particle gives oils a far shorter path to the surface than a whole bean.

That gas measurement is the fast part. How soon the aroma loss becomes a stale cup is slower and harder to pin down. Monica Anese and colleagues stored dark-roasted ground Arabica at 86°F&nbsp;(30°C) in bottles they opened for a minute every day, to mimic a kitchen. Their panel sniffed the dry coffee and said whether they would still brew it. Half the panel rejected it at about 20 days when the coffee was dry, and at about 13 days at the dampest setting they tested. Whatever the moisture, rejection came when the measured headspace volatiles had fallen to about 60% of their starting level, about 40% lost (pp. 5574–5575). A sniff test on dry grounds is a low bar, and the study doesn't say how the coffee tasted brewed.

Krešimir Marin and colleagues followed a ground blend for a year at about 73°F&nbsp;(23°C) in packs filled with air or nitrogen, or punctured. Most volatiles fell in every sample, including the reference kept under nitrogen at −4°F&nbsp;(−20°C), so freezing slowed the loss rather than stopping it. The ratio of 2-furfurylthiol to hexanal tracked the aroma freshness of brews made from the stored coffee best, with correlation coefficients of 0.80 or higher.

You'll often read that ground coffee is stale 15 to 30 minutes after grinding. None of these studies measured such a threshold. The gas measurements show how fast grinding releases what's trapped, and the sensory studies show losses building over days.

## Brewed coffee: the first hour

The fastest aroma loss happens in the cup. Zhenchun Sun and colleagues stored fresh coffee brew for 60 minutes and measured how much of each odorant remained available, that is, not bound. Available 2-furfurylthiol fell by 84%, methanethiol by 72% and 3-methyl-1H-pyrrole by 68%, while 2-pentylfuran rose by 65%. The abstract doesn't give the holding temperature, and we couldn't open the full paper, so this note doesn't state one. What it does settle is that the 84% describes brewed coffee, not beans or grounds.

The same study looked at why. Adding the amino acid cysteine released bound 2-furfurylthiol, so the authors concluded that a reversible binding reaction was the main route of loss in ordinary brew, alongside some irreversible loss. The balance depended on pH: at high pH the reversible binding protected 2-furfurylthiol from permanent loss, while at low pH irreversible losses cut the total. In a model system they reacted 2-furfurylthiol with the phenol hydroxyhydroquinone, and cysteine released it again at high pH.

What the thiols bind to has been studied for two decades:

- **Melanoidins.** Thomas Hofmann and Peter Schieberle mixed the volatiles from a fresh brew with the brown melanoidins isolated from coffee. Five odorous thiols dropped sharply in the headspace, 2-furfurylthiol most of all, by a factor of 16, and the roasty, sulfury aroma weakened with them. Keeping a brew warm in a thermos flask also caused a rapid loss of all the thiols. An earlier model study by the same group found that the small melanoidins, about 1,500 to 3,000 daltons, cut 2-furfurylthiol the most, while aldehydes were unaffected. They traced the binding to pyrazinium structures formed in the Maillard reaction. Our [melanoidins note](/deep-brew/research/melanoidins-body-color-health/) covers the polymers themselves.
- **Small phenols.** Christoph Müller and Hofmann's group identified phenols formed as chlorogenic acids break down in roasting as thiol traps, among them hydroxyhydroquinone. In stored brew, about 60% of the starting 2-furfurylthiol had reacted with hydroxyhydroquinone after only 10 minutes, and they concluded that this coupling accounts for most of the loss of 2-furfurylthiol during storage.
- **Quinones.** Sun and colleagues returned in 2020 and found that quinones, the oxidation products of hydroxyhydroquinone, bind 2-furfurylthiol. Adding 0.045 g/L of cysteine and 0.05 g/L of ascorbic acid protected several odorants during storage, and their panel found more nutty, sulfurous and roasted aroma and preferred the treated coffee over its shelf life. That was a lab study, not advice for home brewing.

Taste changes too, though more slowly. Vitzthum, summarizing a 1998 patent, attributes the sour, bitter taste of coffee left for hours on a hot plate to chlorogenic acid lactones breaking down into quinic and caffeic acids.

## Heat, oxygen and moisture

Gagné lists five enemies of freshness: time, heat, moisture, oxygen and UV light. The studies above put some numbers to three of them.

- **Heat.** Gagné cites a calculation by Labuza and colleagues: coffee in the same package keeps about 21 weeks at 72°F&nbsp;(22°C) and about 16 weeks at 95°F&nbsp;(35°C). That's about a quarter shorter, by our arithmetic. Holscher and Steinhart's reference beans at −40°F&nbsp;(−40°C) under nitrogen barely changed in 100 days.
- **Oxygen.** It turns light thiols into disulfides (Vitzthum), and over months it brings the rancid notes that Kreuml's team read as oxidation. A one-way valve bag helps because escaping carbon dioxide pushes oxygen out, as Gagné explains.
- **Moisture.** In Anese's ground coffee, the dampest samples lost volatiles fastest and were rejected about a week sooner than the drier ones.

Freezing slows all of this, but it doesn't stop it. Marin's frozen reference still lost volatiles over a year. Our [freezing guide](/blog/how-to-freeze-coffee-beans/) covers how to do it well.

## What it means in practice

The chemistry explains the standard advice and sets limits on it.

- **Grind just before you brew.** Grinding releases up to three-quarters of the trapped gas within about 90 seconds and shortens every path out of the coffee. The [freshness timeline](/blog/coffee-freshness-roast-to-stale/) puts the gas numbers in context.
- **Brew what you'll drink soon.** The fastest measured losses happen in brewed coffee, within minutes to an hour. A thermos keeps coffee warm, but in Hofmann and Schieberle's test the thiols disappeared in a warm thermos too.
- **Keep beans sealed, cool, dark and dry.** Light volatiles leave in days, oils oxidize over months, and heat and moisture speed both. The [storage guide](/blog/how-to-store-coffee-beans/) turns this into everyday choices.

The windows our Freshness tool uses (most coffees taste best 7 to 21 days after roasting and stay good to about day 30) describe how the cup usually tastes, not any one compound. The measurements here show the most volatile odorants falling from the first day. Very fresh beans can still brew worse, because trapped gas gets in the way of even extraction; the freshness timeline and our post on [resting beans for espresso](/blog/bean-resting-for-espresso-freshness-trap/) explain why. That matches Gagné's point that early changes in the aroma profile aren't necessarily for the worse. The tool's taste curve (it labels it "how it tastes" and calls it illustrative) rises while the beans rest; the studies in this note don't show the most volatile odorants rising, so read that curve as a picture of the cup, not of the chemistry.
