Green coffee has almost no flavor beyond what James Hoffmann calls "a quite unpleasant vegetal taste." Most of the aromatics in a good cup, he writes, are created during roasting, through three groups of reactions: the Maillard reaction, caramelization and Strecker degradation. Together they can create more than 800 volatile compounds, though any one coffee has only a selection of them.

This note maps those three reactions: what reacts in each, what each one makes, where they overlap, and how much a roaster can steer them. It's the chemistry hub for the other research notes, which take single threads further. This note summarizes published research; the sources are at the end.

| Reaction | Starts from | Makes |
|---|---|---|
| Maillard | Sugars and amino acids | Dicarbonyls, furfurals, pyrazines, brown melanoidins |
| Strecker | Maillard dicarbonyls and amino acids | Aldehydes one carbon shorter; sulfur compounds |
| Caramelization | Sugars alone | Caramel compounds, acids, later bitterness |

*Table sources: Hodge's scheme as summarized by Charnock and colleagues (2022); Nursten's classification as quoted by Hoos (2015); Hoffmann (2018), p. 59. Each row is explained below, with the aromas each one makes.*

## The Maillard reaction in three stages

The Maillard reaction is a cascade, not a single step: a sugar and an amino group react, and their products keep reacting. Food chemists still describe it with the scheme Hodge proposed in 1953, which has three stages. Charnock, Pickering and Kemp's 2022 review lays them out:

1. **Early stage.** The carbonyl group of a reducing sugar joins the amino group of an amino acid, peptide or protein. The product rearranges into a stable Amadori product (from sugars such as glucose) or Heyns product (from sugars such as fructose). These products are colorless.
2. **Intermediate stage.** The Amadori and Heyns products break down into reactive dicarbonyl compounds. The route depends on acidity. Under acidic conditions, the reaction runs toward furfural and hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF). Under neutral or alkaline conditions, it runs toward reductones and other dicarbonyls. This is the stage that matters most for aroma, Charnock and colleagues write: it generates bready, toasted, caramel, meaty, nutty, baked and burnt odors.
3. **Advanced stage.** With strong heat, the intermediates dehydrate and link up into melanoidins: brown, nitrogen-containing polymers that carry most of the color.

In a roaster, visible browning speeds up as the beans lose their moisture and heat up. Hoffmann describes green coffee as 7–11% water and, as a roaster's shorthand, says coffee won't brown while that water is present. The first browning starts at yellowing, once the beans have dried and smell of basmati rice and bread. Andrea Illy and Rinantonio Viani, quoted by the roaster Rob Hoos, describe the Maillard reaction as the source of the colored melanoidins and of "the main part of the organic volatiles."

What comes out of the cascade is easiest to grasp by family. Hoos quotes a classification by the food chemist Nursten, via Ivon Flament's *Coffee Flavor Chemistry*. It has three groups:

- **Sugar breakdown products:** furans, pyrones, cyclopentenes, carbonyl compounds and acids.
- **Amino acid breakdown products:** aldehydes and sulfur compounds. These are the Strecker products below.
- **Products of further reactions between those:** pyrroles, pyridines, imidazoles, pyrazines, oxazoles and thiazoles.

Coffee's melanoidins are still only partly understood. Moreira and colleagues' 2012 review defines them as high-molecular-weight, nitrogen-containing brown compounds, built with polysaccharides, proteins and [chlorogenic acids](/deep-brew/research/chlorogenic-acid-cascades/), and says their exact structures and formation are "far to be elucidated." Hoos links them to body and mouthfeel from his cupping experience. The [melanoidins note](/deep-brew/research/melanoidins-body-color-health/) covers what they do.

## Strecker degradation: aldehydes one carbon shorter

Strecker degradation is a side branch of the Maillard reaction's intermediate stage. A dicarbonyl from the Maillard cascade reacts with a free amino acid. The amino acid loses its amino group and then its carboxyl group, and what's left is an aldehyde with one carbon fewer than the amino acid it came from (Hodge, as summarized by Charnock and colleagues).

Because each amino acid gives its own aldehyde, the green bean's amino acids shape some of the roasted aroma. In model wine experiments that Charnock and colleagues summarize, valine gave 2-methylpropanal, leucine 3-methylbutanal, isoleucine 2-methylbutanal, and phenylalanine phenylacetaldehyde. Methionine's Strecker product is methional, which the review lists among the Strecker products found in aged sparkling wine. The same review's table describes 2-methylpropanal and 2-methylbutanal as malty, methional as cooked potato, and phenylacetaldehyde as honey and floral.

Several of these matter in coffee. Czerny, Mayer and Grosch measured the potent odorants in a roasted arabica and rebuilt its aroma from 27 of them, dissolved in an oil-and-water mix. The model's flavor profile came very close to the real coffee's. They then compared it, in duo and triangle tests, with models missing one or more odorants. Methylpropanal and 2- and 3-methylbutanal were among those with "the greatest impact on the coffee flavor," together with 2-furfurylthiol, 4-vinylguaiacol, several alkylpyrazines, furanones, acetaldehyde and propanal.

**The sulfur route.** 2-Furfurylthiol, one of Czerny's high-impact odorants, contains sulfur. In model experiments, Hofmann and Schieberle (1998) heated sugars with the sulfur amino acid cysteine: pentoses gave far more 2-furfurylthiol than hexoses, though glucose also gave a significant yield. Furfural reacting with hydrogen sulfide was about ten times more efficient at making it than the other pair of intermediates they tested. Furfural is a sugar breakdown product, so 2-furfurylthiol sits where the browning reactions meet. The [aroma compounds note](/deep-brew/research/flavor-volatiles-oav-guide/) covers it among coffee's odorants, and the [aroma staling note](/deep-brew/research/aroma-staling-kinetics/) covers how fast it fades.

**Pyrazines.** Alkylpyrazines were also among Czerny's high-impact odorants. In Nursten's classification, pyrazines come from the further reactions between sugar and amino acid breakdown products, so they depend on the cascade having run on past its early stage.

An earlier version of this note gave odor activity values for each Strecker aldehyde. We couldn't trace those numbers to a study, so they're gone. Published odor thresholds also vary widely: one review lists reported thresholds for furaneol, a caramel-smelling furanone, from 0.03 to 1,700 micrograms per liter. A single odor activity value built on one threshold is less certain than it looks.

## Caramelization: sugar on its own

Caramelization is what sugar does when it's heated without amino acids. In a roasting bean, both routes compete for the same sugars. Hoffmann describes the split: once the water has evaporated, some sugars caramelize, "creating the caramel notes found in certain coffees," while others react with proteins in the Maillard reaction. The sugars that caramelize, he adds, become less sweet and eventually start to add bitterness. By the end of first crack, there are "few or no simple sugars left."

Sugar breakdown also makes acids. Rune and colleagues, citing Ginz and colleagues (2000), note that acetic, formic, lactic and glycolic acids form from the thermal breakdown of soluble carbohydrates, mainly sucrose, glucose and fructose. In their own brews of light roasts, acetic, lactic and glycolic acids rose with roast degree. Hoos, quoting Illy and Viani, names formic and acetic acids as the most prominent acids from the Maillard reaction and caramelization.

**Caramel smell isn't only caramelization.** Furaneol (4-hydroxy-2,5-dimethyl-3(2H)-furanone) has a caramel-like odor. But it was first reported, in 1960, as a product of the Maillard reaction, and Schwab's review describes furanones forming from various sugars during the Maillard reaction. An earlier version of this note said furaneol was "purely a caramelization event"; that was wrong. Furanones were among Czerny's high-impact odorants.

How sugar's disappearance squares with the sweetness people taste in roasted coffee is the subject of the [sweetness note](/deep-brew/research/sweetness-paradox-roasting/).

## Where the three reactions meet

The three reactions share raw materials and intermediates, so they can't be run one at a time:

- **The same sugars feed both browning routes.** As Hoffmann describes, some sugars caramelize and others react with proteins.
- **Furfural comes from both.** Acidic Maillard conditions favor furfural intermediates, which also form when sugars break down under acid (Charnock and colleagues). Either way, furfural can go on to make 2-furfurylthiol.
- **Strecker aldehydes keep reacting.** They can react with each other and with sugar-derived intermediates, re-entering the cascade, or be drawn into melanoidins (Charnock and colleagues).
- **Other bean compounds join in.** Chlorogenic acids are bound into melanoidins (Moreira and colleagues). The [chlorogenic acids note](/deep-brew/research/chlorogenic-acid-cascades/) follows the rest of that story.

Hoos makes the same point from the roaster: after first crack, the Maillard reaction is meeting new reactants from caramelization, acid breakdown and pyrolysis. He says he can't change its course after first crack without changing the other reactions too.

## What a roast profile can steer

Roasters can't set the reactions directly. They control heat and time, and taste the result. Hoffmann describes a roast as the product of two things: the final color, and how long it took to get there. Two coffees roasted to the same color, one fast and one slow, taste different. His troubleshooting rule for home roasters: a coffee that "lacks flavours and sweetness" may have been roasted too slowly, and one that tastes "very sour, astringent and grassy" probably too quickly. A roast that stalls around first crack can "bake" the coffee.

**Hoos's MAI experiments.** Hoos calls the time from yellowing to first crack the MAI, for Maillard-to-first-crack interval. It isn't the whole Maillard reaction, which continues after first crack. He roasted four coffees (one of them in two rounds) with a shorter and a longer MAI, aiming to hold the other phases within 10 seconds, and cupped them blind. In most of these trials, a longer MAI gave a heavier body and "heavier," more complex descriptors. His example of the progression is brown sugar, then maple syrup, then honey and vanilla, then molasses. He reports exceptions, two of which he calls outliers. An earlier version of this note said his data covered "five origins" and showed the effect "consistently"; it's four coffees, with exceptions he reports himself. The [cultivar note](/deep-brew/research/cultivar-roast-profiles-hoos/) covers his later roast timings by variety.

**Development and end temperature.** Hoos ties the time after first crack mainly to the balance of organic acids, and the end temperature mainly to how far caramelization goes. Those threads continue in the [chlorogenic acids note](/deep-brew/research/chlorogenic-acid-cascades/) and the [sweetness note](/deep-brew/research/sweetness-paradox-roasting/). For the practical side of roasting, see [how to roast coffee at home](/blog/how-to-roast-coffee-at-home/).

**The pressure argument.** Hoos also argues that fast roasts build higher pressure inside the bean, up to 25 atm, a figure he quotes from Illy and Viani. Higher pressure, he argues, suppresses Maillard volatiles and melanoidins, so fast roasts taste thinner and less complex. His evidence for that step is a study by Bristow and Isaacs (1999). That study used a model Maillard reaction in solution, not coffee, and Charnock and colleagues report its pressures as above 5,000 atm.

<p class="eq">5,000 atm ÷ 25 atm = 200</p>

That is, the study's pressure was at least 200 times the highest pressure Hoos cites for a roasting bean (calculated). Charnock and colleagues make the same caution about wine: such studies use pressures far above the 6 atm in a bottle of sparkling wine, so their relevance there is questionable. Hoos's observation that fast roasts can taste thinner comes from his cupping. The pressure mechanism behind it hasn't been tested at bean pressures, so treat it as a hypothesis.

## What it means in your cup

Much of what makes coffee smell like coffee is made in the roaster by these reactions: the malty Strecker aldehydes, the roasted pyrazines, the caramel-like furanones and 2-furfurylthiol. Roast degree moves the balance, as our guide to [roast levels](/blog/coffee-roast-levels-explained/) describes. Hoffmann describes how, by second crack, much of the acidity is gone and a generic "roast" flavor develops that comes from charring, whatever the coffee.

These aromas also fade after roasting, as the [aroma staling note](/deep-brew/research/aroma-staling-kinetics/) describes. When a coffee tastes flat, the roast is one suspect, but the beans' age and your water are easier to check first. The [freshness guide](/blog/coffee-freshness-roast-to-stale/) covers roast dates and storage.
