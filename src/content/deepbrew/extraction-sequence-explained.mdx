This note summarizes published research; the sources are at the end. It covers one question: in what order do coffee's compounds come out of the grounds, and how sure is that order? For calculating extraction yield, the SCA's reference ranges by edition and the UC Davis preference studies, see [coffee extraction yield explained](/blog/coffee-extraction-yield-explained/). For how strength and extraction differ, see [strength vs extraction](/blog/coffee-strength-vs-extraction/).

The usual teaching story says acids dissolve first, then sugars and browning products, then bitter compounds, so a cup moves from sour to sweet to bitter as extraction rises. Part of that is well supported and part of it isn't. Here is what has actually been measured.

## Why compounds come out at different speeds

Jonathan Gagné's *The Physics of Filter Coffee* (2021) builds the explanation in steps. First a compound has to dissolve inside a coffee cell. Gagné describes this with the Noyes–Whitney equation (pp. 7–8):

<p class="eq">dm/dt = D·A·(C<sub>s</sub> − C) / L</p>

In words: a solid dissolves faster when its exposed area A is larger and when the water around it is cleaner. C<sub>s</sub> is the concentration right at the solid, C the concentration a short distance L away, and D the compound's diffusion coefficient. As the surrounding water gets stronger, the bracket shrinks and dissolving slows.

The term that differs from one compound to the next is D. Gagné notes that D is each compound's own mobility multiplied by temperature (and Boltzmann's constant), so warmer water speeds up every compound (p. 8), though not all by the same amount (pp. 10–11). Once dissolved, a compound still has to diffuse out to the surface of the particle, and that step also depends on D. Citing Moroney and colleagues (2019), he writes that trigonelline and caffeine diffuse quickly and come out even from large particles, while oils tend to stay behind in them (p. 7).

In his 2019 post "The Dynamics of Coffee Extraction," Gagné models each compound as extracting at a rate that decays exponentially with its own characteristic time:

<p class="eq">rate of compound i ∝ e<sup>−t/τ<sub>i</sub></sup></p>

That reads: every compound starts out fastest and slows, and τ<sub>i</sub> sets how quickly. After about five characteristic times, little more of that compound comes out. He adds that water reaches deeper layers of a particle more slowly, using a characteristic depth of about 100 µm (a Barista Hustle estimate), so a large particle holds back its slow compounds longer than a small one.

Grinding adds a second mechanism. The smallest particles, the fines, consist only of broken cells, and broken cells give up their soluble material almost at once, washed out by the water rather than slowly diffusing (pp. 6–7). Between the fines and the different diffusion speeds, Gagné expects a change of grind to change the flavor even when brew time is adjusted to reach the same extraction yield (p. 7).

## First out

The best direct measurement comes from espresso. Schmieder and colleagues (2023) collected ten consecutive fractions from each shot and measured trigonelline, caffeine, 5-caffeoylquinic acid (5-CQA, one of the chlorogenic acids) and total dissolved solids. Every one of them was most concentrated in the first fraction and fell exponentially after that. Trigonelline and total solids fell fastest, then 5-CQA, then caffeine; the authors note that earlier work linked this order to the compounds' polarity.

So "first" doesn't mean that one class of compound comes out alone at the start. Everything comes out fastest at the start, and the classes differ in how quickly they run down. Caffeine, for example, kept coming through the shot longer than the other three.

On acids, Fuller and Rao (2017) note in their cold brew paper that "most acids in coffee are highly soluble and extract quickly," though they measured no acids besides 3-CGA themselves. In their measurements, caffeine and 3-CGA (another chlorogenic acid) followed first-order kinetics and leveled off between 6 and 7 hours at room temperature, well before the 10 to 24 hours many cold brew recipes call for. Grind size made no significant difference to how much of either compound came out (Results and Discussion; Table 2). A year later, Rao and Fuller (2018) compared hot and cold brews of six light-roasted coffees. The pH was similar (4.85 to 5.13), but every hot brew contained more titratable acid than its cold counterpart. The authors conclude that water temperature changes how much acid comes out, and possibly which acids, not just how fast.

## Last out

The slow end of the sequence is better supported than the fast end. In his book, Gagné writes that above about 28% average extraction a cup would contain almost every dissolvable compound in the coffee, and that "some of the slower-extracting compounds can taste harsh, bitter, and/or metallic" and feel astringent (p. 4). In a note added to his 2019 post, he says the evidence is stronger on this side: bitterness and astringency belong to the slow-extracting compounds.

After talking with the coffee scientist Samo Smrke, he later proposed that astringency is partly a filtration problem (2022). His hypothesis is that the compounds responsible dissolve poorly in water, so they float in the slurry undissolved, and the coffee bed has to filter them out on the way to the cup. That would explain his puzzling observation that a 10-minute steep tasted fine when sampled from the top, but astringent after it had drained through the bed. In one test, brewing very finely ground coffee at 70 °C, he reached 27% extraction with no astringency he could detect, but the cup was less interesting, with much less vibrant acidity. These are his own trials, not a controlled study.

## The middle, where the sweet story breaks down

The weakest part of the teaching story is the middle: the idea that sweetness lives in a fixed band of extraction yield, after the acids and before the bitters.

Gagné himself is careful here. In the same 2019 note he calls the link between fast-extracting compounds and "vegetal and sour" tastes speculative, and says the extraction numbers in his post are examples, not absolutes. In his book he writes only that a coffee extracted to about 15% "would likely lack sweetness and complexity" (p. 4).

The UC Davis studies tested taste directly. Frost and colleagues (2020) brewed one coffee at three roast levels to nine combinations of strength and extraction. Their trained panel found that 21 of 30 attributes changed, and that sweetness was inversely correlated with strength for every roast. Guinard and colleagues (2023) combined that work with two later studies into new brewing charts, and two of their findings matter here:

- **'Sweet' is not sugar.** They write the attribute in quotation marks because it "most likely does not represent true sweet taste." It is a flavor impression that comes with sweet-smelling aromas and with the absence of bitterness and, to a lesser extent, sourness. Sugars are not usually present in brewed coffee above the level at which sweetness can be tasted (pp. 2171–2172).
- **People split.** In their consumer data, 57% liked coffee best at low strength, with liking peaking at 18% extraction, right where 'sweet' sits on their chart. The other 43% liked two different kinds of cup equally: low extraction at medium to high strength, and high extraction at medium strength (p. 2172).

So a sweet impression can peak near the middle of the classic chart for one group of drinkers, but it isn't a dissolved-sugar phase. In their simplified chart, the UC Davis team labels Lockhart's familiar "ideal" box the "classic standard" instead, since it is unclear which consumers found it ideal (p. 2173). The SCA's current chart (2019) uses that 18–22% with a strength band of 1.15–1.45% TDS; the older Golden Cup band is 1.15–1.35%. Gagné's own view is that the best extraction depends on roasting, grinder performance, brewing method and green coffee quality, as well as personal preference (p. 3). Taste can also move unevenly: Rao has described a pleasant peak near 15%, a dip from 15% to 18% that most people dislike, and improvement past 18% (as summarized by Gagné, p. 3).

## Evenness: one average, many extractions

Extraction yield is an average over every cell in the bed. Gagné's example: two cups at 20% can be made of cells all extracted to 20%, or of some cells at 28% and others at 15%, and they will taste different (p. 4). Fines push a bed toward the second case, because they give up everything at once while large particles lag. In his 2019 examples, a brew where the particles extract evenly can reach 22–24% before too much of the slow, bitter material comes through, while equipment that extracts unevenly tops out around 20–21%. He presents those numbers as examples, not limits. For how fines behave in espresso, see our note on [espresso puck physics](/deep-brew/research/espresso-physics-darcy-law/).

## Temperature, time and brewing method

**Temperature.** In Gagné's model, hotter water doesn't just speed extraction up; it changes the mix, because each compound's diffusion responds differently (pp. 10–11). Two studies found less effect than that suggests over normal brewing temperatures. Batali and colleagues (2020) brewed drip coffee at 87, 90 and 93 °C to the same strength and extraction, adjusting grind and time, and their panel found no appreciable difference. In Schmieder's espresso study, changing the water from 80 to 98 °C had no statistically significant effect on the four quantities they measured in the cup in that experiment (§3.3, Table 3 and Conclusions). Gagné's own practice with a V60 is to brew light roasts with water off the boil and darker roasts cooler, from 88 to 96 °C depending on roast (p. 11). For a practical range, see our guide to [brewing temperature](/blog/whats-the-ideal-coffee-brewing-temperature/).

**Immersion and percolation.** In an unstirred immersion, only diffusion moves dissolved compounds away from the grounds, and extraction slows as the brew gets stronger. Gagné notes this makes immersion less sensitive to brew time and easier to repeat, and that compounds which diffuse faster tend to be overrepresented in the cup. In percolation, fresh water keeps carrying compounds away, so the brew tracks what is at the particle surface, and the bed filters out fine material (pp. 12–13).

**Time.** Extraction slows as each compound runs down, so a longer brew mostly adds the slow fraction. But "too long" depends on the method: Gagné's 10-minute steep tasted fine until it drained, and cold brew needs hours to reach the same compounds that hot water extracts in minutes.

## What it means for your cup

The sequence gives a useful way to read a cup, with the caveats above. Harsh and drying points to the slow end of the sequence, or to an uneven bed where some grounds overextracted; that is the better-supported link. Thin and sour is usually read as stopping early, though the evidence that sour compounds come out first is weaker. Either way, taste is a hypothesis. A refractometer reading tells you where your cup sits, at least for the average.
