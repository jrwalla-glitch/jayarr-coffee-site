A drip machine controls the water for you: how hot it is, how fast it falls and for how long. What's left to you is the coffee, how much of it, how it's ground, a couple of stirs, and what happens to the pot afterward. Those are enough to make a big difference.

## Start with the ratio

Weigh both. A machine's "cup" is its maker's own measure, not a measuring cup: Moccamaster rates its KBGV Select at 1.25 liters, or 10 cups, which works out to 125 ml a cup (calculated), about half of an 8 fl oz measuring cup. Read the carafe's milliliter marks, or weigh the water as you fill the tank.

Three published starting points sit close together:

- **Our Brew Guide** starts a drip machine at 1:17, with 1:15.5 as its stronger setting and 1:18 as its lighter one.
- **The SCA's home-brewer standard** (SCA 310-2021) tests machines at 55 g of coffee per kilogram of water, about 1:18 (calculated: 1,000 ÷ 55 = 18.2).
- **Scott Rao**, in *The Professional Barista's Handbook*, uses 3.75 oz of coffee to 64 oz of water, which he derived from the Coffee Brewing Center's standards through secondary sources. That's about 106 g to 1,893 g, or 1:17.8 (calculated, taking the water as US fluid ounces). The Brew Guide's drip recipe is scaled from it.

Here's what the Brew Guide's 1:17 and the SCA's 55 g/kg come to at common batch sizes. The last column is calculated the way the Brew Guide does it: the wet grounds hold back about 2 g of water for every gram of coffee.

| 500&nbsp;g (16.9&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz) | 29.4&nbsp;g; 27.5&nbsp;g | about 441&nbsp;g (14.9&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz) |

The Brew Guide stops at 1,800 g of water, which it treats as more than a typical drip machine holds; check your own machine's maximum. Our [coffee-to-water ratio guide](/blog/coffee-to-water-ratio-guide/) explains how the ratios compare across brewers.

## Batch size and the basket

Every machine has a range of batch sizes it brews best. Rao explains that the basket's diameter sets how deep the bed of coffee is, and the depth decides the grind: a taller bed holds the water back more, so it needs a coarser grind, and a shallow one needs a finer grind. There's no universal ideal depth, he writes, though the Coffee Brewing Center recommended 1 to 2 inches.

So a small batch in a big basket makes a thin bed that drains fast. The Brew Guide warns about this below about 15 g of coffee and suggests grinding a little finer. Some machines adjust for it: Moccamaster's KBGV Select has a switch for a half or a full carafe and changes its cycle to suit. For very small batches, Rao suggests a smaller, tapered basket if your machine has one.

Large café brewers can also have a bypass valve, which sends part of the water around the coffee and into the pot, so a very tall bed can be brewed strong and then diluted. Rao explains how to set one by taste and with a TDS meter.

## Grind medium

The Brew Guide and Grind Guide put a drip machine at a medium grind, like sand, about 600 to 900 µm. That band is illustrative; on a real grinder, start from its maker's chart. Baratza, for example, suggests setting 18 on the Virtuoso+ for an automatic brewer, as published by the maker.

A drip machine sets its own run time, so the clues are in the basket and the cup. The Grind Guide's signs: too fine and the basket can flood or overflow, and the coffee can taste bitter; too coarse and the water runs straight through, and the coffee can taste weak or sour. Rao adds a check for after the brew: with beans roasted three to seven days earlier and ground just before brewing, most of the surface of the wet grounds should be covered in froth. A wet-sand surface with little froth suggests too coarse; a pitted or muddy surface, or dry patches, suggests too fine, though dry patches can also mean the spray head is blocked or too close to the coffee.

## Stir twice

Rao calls stirring the key to the best drip coffee. He describes two problems with how a drip machine brews:

- **The spray head favors some areas.** The coffee under them over-extracts, which promotes channeling.
- **The top extracts more than the bottom.** Fresh water lands on top, so the upper layers see hotter, more dilute water and extract more than the grounds below.

His fix, from *The Professional Barista's Handbook*:

1. About 5 to 10 seconds after the brew starts, gently stir the grounds with a spoon, wetting all of them without puncturing the filter. Three or four turns are enough. He says this first stir acts as a sort of preinfusion, wetting the whole bed almost at once and evening out its temperature.
2. When the spray head stops, stir again, the same way. This mixes the spent grounds from the top with the fuller ones from the bottom, which he says greatly improves how evenly the bed extracts.

He adds that stirring calls for a coarser grind, and that however much you stir, you should stir the same way every batch. You need to be able to reach the grounds, which not every machine allows. Some home brewers have a bloom or pre-infusion setting instead: on the SCA's list, for example, Aarke and KRUPS describe blooming functions and Bonavita lists optional pre-infusion. Our [bloom guide](/blog/what-is-coffee-bloom/) explains what that first wetting does.

## Water temperature

You usually can't set it. The temperatures to know:

- **Rao** brews drip coffee at 195 to 205°F&nbsp;(91 to 96°C), the Coffee Brewing Center's range, adjusting for the roast, the ratio and the flavor he wants. He notes that hotter water raises the perception of acidity, bitterness, body and astringency.
- **The SCA's certification**, as quoted by Batali, Ristenpart and Guinard from the 2017 program, required a brewer to reach 198°F&nbsp;(92°C) within the first minute, stay at or above it, and never exceed 205°F&nbsp;(96°C). An SCA feature from October 2021 says that drip brewers that can't reach 198°F&nbsp;(92°C) fast enough fail.
- **The same UC Davis team** found little difference in taste between brews at 189 and 199°F&nbsp;(87 and 93°C) when they adjusted the grind, ratio and flow so the strength and extraction stayed the same.
- **Our Brew Guide** shows 201°F&nbsp;(94°C) for a medium roast, marked as set by the machine.

So if your coffee tastes sour on a machine you can't adjust, change the grind first. Our [brewing temperature guide](/blog/whats-the-ideal-coffee-brewing-temperature/) goes further.

## Fix the taste one change at a time

A drip machine leaves you three dials: the grind, the ratio and the stirs. Change one per batch.

| What you taste or see | What it often means | One change to try |
|---|---|---|
| Sour, thin | Under-extracted, often with water running straight through | Grind one step finer |
| Bitter, astringent, drying | Over-extracted, often with a flooded basket | Grind one step coarser |
| Weak, watery, but not sour | Not enough coffee for the water | Use more coffee: try 1:15.5 |
| Bitter and weak at once | Rao suggests dull grinder burrs | Check or replace the burrs |
| Sour and bitter at once | Uneven extraction | Level the bed and stir; see [channeling](/blog/fix-channeling-uneven-extraction/) |
| Flat, stale | Coffee held too long, or old beans | Brew smaller batches; check the roast date |

Rao's own diagnosis for drip is by taste: bitter, astringent or drying means the grind is too fine, and weak or watery means it's too coarse. Treat these as common signs, not rules.

## Serve it within about 30 minutes

Brewed coffee doesn't hold well, and a hot plate makes it worse. The sources differ on how long is too long:

- **Rao**, writing for cafés, keeps brewed coffee in a sealed, thermal container at 175 to 185°F&nbsp;(79 to 85°C) to limit sour flavors, says flavor loss is noticeable within 15 to 20 minutes whatever the holding conditions, and calls serving coffee more than 30 minutes after brewing an insult to a paying customer. The Brew Guide's last step follows him: serve within 30 minutes.
- **Jessica Easto**, writing for home brewers in *Craft Coffee: A Manual*, finds that a good preheated thermos or insulated carafe can keep coffee surprisingly fresh for several hours. She notes that below about 175°F&nbsp;(79°C) the coffee turns increasingly sour or bitter, and that reheated coffee tastes sour, bitter, astringent and woody.

Both point the same way: brew what you'll drink soon, and if you're keeping some, pour it into a preheated, insulated carafe instead of leaving it on a hot plate. Rao's advice to cafés is to switch from glass pots and uninsulated urns to enclosed, thermal containers.

## Grinding ahead of time

Grinding right before brewing is the usual advice, because ground coffee loses its gas fast: Smrke and colleagues found that up to 75% of the gas left within about 90 seconds of grinding. Rao, though, reports a few informal blind tests in his 2008 handbook. Coffees one to six days off the roaster were ground either 12 hours ahead or just before tasting, both as drip and at the cupping table. The pre-ground drip samples used a slightly finer grind; the cupping samples used the same grind. He reports that every participant preferred the pre-ground samples. He concluded that pre-grinding isn't harmful to flavor and sometimes helps. In 2013 he wrote that the gas in very fresh beans stirs up a filter brew without much affecting its extraction or flavor.

Those were small, informal tastings of very fresh coffee, so treat grinding ahead as a convenience that may not cost you much, not as an improvement to chase. Our [freshness guide](/blog/coffee-freshness-roast-to-stale/) covers how coffee stales.

## Filters, water and cleaning

- **Rinse the paper filter.** Rao flushes the basket and filter with hot water before every batch, because paper can absorb odors in storage. It also rinses and preheats the basket and pot.
- **Paper or metal.** Paper gives the least body and the most clarity, Rao writes; metal filters give a lot of body and less clarity, and need thorough cleaning after each use. Paper also holds back most of the coffee oils, including cafestol and kahweol, the compounds behind unfiltered coffee's effect on LDL cholesterol (Urgert and Katan, 1997).
- **Water.** The SCA's 2018 standard for brewing water targets about 150 mg/L of total dissolved solids, 68 mg/L of calcium hardness and 40 mg/L of alkalinity. Our [water guide](/blog/why-water-matters-more-than-your-coffee-beans/) explains what those mean, and the [Water Recipe Builder](/deep-brew/water/) mixes water to a recipe.
- **Clean the carafe.** Easto scrubs insulated carafes after every use, because leftover coffee oils go rancid and spoil the next pot. For descaling, follow your machine's manual: makers set their own intervals and descalers.

## Choosing a brewer

The SCA certifies home coffee makers through third-party testing, and its list changes as certifications are granted and expire; a certification runs for a three-year licensing period. When we checked the list on October 18, 2026, it included eight Technivorm Moccamaster models, Bonavita's 8-cup and 5-cup brewers, OXO's Brew 8-Cup and 12-Cup, Breville's Luxe Brewer, Fellow's Aiden, and machines from Aarke, Braun, Café, Cosori, GE Profile, Kismile, KRUPS, Lotus and Zwilling. The Breville Precision Brewer, OXO 9-Cup and Bonavita BV1900TS that this page used to recommend aren't on the current list. The SCA doesn't publish its test criteria on that page; check the [SCA's certified home equipment list](https://sca.coffee/certified-home-brewer) for the current models.
