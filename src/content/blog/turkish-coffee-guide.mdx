Turkish coffee is brewed by heating very finely ground coffee in the water itself, in a small long-handled pot called a cezve, and pouring it out unfiltered. The grounds stay in the cup and settle to the bottom.

In 2013 UNESCO inscribed "Turkish coffee culture and tradition" on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. And in March 2026 Türkiye's patent office registered *Türk Kahvesi / Turkish Coffee* as a traditional product name, with a written description of how the coffee is ground and brewed. The recipe below follows that description.

## What you need

- **A cezve.** The World Coffee Championships, which run a Cezve/Ibrik competition, describe the pot as metal, brass, copper or ceramic, with a distinctive long handle and a brim for serving. It's also called an *ibrik*. One cezve can brew several cups at once.
- **Turkish coffee, ground extra fine.** Arabica, robusta or a blend, at a light, medium or dark roast, all count under the registered description. If your grinder's maker doesn't list a Turkish setting, buy coffee sold ground for Turkish coffee, such as <a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Kurukahveci+Mehmet+Efendi+Turkish+coffee&tag=jayarrcoffee-20" rel="sponsored noopener">Kurukahveci Mehmet Efendi Turkish coffee</a>.
- **Cold drinking water.** The registration asks for water that hasn't been heated beforehand, at room temperature or colder, with a pH of 6.8 to 7 and a hardness of 7.2 to 14.2 French degrees (about 72–142 mg/L as calcium carbonate, calculated).
- **Small cups.** Traditional Turkish coffee cups hold 60–90 ml, about 2 to 3 US fl oz (calculated).
- **Sugar,** if you like it. It goes in before brewing, not after.

## The grind

Turkish coffee is ground to a powder, the finest band in our Grind Guide, which shows it as extra fine, like flour, at about 100–200&nbsp;µm. Türkiye's registered description asks for a finer average than that, 75–125&nbsp;µm. Both are finer than the Grind Guide's espresso band, 200–300&nbsp;µm. A burr grinder whose maker lists a Turkish setting, or coffee bought ready-ground for Turkish coffee, gets you there; our Grind Calibrator doesn't cover Turkish settings yet.

## The recipe

- **Coffee:** 6&nbsp;g for one cup, about a teaspoon. Kurukahveci Mehmet Efendi's own guide uses one teaspoon, about 6&nbsp;g, per cup.
- **Water:** 60&nbsp;ml (60&nbsp;g), cold. The registered ratio is 1&nbsp;g of coffee to 10&nbsp;ml of water, so a bigger cup takes more of both: 8&nbsp;g to 80&nbsp;ml, for example. You can brew several cups together in one cezve.
- **Sugar:** decide now. The registration names four levels per cup: *şekersiz* (none), *az şekerli* (a little: one cube, or 2–3&nbsp;g), *orta şekerli* (medium: 1½ cubes, or 3–4.5&nbsp;g) and *şekerli* (sweet: two cubes, or 4–6&nbsp;g).
- **Time:** about a minute to measure and stir, then 2½ to 3 minutes on the heat.
- **Makes:** one small cup, made with 60&nbsp;ml (about 2 US fl oz) of water.

The glass of water is part of the tradition: UNESCO, the registration and Mehmet Efendi all mention it. The registration says it lets you clear your palate first, so you taste the coffee better. Let the cup sit a moment so the grounds settle, and leave them in the cup when you've finished.

Not every recipe agrees on the heating. Mehmet Efendi's guide stirs while the coffee heats, pours a little of the foam into each cup, then brings the coffee to the boil again before filling the cups. The registered description says the coffee shouldn't boil at all, because boiling makes off flavors. Try the registered method first, and Mehmet Efendi's if you want a second heating.

## When it goes wrong

| What happened | Likely reason | Next time |
|---|---|---|
| Burnt, bitter taste; the foam collapses quickly | It was on the heat too long | Use a little more heat, so it reaches the foam in 2½ to 3 minutes |
| Raw taste, not much foam | It heated too fast | Turn the heat down |
| Harsh or off flavors | It boiled | Take it off as soon as the foam starts to form |
| Thin, with gritty grounds | The grind is too coarse | Use coffee ground for Turkish coffee |

The first three rows are the registration's own warnings; the last is the Grind Guide's sign of a too-coarse grind.

## Turkish coffee and cholesterol

Unfiltered coffee carries cafestol and kahweol, oils from the bean that raise LDL cholesterol. In their review, Rob Urgert and Martijn Katan explain that hot water extracts them and a paper filter holds them back, which is why filtered coffee doesn't affect cholesterol while Scandinavian boiled coffee, cafetière (French press) coffee and Turkish coffee do. A 2022 analysis of 16 Turkish coffees from supermarkets in Northern Cyprus found about 1.4 to 6.9&nbsp;mg of cafestol per cup of brewed coffee; the abstract doesn't give the cup size.

If you drink several cups a day and are watching your cholesterol, ask your clinician whether it matters for you. Our [French press guide](/blog/how-to-use-a-french-press-the-most-amazing-coffee-youve-ever-had/) covers the same question for French press coffee.

## The culture around the cup

UNESCO describes Turkish coffee as a symbol of hospitality, friendship, refinement and entertainment. It is served in small cups with a glass of water, mostly drunk in coffeehouses where people meet to talk, share news and read, and it has a place at occasions such as betrothals and holidays. The know-how passes down informally in families. After the coffee, the grounds left in the cup are read to tell the drinker's fortune.

The registration adds the history: coffee became known in Anatolia in the 1500s, and the first coffeehouse opened in Istanbul's Tahtakale district. Cezve coffee isn't only Turkish, either. The World Coffee Championships note that it is drunk widely in parts of Eastern Europe, the Middle East and North Africa.
