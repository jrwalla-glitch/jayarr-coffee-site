Myanmar is a newcomer to specialty coffee. Ten-odd years ago its coffee was sold informally across the border for whatever a trader offered; now its best lots go to roasters in the US, Europe and Asia. The sector is small and has been through a military coup. Here's how it started, what the coffee tastes like, and what to look for.

## What Myanmar coffee tastes like

Much of Myanmar's specialty coffee is natural-process coffee from Shan State smallholders. When an importer sent green coffee from the Ywangan village of Myin Dwin to 36 US roasters for a US Coffee Championships roasting qualifier in Nashville in January 2019, Winrock International reported their reactions: one roaster said it "blew my mind because it's really complex," and another said that opening the bag of green coffee was "kind of like you were standing at a tropical fruit stand." Coffee Origins quotes Nilaa Coffee's notes of watermelon, chocolate and tea for a natural lot from Ywangan.

Washed coffees from the Mandalay estates are a different style, and the importers' notes for the Shan naturals shouldn't be read across to them. These are what a Myanmar coffee *can* taste like, not a promise for any bag.

## How coffee came to Myanmar

Accounts of the start differ. Sucafina, and a 2022 market brief hosted by the Myanmar Information Management Unit, say missionaries introduced coffee in 1885 at Myeik and Dawei, on the southern coast. Sucafina adds that robusta growing began in Kayin State around the same time and continues there, and that Catholic missionaries brought arabica to northeastern Shan State in the 1930s. Daily Coffee News, in a 2021 report by Susan Heller Evenson of Atlas Coffee Importers, says British colonists and missionaries started small farms around Pyin Oo Lwin, in the Mandalay region, in the 1880s.

Sucafina writes that coffee was then neither a major focus nor a major export. Evenson writes that after colonial rule ended in 1948, it was sold locally at low prices or across the borders to China, Laos or Thailand through unofficial channels, and that by 2014 specialty coffee didn't exist: the country's only dry mill was a government mill in Pyin Oo Lwin.

## How the specialty sector started

The change came quickly:

- **2013.** Rick Peyser, a volunteer on Winrock's USAID-funded Farmer-to-Farmer program, assessed the coffee value chain in Shan State. Winrock's accounts of his trip say farmers were selling to Chinese traders who paid one price regardless of quality, and that his advice was to get organized, focus on quality and diversify.
- **2014.** Winrock's USAID project, Value Chains for Rural Development, chose specialty coffee as a crop to support; Evenson dates the five-year project to late 2014, with support from the Coffee Quality Institute. The Myanmar Coffee Association was founded in late 2014.
- **2015.** Winrock and the association held the first national coffee cupping competition, which became a yearly event.
- **2016.** Atlas Coffee Importers bought the entire first crop of specialty coffee, about 30 tonnes, Winrock reports.
- **2019.** Winrock puts exports at nearly 240 tons, worth 1.5 million US dollars, up from a few containers in 2013, and says prices paid to farmers rose up to three times.

Volunteers also shaped how the coffee is made. Winrock writes that because water is scarce in the highlands, they suggested farmers focus on sun-dried natural processing rather than washing, and taught drying on raised tables instead of on the ground.

A separate program ran in the opium-growing areas of southern Shan. The United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime wrote in December 2018 that southern Shan State accounts for 90 percent of Myanmar's opium poppy cultivation, that the first coffee was planted there under its alternative-development program in 2014, and that it worked with farmers in 55 villages. Their Green Gold cooperative, set up in 2015, had 968 members by then, signed a five-year agreement with the French roaster Malongo in 2017, and exported its first container to Europe in 2018.

## Where and how it's grown

Shan State and the Mandalay region grow most of the coffee, with specialty lots also coming from Chin and Kachin states. Evenson describes the two main styles: in Mandalay, most farmers own larger estates and produce mainly washed coffee, plus some naturals and honeys; in Shan State, producers are almost all smallholders on less than a hectare, making natural-process coffee.

Winrock's 2017 profiles of 21 producer groups in Ywangan Township, in southern Shan, give a picture of the specialty heartland:

- **Altitude:** 1,005 to 1,601 m.
- **Variety:** Catuai in every group, listed at 80 to 100 percent, with some Catimor in three groups and a little Caturra in one.
- **Process:** each group's target was natural-process green coffee, between 0.4 and 2.9 tonnes, about 32 tonnes in all (calculated).
- **Farmers:** Danu and Pa-O communities.

Sucafina lists Catuai, Caturra, Catimor, Bourbon, Typica, S795 and SL34 as Myanmar's common arabica varieties. S795 is an Indian selection with some Liberica in its ancestry; our [Indian coffee guide](/blog/what-is-coffee-from-india-like/) covers it. Sucafina and Evenson both put the harvest from about late December to March.

## How much Myanmar grows

USDA doesn't include Myanmar in its coffee tables, and the figures we found differ:

- **Evenson (2021)** estimated total production in 2019 at 3,500 to 4,000 tonnes, of which 400 to 500 tonnes were exported, more than the nearly 240 tons Winrock gives for that year's exports.
- **The 2022 market brief** cites Myanmar's 2021 agricultural statistics book: about 50,000 acres (20,243 hectares) of coffee in 2020, 75 to 80 percent of it arabica, and 8,769 tonnes harvested in 2019–20, about 146,000 60&nbsp;kg bags (calculated).

Either way, it's a tiny crop by world standards.

## The 2021 coup and the coffee trade

The military seized power on February 1, 2021, in the middle of the harvest. Writing two months later, Evenson reported what producers were facing: bank closures during the civil disobedience movement made cash hard to get, so some farmers sold their cherry on the local market for quick money; roadblocks and internet blackouts went up; and DHL in Yangon, the main route for sending samples, was closed. The US suspended engagement under its 2013 trade and investment framework agreement with Myanmar, but trade, including imports from Myanmar, was still allowed.

The 2022 market brief, written for Chin State's coffee sector, lists currency depreciation, steep rises in the cost of farm inputs, logistics and transport, high inflation and lower productivity since the coup. It also reports that Myanmar's domestic coffee market peaked in the 2022 season, driven by strong demand from Thailand that paid high prices even for ordinary cherry and green coffee. We didn't find an official export figure for the years since the coup.

## Buying and brewing it

Myanmar coffee reaches buyers mainly through specialty importers and roasters. On a bag, look for:

- **The region:** Ywangan or another southern Shan township for the naturals; Pyin Oo Lwin for Mandalay's washed estate coffee.
- **A producer group or village,** such as Myin Dwin, or a named processor.
- **The process:** natural for the fruit-forward Shan style, washed for a cleaner cup.

Brew it as you would any good coffee. A pour-over shows a natural's fruit clearly; our [processing guide](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/) explains what natural processing does.
