Bourbon, Typica, Gesha, SL28, Caturra, Pacamara: specialty bags print the variety next to the origin and the process. This guide sorts those names into families, says where each came from, and gives World Coffee Research's ratings for each one.

## Variety, varietal or cultivar?

James Hoffmann, in *The World Atlas of Coffee*, draws the line this way. A **variety** is a genetically distinct type within one species, here *Coffea arabica*, that can differ in the tree, the leaves or the fruit. **Cultivar** means the same thing; it's short for "cultivated variety." **Varietal**, strictly, describes a particular instance of a variety, as in a farm's crop being "100 percent Bourbon varietal." In practice most people use "varietal" and "variety" interchangeably, and so does this page's title.

## Where arabica's varieties come from

Arabica is itself a cross. A 2024 genome study in *Nature Genetics* (Salojärvi and colleagues) describes it as a hybrid of two other species, *C. eugenioides* and *C. canephora* (robusta), formed roughly 350,000 to 610,000 years ago and squeezed by several bottlenecks before people ever farmed it. That's why arabica has so little genetic variation.

Farming narrowed it further. World Coffee Research (WCR), the industry's research nonprofit, writes that the seeds first taken from Ethiopia to Yemen were related to Bourbon and Typica, and that their descendants became the basis of most arabica grown worldwide. Its catalog notes that in Brazil, which grows about 40 percent of the world's coffee, 97.55 percent of the cultivars derive from Typica and Bourbon.

WCR sorts arabica varieties into these groups, and this guide follows them:

- **Ethiopian landraces:** traditional varieties from the forests where arabica evolved, generally linked with very high cup quality and lower yields.
- **The Bourbon–Typica group:** descendants of the seeds taken out of Yemen, split into Typica-related, Bourbon-related, and crosses of both.
- **Introgressed varieties:** those carrying genes from another species, mostly robusta via the Timor Hybrid. Catimors and Sarchimors belong here.
- **F1 hybrids:** first-generation crosses of two genetically distinct arabica parents.

### How to read WCR's ratings

The tables below use WCR's *Coffee Varieties Catalog*, checked October 15, 2026. Three things to keep in mind:

- **Cup quality is a potential.** WCR rates "quality potential at high altitude" on a scale of Very Low, Low, Good, Very Good and Exceptional. It's what the variety can reach when grown high and well, not what every lot tastes like.
- **The rating table wins.** WCR's one-line summaries sometimes word things differently from its rating table. SL28's summary says "very good cup quality potential," for instance, while its table rates it Exceptional. This guide uses the table and notes where the two differ.
- **Rust resistance can change.** WCR's own caution: "A variety that is resistant to a disease today may not be resistant tomorrow." Leaf rust is the fungus *Hemileia vastatrix*; our [coffee leaf rust history](/blog/coffee-leaf-rust-hemileia-vastatrix-history/) explains it.

## The Typica line

WCR's account is that the Typica branch split off when the Dutch sent seeds from India's Malabar coast to Java in 1696 and 1699. In 1706 a single plant went from Java to the botanical garden in Amsterdam, and in 1714 the mayor of Amsterdam gave one to Louis XIV. From the Netherlands and Paris, Typica spread along colonial trade routes: to Suriname in 1719, French Guiana in 1722, northern Brazil in 1727, Martinique in 1723 and Jamaica in 1730. Until the 1940s most plantations in Central America grew it.

| Variety | What it is | Yield | Cup quality (WCR) | Leaf rust (WCR) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Typica | The original line; tall | Low | Very Good | Susceptible |
| Maragogipe | Giant-bean Typica mutation, Brazil | Low | Very Good | Susceptible |
| Pache | Dwarf Typica mutation, Guatemala | Medium | Good | Susceptible |
| SL34 | Kenyan selection, Typica-related | Medium | Exceptional | Susceptible |

- **Typica.** Hoffmann calls it the variety from which the others mutated or were selected, capable of excellent cup quality with a relatively small yield. It goes by other names, including criollo, arabigo and Blue Mountain. Because it yields little and is highly susceptible to disease, WCR writes, it has been replaced across much of the Americas, but it's still widely planted in Peru, the Dominican Republic and Jamaica.
- **Maragogipe** (often spelled Maragogype). WCR dates its discovery to 1870, near the Brazilian city of Maragogipe. A single dominant gene makes its beans, leaves and the gaps between its leaves unusually large; Hoffmann notes it's often called "Elephant Bean." Its main legacy is as a parent of Pacamara.
- **Pache.** A dwarf mutation of Typica found in 1949 on a farm in Santa Rosa, Guatemala. Being small, it can be planted more densely for more fruit per hectare.
- **SL34.** Selected at Kenya's Scott Agricultural Laboratories in the late 1930s from a single tree on the Loresho Estate, labeled "French Mission." French Mission coffee is usually taken to be Bourbon, but WCR writes that recent genetic tests place SL34 in the Typica group, so the original story may be wrong. Hoffmann, following the older account, calls it a French Mission Bourbon selection that's generally considered inferior to SL28; WCR's table rates both Exceptional. WCR also flags SL34 as highly susceptible to coffee berry disease, a fungal disease of the fruit that WCR notes isn't present in Central America.

## The Bourbon line

French colonists tried to bring coffee from Yemen to the island of Bourbon, now Réunion, three times, in 1708, 1715 and 1718, WCR writes, and only a few plants from the second and third attempts survived. Bourbon didn't leave the island until the mid-1800s. Then two routes carried it out:

- **To East Africa,** with French Spiritan missionaries: a mission in Zanzibar in 1859, Bagamoyo in Tanzania in 1862 and Bura in Kenya in 1893. Seedlings from Bura went to the St. Austin mission near Nairobi in 1899, and this is the "French Mission" coffee behind several Kenyan selections.
- **To the Americas,** arriving near Campinas in southern Brazil in 1860 and spreading north into Central America.

Hoffmann describes Bourbon as a natural mutation of Typica with a higher yield and, many believe, a distinctive sweetness, and notes that its fruit can ripen red, yellow or occasionally orange. WCR's genetic account treats the two as separate branches, both taken out of Yemen.

| Variety | What it is | Yield | Cup quality (WCR) | Leaf rust (WCR) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bourbon | The original line; tall | Medium | Very Good | Susceptible |
| Caturra | Dwarf mutation, Brazil | Medium | Good | Susceptible |
| Villa Sarchi | Dwarf mutation, Costa Rica | Medium | Good | Susceptible |
| Pacas | Dwarf mutation, El Salvador | Medium | Good | Susceptible |
| Tekisic | Salvadoran selection; tall | Medium | Very Good | Susceptible |
| SL28 | Kenyan selection; tall | Low | Exceptional | Susceptible |
| K7 | Kenyan selection; tall | Medium | Good | Susceptible |

- **Bourbon.** WCR calls it one of the most culturally and genetically important arabica varieties in the world. In Latin America it has largely been replaced by its own descendants, such as Caturra, Catuaí and Mundo Novo, but it's still grown in El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras and Peru.
- **Caturra.** A single-gene dwarf mutation found on a farm in Minas Gerais, Brazil, between 1915 and 1918; its name comes from a Guarani word for "small." Brazil's Instituto Agronômico (IAC) began selecting it in 1937 (Hoffmann gives 1937 as the year of discovery). It became common in Central America and was long the benchmark that new cultivars were tested against. In Colombia it was thought to be nearly half of production until a government-backed program from 2008 replanted over three billion trees with Castillo.
- **Villa Sarchi.** Another dwarf Bourbon mutation, found in Alajuela, Costa Rica, in the 1950s or 1960s. WCR says it suits the highest altitudes and tolerates strong winds. Its bigger role is as a parent of the Sarchimor group (below).
- **Pacas.** Found in 1949 on a Pacas family farm in Santa Ana, El Salvador. WCR says it's about 25 percent of El Salvador's production; it's a parent of Pacamara.
- **Tekisic.** A Bourbon selection made by El Salvador's coffee research institute, ISIC. Our [El Salvador guide](/blog/el-salvador-coffee-guide-pacamara/) covers it with Pacas and Pacamara.
- **SL28.** Selected at Scott Agricultural Laboratories in 1935 from a single tree in a drought-resistant population from Tanganyika (now Tanzania). Genetic tests place it in the Bourbon group. WCR notes its drought tolerance and its "rusticity": trees 60 to 80 years old are still productive in Kenya. It's susceptible to leaf rust and coffee berry disease. Hoffmann writes that it can give a distinct fruit flavor, often described as blackcurrant. Our [Kenyan coffee guide](/blog/kenyan-coffee-flavor-notes-and-best-coffees/) goes further.
- **K7.** Released in Kenya in 1936 after five generations of selection from French Mission coffee. WCR rates it tolerant of coffee berry disease, and it has been used widely in Kenyan and Tanzanian breeding.
- **Kent.** WCR doesn't rate Kent itself, but its entry for KP423, a Kent selection, describes Kent as a Bourbon-related population from southwestern India, traced to a single tree selected by L. P. Kent in 1911 on his estate in Mysore. It was the first coffee selected for rust resistance, and that resistance has since broken down. Hoffmann dates the selection program to the 1920s and agrees that new strains of rust can destroy it.

## Typica and Bourbon crossed

| Variety | What it is | Yield | Cup quality (WCR) | Leaf rust (WCR) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mundo Novo | Natural Typica × Bourbon cross, Brazil; tall | Medium | Good | Susceptible |
| Catuaí | Mundo Novo × Caturra, bred in Brazil; dwarf | Medium | Good | Susceptible |
| Pacamara | Pacas × Maragogipe, bred in El Salvador | Low | Exceptional | Susceptible |

- **Mundo Novo.** A natural cross found in São Paulo state and named for the town where its seeds were planted. WCR dates the discovery to 1943 and its distribution to Brazilian farmers to 1952. It's grown widely in South America but rarely in Central America: it reached Costa Rica in 1952, but farmers there disliked how tall it grows.
- **Catuaí.** IAC crossed yellow Caturra with Mundo Novo in 1949 and released the result in Brazil in 1972. Its small size lets it be planted at nearly double the density of Bourbon. WCR calls its cup "good but not great" and its leaf-rust susceptibility high; it's about 20 percent of Guatemala's production. WCR's summary line calls it high-yielding, while its rating table puts the yield at Medium.
- **Pacamara.** Created by ISIC in El Salvador in 1958. WCR rates it capable of exceptional cup quality, with very large beans, a low yield and very high susceptibility to leaf rust, and notes that it isn't uniform: plants aren't stable from one generation to the next. Hoffmann writes that it can taste of chocolate and fruit, but can also give unpleasantly herbal, onion-like cups.

## Ethiopian landraces

A landrace is a traditional variety that has adapted to its place over generations of farming. In coffee, WCR writes, most landraces come from the forests of Ethiopia, where arabica evolved, and they're generally linked with very high cup quality and lower yields. Hoffmann adds that many of Ethiopia's trees are indigenous heirloom varieties that have barely been catalogued, which is why so many Ethiopian bags just say "heirloom." Our guide to [Ethiopian heirloom and named varieties](/blog/ethiopian-heirloom-vs-named-varietals/) explains the JARC selections.

| Variety | What it is | Yield | Cup quality (WCR) | Leaf rust (WCR) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Geisha (Panama) | Ethiopian landrace via CATIE; tall | Low | Exceptional | Intermediate resistance |
| Java | Ethiopian landrace via Java and Cameroon; tall | Medium | Very Good | Susceptible |

**Gesha, or Geisha.** WCR's history runs like this:

- It was collected from Ethiopia's coffee forests in the 1930s, then sent to the Lyamungu research station in Tanzania.
- In 1953 it reached the CATIE research center in Costa Rica, logged as accession T2722.
- CATIE distributed it in Panama in the 1960s for its tolerance of leaf rust, but its brittle branches made it unpopular with farmers.
- It came to prominence when the Peterson family of Boquete entered it in the Best of Panama competition and auction. WCR gives the year as 2005; Hoffmann and the Petersons' Hacienda La Esmeralda give 2004.

WCR's genetic analyses show that Panamanian Geisha descended from T2722 is distinct and uniform. Other coffees sold as "Geisha" may not share those genetics. WCR describes its aromas as delicate, floral, jasmine-like and peach-like, and notes that it's also grown widely in Malawi. The two spellings come from transliteration: the germplasm records used "Geisha," while the mountain near where it was collected is usually rendered "Gesha." Our [Gesha guide](/blog/gesha-coffee-worth-the-price/) and [Panama guide](/blog/panama-coffee-guide-geisha-boquete/) tell the auction story.

**Java.** Despite the name, Java isn't a Typica. WCR writes that the Dutch brought it to Java directly from Ethiopia in the early 19th century, and that genetic fingerprinting shows it's a selection from an Ethiopian landrace population called Abysinia. From Java it went to Cameroon in the mid-20th century, where it was found partially tolerant to coffee berry disease and released in 1980–90. CIRAD took it to Costa Rica in 1991, and Panama was the first Central American country to recognize it officially, in 2016. WCR's summary calls it "tolerant to major diseases," but its rating table lists it as susceptible to leaf rust and tolerant of coffee berry disease.

**Pink Bourbon.** It isn't in WCR's catalog, and despite its name it doesn't seem to be a Bourbon. The importer Café Imports had samples DNA-tested twice, in 2017 and in 2023, the second time on five lots from Colombia and Costa Rica. It concluded that Pink Bourbon is an Ethiopian landrace, not a Bourbon or a new hybrid, though some samples were mixed with Bourbon or Catimor.

Rume Sudan, which WCR describes as an Ethiopian landrace, appears below as a parent of Centroamericano, Batian and Ruiru 11.

## Rust-resistant varieties: the Timor Hybrid's family

In the 1920s, WCR writes, an arabica and a robusta plant crossed naturally on the island of Timor. The offspring, the Timor Hybrid, carried robusta genes that resist leaf rust. Portugal's coffee rust research center, CIFC, received Timor Hybrid seed in 1958 or 1959, and from 1967 its breeders crossed it with compact varieties:

- **Catimors:** Caturra crossed with the Timor Hybrid.
- **Sarchimors:** Villa Sarchi crossed with the Timor Hybrid.

Neither name is a single variety; each covers a group of distinct varieties with similar parentage. WCR writes that introgressed varieties have traditionally been linked with lower cup quality, but have been essential for farmers facing leaf rust and coffee berry disease.

The resistance is wearing thin. Early in the 21st century, WCR writes, Lempira in Honduras and Costa Rica 95 in Costa Rica, both bred for rust resistance, began to be infected. Because most resistant varieties got their resistance from the same parent, most experts believe most existing rust-resistant varieties will lose it in the near to medium term.

| Variety | What it is | Yield | Cup quality (WCR) | Leaf rust (WCR) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Costa Rica 95 | Catimor (Timor Hybrid 832/1 × Caturra) | High | Low | Susceptible (confirmed in Costa Rica) |
| Lempira | Catimor, Honduras | High | Low | Intermediate resistance |
| IHCAFE 90 | Catimor, Honduras | High | Very Low | Susceptible |
| Catimor 129 | Catimor line from Colombia, grown in Malawi | High | Good | Intermediate resistance |
| Marsellesa | Sarchimor, released in Nicaragua in 2009 | High | Good | Intermediate resistance |
| Parainema | Sarchimor, released in Honduras in 2004 | Very High | Good | Highly resistant |
| IAPAR 59 | Sarchimor, Brazil | Medium | Low | Intermediate resistance |
| Obatã (red) | Sarchimor, Brazil | High | Good | Intermediate resistance |
| Batian | Kenyan composite with Timor Hybrid parentage; tall | High | Very Good | Intermediate resistance |

A few notes on the table:

- **Costa Rica 95.** WCR says it has been confirmed susceptible to rust in Costa Rica and may be in other parts of Central America.
- **Parainema and IAPAR 59.** Both also resist some nematodes, microscopic worms that attack the roots.
- **Marsellesa.** WCR notes a notably high acidity in the cup.
- **Batian.** Released in Kenya in 2010, it mixes three pure lines with parentage from SL28, SL34, Rume Sudan, N39, K7, SL4 and the Timor Hybrid. WCR rates it resistant to coffee berry disease and calls it well suited to smallholders, a rare combination of a tall variety with disease resistance.

### Castillo, Colombia's workhorse

Castillo isn't in WCR's catalog, so its lineage comes from Cenicafé, the research arm of Colombia's coffee growers' federation (FNC):

- **Parentage.** Cenicafé developed it from crosses of Caturra and the Timor Hybrid line CIFC 1343, selecting the offspring over five generations.
- **Release.** The FNC released it by Resolution 03 of May 13, 2005, together with seven regional versions chosen for different growing zones.
- **A blend of lines.** It's a composite variety: a mix of selected lines that differ in their rust-resistance genes, a strategy meant to make the resistance last.
- **Its name** honors the Cenicafé researcher Jaime Castillo Zapata (1928–2001).

Castillo is a Caturra × Timor Hybrid cross, so by WCR's broad definition it has Catimor-type parentage. It isn't a Sarchimor. Cenicafé's own identification guide files it, with the older Colombia variety and Cenicafé 1, as a separate "Colombia" group, named for the Timor Hybrid line 1343 used to breed them.

Specialty buyers have argued about Castillo since it spread. Hoffmann writes that many believe the FNC's promotion of varieties like Castillo has favored yield over cup quality, but that with climate change it's hard to argue against varieties that keep farmers in business. Cenicafé reports that its own cup tests haven't shown the Colombia and Castillo lines differing from Caturra. Our [Colombian coffee guide](/blog/single-origin-colombian-coffee-what-flavor-notes-to-expect/) covers its newer successors, Cenicafé 1 and Castillo 2.0.

## F1 hybrids

An F1 hybrid is the first generation of a cross between two genetically distinct arabica parents. WCR writes that these varieties tend to yield significantly more than non-hybrids, and that many were made to combine cup quality, yield and disease resistance.

There's a catch. Seed saved from an F1 plant won't grow true: the offspring "segregate," losing some of the parent's yield, resistance or quality. WCR's advice is that F1 hybrids be reproduced by cloning and bought as seedlings from trusted nurseries. Our article on [F1 hybrids](/blog/coffee-f1-hybrids-future-of-coffee/) goes further.

| Variety | What it is | Yield | Cup quality (WCR) | Leaf rust (WCR) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Centroamericano (H1) | T5296 (Sarchimor) × Rume Sudan; dwarf | Very High | Very Good | Highly resistant |
| Starmaya | Marsellesa × a male-sterile Ethiopian or Sudanese landrace | High | Very Good | Highly resistant |
| Ruiru 11 | Kenyan composite: Catimor × a complex male parent; dwarf | Very High | Good | Highly resistant |

- **Centroamericano.** Released in 2010 for Central American farmers by a group that included the French research institute CIRAD, the regional network of coffee institutes PROMECAFE and CATIE. In breeding trials it yielded 22 to 47 percent more than the standard Central American varieties. WCR's rating table puts its cup quality at Very Good, though its history text says it has "proven to have exceptional cup quality potential when well-managed at high altitudes."
- **Starmaya.** WCR calls it the only F1 hybrid that professional seed producers can propagate by seed rather than by cloning. One parent is male-sterile, so any fruit on those plants must be the hybrid. That makes hybrid seed cheaper to produce, but it doesn't make farm-saved seed grow true, and WCR notes that about 15 percent of planted Starmaya segregate.
- **Ruiru 11.** Kenya bred it after a 1968 coffee berry disease epidemic cost half the country's production, and released it in 1985. Its seed is made by hand-pollinating Catimor mother plants with pollen from a complex male parent bred from Rume Sudan, Timor Hybrid lines, K7, N39, SL28, SL34 and Bourbon. That hand work has made seed hard to supply. WCR rates it resistant to coffee berry disease.

## What a variety on the bag tells you

Less than the marketing suggests, and more than nothing.

Hoffmann is careful here. Coffee has mostly been traded by origin, not variety, and he writes that we still know relatively little about how much a tree's variety changes the cup. So he gives tasting notes only where something is distinct: Geisha's floral, citrusy cups, SL28's blackcurrant, Pacamara's chocolate and fruit (or onion). WCR's ratings, too, describe a potential at high altitude, not a guarantee.

Read the variety alongside the rest of the label:

- **Where and how high it grew.** WCR's own ratings assume high altitudes, and the best altitude for a variety depends on how close the farm is to the equator. Our [altitude guide](/blog/coffee-altitude-guide/) explains why.
- **How it was processed.** A natural and a washed lot of the same variety can taste very different. See our guide to [processing methods](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/).
- **The variety itself.** A named variety at least tells you someone kept track of the trees: Hoffmann points out that when lots from many farms are mixed, no one knows which varieties went in. Some names, like Geisha, SL28 or Pacamara, point to a distinct style. A Catimor or Castillo isn't a reason to skip a bag.
