Two people drink the same espresso after dinner. One sleeps; the other is awake at 1 am. Part of the difference is how fast each body clears caffeine, and part is how sensitive each brain is to it. This page is about the clearing. For how much caffeine is in each kind of cup, see our [caffeine in coffee guide](/blog/caffeine-in-coffee-guide/); for when to have your last one, see [coffee and sleep](/blog/coffee-and-sleep-when-to-stop/).

## How caffeine works

Much of what caffeine does to alertness comes from blocking adenosine receptors. A 2001 Institute of Medicine review calls that ability "highly important" to caffeine's effects on behavior and thinking. Caffeine and its main breakdown product, paraxanthine, compete with adenosine for its receptors, and by blocking adenosine's inhibitory effect, caffeine indirectly changes the release of messengers such as norepinephrine, dopamine, acetylcholine, serotonin and glutamate.

It gets into the blood quickly. The same review reports that about 99% is absorbed within 45 minutes and that blood levels peak between 15 and 120 minutes after drinking. The review suggests the spread may reflect how fast the stomach empties and what else is in it.

## Half-life, and the model our calculator uses

Once caffeine is absorbed, the body removes a fixed share of what's left each hour. The Institute of Medicine describes this as first-order elimination. So the amount halves over a set time, the half-life, whatever the starting dose.

Published averages differ with the people studied:

- **2.5 to 4.5 hours** in a study of adult men given 4 mg per kilogram, about 280 mg for a 70 kg man (as reported by the Institute of Medicine).
- **3.4 hours on average, range 2 to 5,** in 25 men and non-pregnant women (Knutti et al., 1981).
- **"Typically 3–6 hours,"** as the authors of a 2025 sleep trial put it (Gardiner et al.).

Our [Caffeine Calculator](/tools/caffeine/) needs a single number to draw a curve, so it uses 5 hours as typical. That's inside the 2025 trial's 3 to 6 hours but above the other two averages, so if anything its bedtime figures lean high. It also offers a Faster setting (3 hours) and a Slower one (8 hours). It lets each drink's caffeine rise to a peak about 45 minutes after you drink it, then fall by half every half-life. That's an illustration of the average pattern. It can't tell you your own half-life, when you'll be caffeine-free, or what dose is right for you.

## Why people clear caffeine at different speeds

**One enzyme does most of the work.** In a 2018 review, Astrid Nehlig writes that the CYP1A2 enzyme in the liver "metabolizes 95% of the caffeine ingested." How active that enzyme is varies about five- to sixfold between people, according to a 2022 systematic analysis of caffeine studies by Grzegorzewski and colleagues.

**Genes explain much of the difference, but no single variant tells you your speed.** The same analysis summarises twin research in which, with smokers and contraceptive users left out, 89% of the variation in caffeine exposure was down to genetic effects, but only 8% to one variant in the promoter region shared by the CYP1A1 and CYP1A2 genes. The CYP1A2 variant studied most often, rs762551, sorts people into groups: Cornelis and colleagues (2006), for example, call people with two copies of the \*1A allele "rapid" metabolizers and carriers of \*1F "slow." A group label isn't a half-life, though, and plenty of the variation comes from elsewhere.

**Sensitivity is a separate question.** The authors of the 2025 sleep trial note that variants in CYP1A2 can change how fast caffeine is cleared, while variants in the adenosine A2A receptor gene, ADORA2A, can change how sensitive someone is to it. Two people with the same caffeine in their blood can feel it differently.

## What speeds clearance up or slows it down

- **Smoking: faster.** Smoking increases caffeine clearance, and after quitting, clearance returns to non-smoking levels (Institute of Medicine, 2001; Grzegorzewski et al., 2022).
- **Oral contraceptives: slower.** The Institute of Medicine review reports that "oral contraceptive use can double caffeine half-life," citing studies from 1980 and 1985, and adds that newer pills hadn't been studied.
- **Pregnancy: slower.** Knutti and colleagues (1982) measured an average half-life of 3 hours in non-pregnant women and 10.5 hours in the last four weeks of pregnancy, about 3½ times as long (calculated). The WHO says that during pregnancy, caffeine clearance "slows down significantly."
- **Some medicines: slower.** Grzegorzewski and colleagues single out the antidepressant fluvoxamine and the antibacterials norfloxacin and pipemidic acid.
- **Liver disease: much slower.** In one report, two patients with alcohol-related liver disease had half-lives of 60 and 168 hours (Statland and Demas, 1980); the largest reductions are seen in cirrhosis (Grzegorzewski et al., 2022).

If you start or stop smoking, start a new medicine, become pregnant, or have a liver condition, ask your clinician or pharmacist how it affects caffeine for you. The calculator's Slower setting is a rough picture of a long half-life, not a substitute for that advice.

## What the agencies say

The FDA puts the variation plainly: "there is wide variation in both how sensitive people are to the effects of caffeine and how fast they eliminate it from the body." Its population figure is that "for most adults, the FDA has cited 400 milligrams a day — that's about two to three 12-fluid-ounce cups of coffee — as an amount not generally associated with negative effects."

For pregnancy, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists says that "moderate caffeine consumption (less than 200 mg per day) does not appear to be a major contributing factor in miscarriage or preterm birth." The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) says intakes up to 200 mg a day "do not raise safety concerns for the foetus."

These are population guides, not personal limits, and none of them sets a figure for a fast or a slow metabolizer. If you're pregnant, take medicines that interact with caffeine, or have a condition you're unsure about, ask your clinician. The full list of agency guidance, in their own words, is in the [caffeine in coffee guide](/blog/caffeine-in-coffee-guide/).

## Can you tell your own speed?

You can notice patterns: whether an afternoon coffee changes how you sleep, or how long the lift lasts. But people are poor judges of caffeine's effect on sleep. In Drake and colleagues' 2013 study, 400 mg six hours before bed cut sleep by more than an hour on a sleep monitor, while the participants' own sleep diaries didn't pick up a significant change. Treat your impressions as a clue, keep your coffee times steady while you watch, and see [coffee and sleep](/blog/coffee-and-sleep-when-to-stop/) for what the studies found.
