Most people don't think of China as a coffee-growing country, but Yunnan, in the southwest, has grown coffee commercially since the late 1980s, in the same hills that are famous for Pu'er tea. Big buyers such as Nestlé built the industry, and China's own cafés now buy many of its best lots. Here's how that happened, what the coffee tastes like, and what to look for on a bag.

## How much China grows

USDA's Foreign Agricultural Service puts China's crop at 1.9 million 60&nbsp;kg bags for 2026/27, all of it counted as arabica. That's about 1 percent of USDA's world total of 189.7 million bags (calculated). The crop has been steady at 1.8 to 1.9 million bags in each of USDA's estimates since 2021/22.

Yunnan's own figures are higher. Xinhua reported in January 2026 that, according to the Yunnan Provincial Department of Agriculture and Rural Affairs, the province grew 138,900 tonnes of green coffee in 2025, up 16.9 percent, on 1.463 million mu (about 97,500 hectares, calculated). That's about 2.3 million 60&nbsp;kg bags (calculated). The two series count different years and come from different agencies, so treat them as two estimates of a crop of roughly two million bags. The same Xinhua report gives the department's "specialty rate" for Yunnan coffee as 31.6 percent, without saying how it's measured; in 2022, China Daily had reported a provincial target of 30 percent by 2024.

China drinks far more than it grows. USDA forecasts domestic consumption at 6.75 million bags for 2026/27, about three and a half times the crop (calculated). Hoffmann noted in 2018 that China was beginning to take a taste for its own coffee, and that the best Chinese coffee he had tasted was only available in China. Sucafina, which runs a mill and lab outside Pu'er, writes that domestic buyers pay so well for high-scoring Yunnan lots that there's little incentive to export them.

## How coffee came to Yunnan

Hoffmann tells the usual story: a French missionary crossed into Yunnan from Vietnam in 1892 and planted coffee seeds near his church in the village of Zhukula. For almost a hundred years very little happened, which he calls unsurprising in a region famous for its tea. Sucafina writes that about 4,000 hectares were planted with coffee by the 1950s.

The modern industry dates from 1988, when, in Hoffmann's account, coffee production in Yunnan was revived as a joint venture with the United Nations Development Program and the World Bank, and its success probably came from the interest and support of Nestlé. Production stayed fairly small until about 2009, when it jumped; he puts that down to lower tea prices and a brief spike in world coffee prices.

## The big buyers: Nestlé and Starbucks

Both companies describe their own role in Yunnan:

- **Nestlé** says it has worked in Yunnan since 1988. Its Chinese site says it set up a coffee agronomy service in the province in 1992 to support local growers, that its aim was to secure good beans for Nescafé, and that it has become, in our translation, "a stable, trustworthy and fair buyer" of Yunnan coffee beans; its Pu'er purchasing station was certified to the 4C standard in January 2013. In March 2016 Nestlé opened its "Nescafé Coffee Centre" in Pu'er for research, a green coffee laboratory and farmer training, by which time it said it had trained almost 15,000 farmers.
- **Starbucks** opened its first farmer support center in Asia in Pu'er in December 2012, Daily Coffee News reported, with John Culver, then president of Starbucks China and Asia Pacific, saying that Yunnan would play "an important strategic role in our long-term supply of premium arabica coffee." In November 2025, announcing a joint venture for its Chinese stores, Starbucks said it would keep operating the Yunnan Farmer Support Center itself.

## Catimor, and what has started to replace it

Catimor is a cross of Caturra with the Timor Hybrid, so part of its ancestry is robusta. Sucafina writes that early experiments in Yunnan ran into leaf rust and pests, and nearly all the early arabica varieties were replaced with Catimor; around 30 varieties are grown in China now, but Catimor is by far the most popular, for its yields and robustness. China Daily reported in September 2022 that Catimor accounted for over 95 percent of the total planting yield in Yunnan. Hoffmann's regional listing for Yunnan is "Catimor, with some Caturra and Bourbon," and he wrote in 2018 that many lots still came from varieties chosen for disease resistance rather than taste. Our [varietal guide](/blog/coffee-varietal-guide/) explains where Catimor sits among the introgressed varieties.

Catimor doesn't rule out a good cup. In the 2025 Gems of Yunnan competition, a honey-processed Catimor from the Lanni Tang group in Maliping village placed third at 89.08. But the top of the table has moved to other varieties. Of the 19 coffees the Alliance for Coffee Excellence lists as scoring 87 or more that year, 10 were Geisha and 6 were the Yunka selections (counted from the results). In 2026, 17 of the 20 award winners were Geisha or a named Geisha selection.

## Where it grows

Coffee is concentrated in Yunnan's southwest, along the borders with Myanmar, Laos and Vietnam. Sucafina names Pu'er (including Menglian), Baoshan, Dehong, Lincang, Xishuangbanna, Dali and Wenshan, and writes that most coffee grows between 1,000 and 1,500 m, within a range of 800 to 1,800 m. Hoffmann gives Yunnan 900 to 1,700 m with an October–January harvest; Sucafina gives December to March, with coffee traded, milled and shipped from December to May. Hoffmann also lists robusta in Fujian and Hainan, though USDA counts all of China's crop as arabica.

Processing is mostly washed. Sucafina writes that over 90 percent of Yunnan's coffee is fully washed, sold in parchment to dry mills, and that naturals and honeys make up the rest; most cherry is picked by hand, which Sucafina puts down to the mountainside farms and farmers' limited means. The competition lots are more varied: the 2026 Gems of Yunnan winners include washed, natural, honey and anaerobic lots from 1,000 to 2,000 m.

## The Gems of Yunnan competition

The Alliance for Coffee Excellence, which runs the Cup of Excellence, ran a pilot competition in Yunnan in May 2025 with the Specialty Coffee Society of Yunnan. The ACE results pages give:

| Year | Entries | Top coffee | Score |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2025 | 144 | Lincang Autumn Amber Estate (Min Sai), Batian, washed, 1,650 m | 89.96 |
| 2026 | 169, of which 30 scored above 87 | Lincang Gengxia Manor (Yan Li), Geisha, washed, 1,600–1,900 m | 91.78 |

A competition shows what the best farms can do, not what an ordinary bag of Yunnan coffee tastes like. Sucafina writes that most of Yunnan's specialty coffee so far has had simple, mild profiles scoring 80 to 82.

## What Yunnan coffee tastes like

Hoffmann writes that the better coffees from China have a pleasant sweetness and fruitiness, though many still carry a little woodiness or earthiness, and that they're relatively low in acidity and often fairly full-bodied. Coffee Origins sums up how importers describe selected washed Yunnan coffees as chocolate, sweet and dark fruit. Lots from other varieties and processes, like the competition winners above, can taste quite different. These are tendencies, not a promise for any bag.

## Buying and brewing it

Yunnan coffee is easiest to find in China; outside it, a few specialty roasters and importers carry lots. A bag that names the prefecture, the variety and the process tells you most of what you need:

- **The variety.** "Catimor" on a commercial bag means the region's everyday coffee; Geisha or a Yunka selection usually means a lot grown for the specialty market.
- **The process.** Washed is the norm. Natural, honey and anaerobic lots are made in smaller amounts and taste fruitier; our [processing guide](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/) explains each.
- **The place.** Pu'er, Baoshan, Lincang and Dehong are the names you'll see most.

No special method is needed. A pour-over shows a washed lot's sweetness clearly; our [V60 guide](/blog/ultimate-pour-over-coffee-guide-and-best-v60-technique/) has a full recipe.
