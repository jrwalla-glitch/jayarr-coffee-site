Water always takes the easiest way through coffee. If part of the bed is looser, thinner, wetter or less clogged than the rest, more water goes there. That's channeling, and it's behind one of the most confusing cups in coffee: one that tastes sour and bitter at the same time.

## What channeling is

Scott Rao defines a channel in *The Professional Barista's Handbook* as an area of high-velocity flow through the coffee bed. In an espresso, he explains, the large volume of water rushing through a channel dilutes the shot and over-extracts the grounds along it, while less water reaches the denser parts of the bed, which under-extract.

Jonathan Gagné, who writes about the physics of coffee brewing, uses the word strictly for a hollow in the bed, large or microscopic, that lets a lot of water through in one place. He points out that flow can be uneven even without a classic channel, and that it can happen even when the surface of a pour-over bed looks flat at the end.

## Why the average hides it

Extraction yield is an average over every particle. In "The Dynamics of Coffee Extraction," Gagné explains that if some grounds extract much more than others, a cup with a normal-looking average still carries the less desirable tastes of the over-extracted grounds. Cameron and colleagues describe the espresso version: past a certain grind setting, the flow stopped being even and the average extraction fell, and they note that such a shot can taste both bitter and acidic. So a refractometer reading in the normal range doesn't rule channeling out.

## What causes it in pour-over

Gagné's posts, including "The Four Rules of Optimal Coffee Percolation," and his book *The Physics of Filter Coffee* name these:

- **A bed or dripper that isn't level.** Water runs to the low side.
- **A very uneven spread of particle sizes.** Rao makes the same point for drip coffee: too much variation in particle size gives a mix of over- and under-extraction through the bed.
- **Grounds left dry after the bloom.** Dry coffee resists water much more than wet coffee, so water keeps flowing through the patches that wetted first. Gagné adds that bubbles still rising after the bloom mean some coffee was dry, and the bubbles themselves open easy paths for the water. Our [bloom guide](/blog/what-is-coffee-bloom/) covers how to wet the bed evenly.
- **A clogging filter.** Fines can clog the paper, and clogging is usually uneven, so the water goes through the parts that are still open. Gagné warns that too much agitation can push fines into the filter, and that very slow drips tend to flow less evenly.
- **Pouring in one spot.** Pouring only into the center doesn't stop water from bypassing the coffee, Gagné finds, and it can dig a crater that leaves some of the coffee under-extracted.
- **A dose that doesn't suit the dripper.** In his book, Gagné writes that a too-shallow bed favors channels, and a too-tall one can leave the bottom under-extracted and clog the filter more easily.

Drip machines add one of their own. Rao notes that every spray head favors some areas of the bed, which over-extracts the coffee there and promotes channeling; our [batch brew guide](/blog/batch-brew-drip-coffee-guide/) covers his stirring fix.

## What causes it in espresso

Pressure makes every weak spot count. Gagné writes that mistakes in puck preparation, combined with espresso's higher pressure, favor channels. Rao's two books name most of the causes, and the research adds one:

- **Uneven distribution.** If more coffee lands in one part of the basket, that part is denser, and Rao says uneven distribution causes uneven extraction. Grinders that dose straight into the portafilter, he finds, tend to leave clumps.
- **A tilted or loose bed.** He wants a smooth, level surface, a tight seal against the basket wall and an even density throughout.
- **A broken seal at the edge.** Tapping the side of the portafilter after tamping can break the seal between the coffee and the basket wall. Moisture on the basket wall can also encourage channeling around the edge, so he wipes the basket dry before dosing.
- **A loose tamper.** A tamper that's too small for the basket makes channeling frequent, and it isn't always visible. See our [tamping guide](/blog/tamping-pressure-doesnt-matter-myth/).
- **Fines building up at the bottom.** Water can carry fines down through the puck, where they pack into a dense layer that clogs the basket holes and makes the resistance uneven.
- **Grinding too fine.** Cameron and colleagues found that below a certain grind setting, extraction fell and varied more from shot to shot, which they explain as the flow becoming uneven, with parts of the bed clogged.

Static can make grounds clump too; our guide to [RDT](/blog/rdt-ross-droplet-technique-grinding/) covers what the research found about a few drops of water on the beans.

## How to tell it's channeling

No single sign proves it, so look for two or three together.

- **The taste.** Sour and bitter at once, hollow in the middle. Treat it as a hypothesis: stale coffee, water and a dark roast can all cause harsh tastes.
- **The espresso stream.** With a bottomless portafilter, Rao writes, channeling shows when coffee flows faster or turns pale sooner from some parts of the basket. Spurts and a split stream are the same story. He also notes that channels aren't always visible this way.
- **The spent puck.** Gagné found a hollow near the center of all his spent pucks in one series of shots, which he put down to the way he distributed the coffee; in a later test, a paper filter under the puck made it disappear.
- **The spent pour-over bed.** A crater or hollows in the surface can point to channels, but a flat bed doesn't rule them out.
- **The drawdown.** A pour-over that drains much faster or slower than your recipe expects is worth noting alongside the taste. Our Brew Guide's V60 expects about 3:00 to 4:00; Gagné's V60 recipe usually drains in 2½ to 3½ minutes.
- **A refractometer.** Rao notes that the channels a loose tamper causes may not be visible, but they show up as lower average extraction when measured.

## Fixing it in pour-over, one change at a time

1. **Level the bed.** Shake the dripper gently after adding the coffee so the surface is flat, as Rao does with a batch brewer's basket. Gagné then presses a shallow nest in the middle with a finger before the bloom.
2. **Bloom thoroughly.** Wet every ground and swirl straight away, so no dry pockets are left when the main pour starts.
3. **Pour gently over the whole bed.** Aim at any dry or dark spots rather than one place, with a steady stream that doesn't dig a hole.
4. **Swirl, gently.** A gentle swirl after each pour can reset forming channels and leave a flat bed, Gagné writes. Keep it brief: he cautions that hard or long swirling can push fines into the filter and clog it, especially with grinders or coffees that make more fines.
5. **Check the grind.** If the brew is slow and tastes sour and bitter, the filter may be clogging; try one step coarser. A burr grinder makes a more consistent spread of sizes than a blade grinder.
6. **Match the dose to the dripper.** A very small dose in a large cone makes a shallow bed that channels easily.

Dripper shape plays a part too: our [flat-bottom vs cone guide](/blog/flat-bottom-vs-cone-pour-over/) covers what Gagné measured, and the [pour-over guide](/blog/ultimate-pour-over-coffee-guide-and-best-v60-technique/) puts the steps into a full V60 recipe.

## Fixing it in espresso, one change at a time

1. **Distribute evenly.** Wipe the basket dry, dose evenly and break up clumps. A [WDT tool](/blog/best-wdt-tool-espresso-distribution/) stirs needles through the grounds; in Gagné's tests, stirring all the way down to the bottom made the shots' resistance a little more consistent than stirring only the top, though some outlier shots remained.
2. **Tamp level, once, and don't tap.** Rao wants a tamper that fits the basket closely; our [tamping guide](/blog/tamping-pressure-doesnt-matter-myth/) explains why the level matters more than the force.
3. **Try a paper filter under the puck.** In a 10-shot test on his machine, Gagné found that a paper disc below the puck lowered its resistance to flow by a factor of about 1.4 once the shot had stabilized (calculated: about 30% lower), without changing the shot-to-shot spread. All five of his pucks without paper had a hollow in the center; none of the five with paper did. He didn't measure extraction in that test, and the lower resistance means water flows through faster, so expect to adjust the grind.
4. **Check the grind.** If the shot is slow as well as sour and bitter, grind one step coarser, keeping the dose and yield the same. Cameron's team found more even, repeatable shots with less coffee ground much coarser, at the cost of a faster, weaker shot with a different flavor; our [turbo shot guide](/blog/turbo-shots-fast-espresso-science/) covers it.
5. **Use preinfusion if you have it.** Rao writes that a short, low-pressure wetting lets the grounds swell and settle before full pressure, and lists fewer channels among its benefits; see [pressure profiling](/blog/pressure-profiling-espresso-guide/).
6. **Change one thing per shot, and write it down.** Our [dial-in guide](/blog/how-to-dial-in-espresso/) has a shot log.
