"Strong" and "weak" get used for everything from bitterness to caffeine. In brewing they mean one thing: how much coffee is dissolved in the cup. Extraction is a different number, and the two can point in opposite directions: Jessica Easto, in *Craft Coffee: A Manual*, notes that you can brew a cup that is strong and under-extracted, or weak and over-extracted, which she says describes typical diner coffee. Telling them apart tells you which change to make.

## Two numbers, one link

**Strength** is TDS: the share of the drink that is dissolved coffee. A cup at 1.4% TDS is 1.4% coffee solids and 98.6% water.

**Extraction yield** is the share of the dry coffee that dissolved: drink weight × TDS ÷ dose. Our guide to [extraction yield](/blog/coffee-extraction-yield-explained/) covers how to measure it.

The two are linked by the amount of water: strength = extraction × dose ÷ drink weight. That gives two quite different ways to change strength, and the worked examples below show the difference. The readings in them are illustrative; the arithmetic is calculated.

## Diluting after brewing changes strength only

Take the Brew Guide's espresso: 18 g of coffee makes a 36 g shot. Say it reads 10% TDS. That's 3.6 g of dissolved coffee, an extraction of 3.6 ÷ 18 = 20%.

Now add 144 g of hot water to make an americano. The drink weighs 180 g and still holds the same 3.6 g of dissolved coffee, so it reads 3.6 ÷ 180 = 2.0% TDS. Strength has fallen to a fifth. Extraction hasn't moved: it's still 3.6 ÷ 18 = 20%, because nothing more came out of the coffee.

The same goes for cold brew concentrate. Easto's French-press cold brew, which the Brew Guide uses, steeps 96 g of coffee in 600 g of cold water, and the concentrate is mixed one to one with water or milk to serve. If the concentrate read 2.5%, its extraction would be 600 × 2.5% ÷ 96 ≈ 15.6% (for immersion brews you use all the water added), and the served drink would read about 1.25%: half the strength, the same extraction. Read an immersion brew before you dilute it: as the Extraction Map explains, a reading taken after dilution makes the extraction look too low.

## Pouring more water through the coffee changes both

A percolation brew is different, because the extra water passes through the grounds. Take the V60 recipe in our [pour-over guide](/blog/ultimate-pour-over-coffee-guide-and-best-v60-technique/): 22 g of coffee and 352 g of water (1:16) give about 308 g in the cup, since the grounds hold back about 2 g of water per gram. Say it reads 1.43%: that's about 4.4 g dissolved, an extraction of 20%.

Pour to 374 g instead (1:17) and about 330 g reaches the cup. If extraction stayed at 20%, the same 4.4 g in 330 g would read about 1.33%. In practice extraction rises a little, because more water has passed through the coffee. Scott Rao makes this point about espresso in *Espresso Extraction: Measurement and Mastery*: run extra water through the grounds and extraction increases while strength decreases. So the lighter ratio lands a little above 1.33%, but it's still weaker.

The Extraction Map draws the ratio the same way: more water for the same coffee mostly lowers strength and nudges extraction up; more coffee for the same water mostly raises strength and nudges extraction down.

## Reading your cup on both axes

Taste can suggest where a cup sits, but only as a hypothesis: water, freshness and an uneven bed cause similar tastes. Easto describes the strength axis as body. Too much coffee for the water gives a thick mouthfeel and muddled flavors; too little gives a thin mouthfeel, weak flavors and a faint aroma. She describes under-extracted cups as often sour and lacking complexity, and over-extracted ones as often bitter and astringent.

These are the Extraction Map's zones for those tastes, and the first moves it suggests:

| How it tastes | Where it likely sits | First change |
|---|---|---|
| Sour and watery | Under-extracted and weak | Grind finer; then more coffee if it's still thin |
| Sour, body about right | Under-extracted | Grind finer, or hotter water, or longer contact |
| Pleasant but thin | Well extracted, too dilute | More coffee for the same water |
| Heavy and intense, but clean | Well extracted, too strong | More water for the same coffee, or dilute in the cup |
| Bitter and thin | Over-extracted and weak | Grind coarser; then more coffee if it's still thin |
| Bitter, drying finish | Over-extracted | Grind coarser, or slightly cooler water, or shorter contact |
| Harsh and heavy | Over-extracted and strong | Grind coarser; then more water if it's still heavy |

Two cups don't fit the grid. A cup that tastes sour and bitter at once, hollow in the middle, usually points to uneven extraction rather than a wrong average; see our guide to [channeling](/blog/fix-channeling-uneven-extraction/). And a cup can be bitter from a dark roast however well it's brewed; our guide to [bitter coffee](/blog/why-coffee-tastes-bitter-how-to-fix/) separates the causes.

## Which to fix first

Fix the taste before the strength. If a cup is sour or bitter, change the grind one step and keep the ratio. Once it tastes balanced, set the strength with the ratio: one step stronger (1:16 to 1:15) or lighter (1:16 to 1:17). Fix My Coffee works in that order and suggests one change at a time, so you can tell which change helped. Our [ratio guide](/blog/coffee-to-water-ratio-guide/) has the amounts for each step.

## How strong is normal?

There's no single right strength. Easto writes that most good cups fall somewhere between 1% and 2% TDS, and that most people in the US would find 1% too weak and 2% too strong. Rao reports that most professionals seem to prefer 1.25% to 1.45% for brews made without pressure, such as filter coffee, and 10% to 13% for espresso. The SCA's 2019 brewing chart uses 1.15% to 1.45% as its reference range for brewed coffee; our [extraction yield guide](/blog/coffee-extraction-yield-explained/) covers it by edition.

Espresso has no SCA chart. The Extraction Map treats 8% to 12% TDS as a rule of thumb for espresso strength, a little lower than the range Rao reports; neither is a target your shot has to hit.

Preference really does vary. In a UC Davis study, Cotter and colleagues had 118 black-coffee drinkers rate brews of one coffee at different strengths and extractions. They split into two groups whose preferences varied most with strength, and the authors concluded that the chart's single "ideal" should be reconsidered.
