A phin is a small metal drip filter that sits on top of a glass or mug. Hot water seeps slowly through a bed of coffee held under a metal press, and what drips out is a small, strong cup. In Vietnam it's often brewed onto sweetened condensed milk and poured over ice as cà phê sữa đá, which translates as "coffee, milk, ice."

The recipe below follows Nguyen Coffee Supply, a Brooklyn roaster of Vietnamese coffee whose online guides give the amounts, the bloom and the drip timings. For Vietnam as a coffee origin, see our [Vietnam coffee guide](/blog/vietnam-coffee-origin-guide/).

## How a phin works

Nguyen Coffee Supply describes four parts:

- **The plate,** which rests on the rim of your glass.
- **The brewing chamber,** a small cup with holes in its floor that holds the coffee and water. The plate and the chamber are the filter: there's no paper.
- **The press,** a perforated disc that sits on the coffee and holds the bed in place. Nguyen notes that, unlike in most brewers, it goes in before the water.
- **The lid,** which keeps the heat in and doubles as a stand for the dripping phin afterward.

Presses come in two kinds. A gravity press simply rests on the coffee. A screw-down press threads into the chamber, so you choose how tightly the bed is packed and, with it, how fast the coffee drips. Nguyen says the screw-down kind works best with pre-ground coffee, while a gravity press lets fresh coffee's gas escape as it brews, which is why the company prefers it.

Phins also come in sizes. Nguyen describes three of its own: a 4 oz phin for a single cup, a 12 oz model for about two to three servings and a 24 oz model for 6 to 12. The recipe here is for a 4 oz phin; for larger ones Nguyen keeps the same proportion, about 1 tablespoon of coffee for every 2 oz of water, and suggests a second pour of hot water, since the grounds soak some up.

## A recipe for one glass of cà phê sữa đá

This is Nguyen Coffee Supply's recipe for Vietnamese iced coffee, with its numbers. The water's weight in grams is calculated from its ounces.

- **Coffee:** 14 g, about 2 tablespoons, ground fine, "like sand"
- **Water:** 4 oz, about 118 g, at 195–200°F&nbsp;(91–93°C): about 1 oz (30 g) to bloom, then the rest. That's about 1:8.5 coffee to water by weight (calculated): nearly twice the coffee per gram of water of a V60 at 1:16.
- **Condensed milk:** Nguyen leaves the amount to you. About 2 tablespoons (1 fl oz, 38 g) is a reasonable place to start; that's our suggestion, not Nguyen's, so adjust it to taste.
- **Time:** about a minute to set up, a 45-second bloom and about 5 minutes of dripping; 6 to 7 minutes in all, nearly all of it waiting.
- **Makes:** about 90 g of coffee, about 3 US fl oz (90 ml), before the milk and ice. That's calculated: 118 g of water, less about 28 g held back by the grounds at roughly 2 g per gram of coffee, the figure our Brew Guide uses.

Nguyen's own pages don't all give the same water temperature: this recipe says 195–200°F, and its phin explainer heats the water to 205°F&nbsp;(96°C). Either is a reasonable start. If the coffee tastes too strong, Nguyen's answer is simple: add hot water to the cup, or give the phin a second pour.

## Grind finer than you might expect

A phin wants a fine grind. Nguyen's recipe says "fine, like sand," and its explainer puts the grind between medium and fine, about what you'd use for a moka pot: finer than for pour-over, but not as fine as for espresso. Too coarse, as Nguyen puts it, and the coffee drips too fast.

## When the drip runs too fast or too slow

Nguyen treats its timings as a guide: the first drop at about 2 minutes and the last at about 5. "By no means are you doing it wrong," it says, if yours is a little faster or slower. The trouble starts when a phin empties in under a minute, or doesn't drip at all for more than 5 minutes. Its troubleshooting points to these causes:

- **Stalled or very slow.** The grind may be too fine. A phin that's still wet from washing can also block its own holes, so dry it completely. And fresh coffee releases gas that can create suction in the chamber and stop the flow: lift the press with a spoon or chopstick for about 2 seconds to let the gas out, and the coffee should start dripping. The same page suggests blooming the coffee for about 45 seconds before you set the press in, to avoid it.
- **Rushing through.** The grind is probably too coarse. Too little coffee does it too: Nguyen says less than 2 tablespoons in a 4 oz phin may drip fast.
- **With a screw-down press,** how tightly you screw it down sets the speed of the drip, Nguyen says, so adjust that before the grind.

Change one thing at a time, so you know what fixed it.

## Choosing the coffee

Vietnam grows mostly robusta. USDA's July 2026 forecast puts robusta at 31.4 of Vietnam's 32.5 million 60 kg bags for 2026/27, about 97 percent (calculated). James Hoffmann describes robusta's taste as woody, with a burnt-rubber quality, very little acidity and a heavy body, and notes that it has about twice the caffeine of arabica. Nguyen calls its robusta "indicative of Vietnamese coffee culture," and for stronger coffee it suggests its darker roasts.

Any coffee will brew in a phin, arabica included, and Nguyen sells all-arabica, all-robusta and mixed blends for it. For the traditional cup, look for a Vietnamese robusta or a blend that names its robusta share.

## Condensed milk, and the other ways to drink it

**Sweetened condensed milk** is milk cooked down with sugar. USDA's figures for it work out to about 120 calories and 21 g of sugar in 2 tablespoons (1 fl oz, 38 g), calculated from its values per 100 g. Use less if you want a less sweet glass.

**Read the can.** Some Vietnamese "condensed milk" is a creamer made partly with vegetable oil. Vinamilk's Ông Thọ Green Label, for example, is labeled a sweetened creamer, and its ingredients begin with sugar (47.2 percent), water and vegetable oil.

**Black.** Nguyen's guide ends with "enjoy with sweetened condensed milk, sugar, milk, or black." Black phin coffee is cà phê đen, served hot (nóng) or over ice (đá).

**Egg coffee.** Cà phê trứng, coffee under a sweet whipped egg topping, is a Hanoi specialty. Café Giảng in Hanoi, where it is the signature drink, dates it to 1946 on its own site.

## Caffeine, cholesterol and sugar

A phin uses a lot of coffee for a small cup. Our Caffeine Calculator has no phin or robusta setting, but with its assumptions (caffeine at 0.9–1.3 percent of roasted arabica and 1.7–2.5 percent of robusta, and 75 to 95 percent of it reaching the cup), 14 g of coffee works out to about 95–175 mg with arabica and about 180–330 mg with robusta (calculated). The European Food Safety Authority says "single doses of caffeine up to 200mg" don't raise safety concerns for the general healthy adult population, so a glass of all-robusta phin coffee can reach that on its own. For a whole day, the FDA cites 400 mg for most adults as an amount not generally associated with negative effects. Those are population guides, not personal limits; if you're pregnant or caffeine affects you, ask your clinician. Our [caffeine guide](/blog/caffeine-in-coffee-guide/) has more.

A phin filters through metal, not paper. In their review, Rob Urgert and Martijn Katan found that paper filters hold back cafestol and kahweol, the coffee oils that raise LDL cholesterol, which is why filtered coffee doesn't raise it while boiled and plunger coffee do. We found no measurement of phin coffee itself; like the [French press](/blog/how-to-use-a-french-press-the-most-amazing-coffee-youve-ever-had/), it has no paper. If you drink several cups a day and are watching your cholesterol, ask your clinician whether a paper-filtered brew is worth choosing.
