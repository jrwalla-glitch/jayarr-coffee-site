A latte-art heart is two skills: milk with the right texture, and a pour that puts that milk either under the crema or on top of it. The pour below follows La Marzocco's beginner's guide to the heart and Scott Rao's *The Professional Barista's Handbook*. If the milk isn't right, no pour will save it, so that comes first.

## What you need

- **A fresh espresso with crema.** Rao's first condition for latte art is "a fresh shot of espresso with a reasonable amount of crema." Fresher beans give more crema: in *Espresso Extraction*, Rao explains that they release more carbon dioxide while brewing, so their shots have larger crema. Our [espresso dial-in guide](/blog/how-to-dial-in-espresso/) covers the shot.
- **A plain steam wand.** The method here is for a traditional wand. If your machine has a frothing sleeve or an automatic milk setting, James Hoffmann's advice is to follow the maker's instructions. No espresso machine? Our [milk frother comparison](/blog/best-milk-frothers-home-baristas/) covers what the alternatives claim to make.
- **A spouted pitcher.** Rao notes that the spout lets you direct the milk, which is what makes latte art easier to pour. Use the smallest pitcher that comfortably holds the milk you need.
- **Whole milk, to learn with.** Protein holds the bubbles, so skim milk foams too, Hoffmann says, but he recommends whole milk for its texture.
- **A wide cup.** Rao's drink recipes assume a classic 6 to 8&nbsp;oz wide-mouthed ceramic cup.

## Get the milk right first

Our [milk steaming guide](/blog/how-to-steam-milk-for-lattes/) has the full method. In short, from Rao, Hoffmann and Breville's Bambino manual:

1. **Add the air while the milk is cold.** Keep the wand tip just under the surface so it makes a steady, subtle sucking sound, and finish stretching before the milk reaches 100°F&nbsp;(38°C). Rao finds good foam harder to make once the milk is warmer than that.
2. **Then spin and heat.** Sink the tip a little, off to one side, so the milk rolls quietly.
3. **Stop in time.** Breville's manual puts the milk in range at 140–149°F&nbsp;(60–65°C), when the jug is hot to touch. Hoffmann says milk begins to degrade above 154°F&nbsp;(68°C). Rao's café target is 150–160°F&nbsp;(66–71°C), and the top of that is past Hoffmann's limit.
4. **Tap, swirl and pour soon.** Tap the pitcher to pop big bubbles, then swirl until the milk and foam are combined and glossy, Hoffmann's last step. Rao pours within 30 seconds of steaming, because the milk's texture degrades with time.

For a latte, Rao wants moderately aerated milk, with about ¼&nbsp;inch (about 6&nbsp;mm) of foam once the drink separates; for a cappuccino, about ½&nbsp;inch (about 13&nbsp;mm). For latte art, La Marzocco describes the milk as like wet paint: it should cling to the sides of the pitcher and move freely, without being stiff. Rao's test is the surface: creamy and glassy, with no visible bubbles.

## Why the height of the pitcher matters

Rao explains it with a diving board. Pouring from high up is like diving from a high board: the stream picks up speed, plunges to the bottom of the cup and hardly disturbs the crema. Pouring with the spout right at the surface is like stepping off the edge of a pool: the milk skims across the top, and that's where the white shape forms.

So the first part of the pour, from up high, mixes milk into the coffee, and the shape only starts when the spout comes down. La Marzocco also notes that the closer the pitcher is to the drink, the thicker the lines.

Rao names the two most common beginner mistakes as pouring too slowly and lifting the pitcher away from the surface. A slow pour lets the milk separate in the pitcher, so the thinner milk goes into the cup and the foam stays behind. A lifted pitcher sends the milk under the crema just when you want it on top.

## Fixing common pour problems

| What you see | Likely cause | What to try |
|---|---|---|
| No white shape; the milk disappears | The spout is too high | Bring it down almost to the surface and keep it there until the final line. |
| A flat, brown drink with foam left in the pitcher | Poured too slowly, so the milk separated | Swirl just before pouring, and pour a little faster. |
| Big bubbles on the surface | Coarse foam | Tap the pitcher and swirl until glossy. Next time, keep the tip just under the surface while stretching. |
| Thick foam that plops out in lumps | Too much air | Skim some foam off with a spoon and swirl again, Rao's "grooming." |
| Thin milk that won't make a white shape | Too little air | You can't add air afterward. Steam a fresh pitcher and stretch it a little longer. |
| Pale, thin crema with little contrast | Little crema in the shot | Check the roast date: Rao links larger crema to fresher beans. Pour as soon as the shot finishes. |

These are common causes, not certainties: the fixes for milk come from Rao and Hoffmann, and the pour faults from Rao.

## After the heart: the rosetta

Rao's step-by-step pour in *The Professional Barista's Handbook* is the rosetta, the fern-shaped pattern, and it uses the same two ideas as the heart: a steady flow and a low pitcher.

1. Pour the milk into the center of the crema, quickly enough that it doesn't separate in the pitcher but slowly enough to keep the crema intact.
2. Once the white cloud appears, rock the pitcher back and forth to make a zigzag. Keep the pitcher as low as possible, and keep tipping it further so the flow stays the same as it empties.
3. Back the pitcher toward the edge of the cup while you zigzag.
4. At the edge, lift the pitcher a couple of inches and drizzle a small stream back across the center of the zigzags.

The heart uses only the high-then-low pour and the final line, so it's a good pattern to master first.

## Plant milks

Barista plant milks steam in the same two phases. In Tarasov and colleagues' 2025 tests, barista plant drinks steamed at 149°F&nbsp;(65°C) foamed well enough that the authors judged all of them workable alternatives to milk for a cappuccino. Our [plant milk guide](/blog/best-plant-milks-for-coffee/) compares the cartons, and the [oat milk vs dairy](/blog/oat-milk-vs-dairy-in-coffee/) comparison covers what barista formulations add.
