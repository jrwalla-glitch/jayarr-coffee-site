Espresso and drip coffee can start from the same bag of beans. What differs is how much water meets the coffee, how fine the coffee is ground, how fast the water goes through and what pushes it. Those differences set how strong each cup is and how much caffeine it carries, and the two answers aren't the same.

## Two methods, side by side

James Hoffmann defines espresso broadly in *The World Atlas of Coffee*: a small, strong drink made from finely ground coffee under high water pressure, with crema on top, at about 1 part coffee to 2 parts drink by weight. That's a method, so any bean or roast can be brewed as espresso; a bag labeled "espresso" tells you what the roaster had in mind. Our guide to [what espresso is](/blog/what-espresso-actually-is/) covers the definition, its history and the puck in depth.

Here are our Brew Guide's starting points for each:

| | Espresso | Drip machine | Pour-over (V60) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coffee and water | 18&nbsp;g of coffee to 36&nbsp;g in the cup | 1:17 by weight | 1:16 by weight |
| Grind | Fine, like powdered sugar, about 200–300&nbsp;µm | Medium, like sand, about 600–900&nbsp;µm | Medium-fine, like table salt, about 450–650&nbsp;µm |
| Time | 25–30 seconds | The brewer showers the water over about 4 minutes | Done around 3:00–4:00 |
| Water | 199°F&nbsp;(93°C), pushed by the machine's pump | 201°F&nbsp;(94°C), by gravity | 201°F&nbsp;(94°C), by gravity |

Those temperatures are for a medium roast; the Brew Guide raises them a little for light roasts and lowers them for dark. The drip ratio is the Brew Guide's adaptation of Scott Rao's batch-brew ratio, and the V60's is the one in our [pour-over guide](/blog/ultimate-pour-over-coffee-guide-and-best-v60-technique/).

The ratio is the big difference. Per gram of coffee, a shot holds about 2 g of drink, while a drip cup holds about 15 g, once you take off the water the wet grounds keep, at roughly 2 g per gram of coffee (calculated from the Brew Guide's ratios).

Pressure is what makes that possible. Hoffmann explains that the finer coffee is ground, the less water it takes to extract it, so the stronger the cup can be; but grind finely enough and gravity alone can't push the water through. Pressure gets past that limit. Rao explains in *Espresso Extraction* that more pump pressure pushes water through faster only up to a point: somewhere between 7 and 9 bar, measured at the group head, the flow peaks, because beyond that the pressure compacts the coffee more than it pushes the water.

## Strength: how much coffee is dissolved

Strength is the share of the drink that's dissolved coffee, measured with a refractometer as total dissolved solids (TDS).

- **Filter coffee:** the Specialty Coffee Association's brewing control chart, revised in March 2019, uses 1.15 to 1.45% TDS as its reference range.
- **Espresso:** the SCA has no chart for espresso. Our Extraction Map uses 8 to 12% TDS as a common rule of thumb, and Rao reports that most professionals seem to prefer 10 to 13% for espresso, and 1.25 to 1.45% for brews made without pressure.

Comparing the middle of each range, 10% against 1.3%, a shot is about eight times as strong as a cup of drip (calculated). Almost all of that comes from the ratio. Strength works out as extraction × coffee ÷ drink: at a 20% extraction, 18 g of coffee in a 36 g shot gives 10% TDS, while a drip cup holding 15 g of drink per gram of coffee gives about 1.3% (calculated). Our guide to [strength versus extraction](/blog/coffee-strength-vs-extraction/) explains the difference between the two.

## Extraction: a similar target

Extraction is how much of the coffee ended up dissolved in the drink. The SCA's 2019 chart uses 18 to 22% as its reference range for filter coffee, and the Extraction Map applies the same range to espresso. Rao's espresso book assumes a goal of 19 to 20% for a normal shot, and notes that when pulling a normal shot it's easy to under-extract and difficult, if not impossible, to over-extract.

With a refractometer, extraction is arithmetic: drink weight × TDS ÷ coffee dose. An 18 g dose that makes a 36 g shot at 10% TDS has extracted 3.6 g, or 20%. A 20 g pour-over that makes 300 g of coffee at 1.3% TDS has extracted 3.9 g, or 19.5% (both calculated). So a well-made shot and a well-made cup can extract about the same share of the coffee, at very different strengths.

These ranges are references for comparing brews, not grades. On the Extraction Map's account, a cup below the range tends to taste sour and hollow and one above it bitter and drying, but taste is a hypothesis and only a refractometer gives the number. Our [extraction yield guide](/blog/coffee-extraction-yield-explained/) explains the chart by edition.

## Caffeine: per drink and per ounce

Here the comparison flips. These are our Caffeine Calculator's estimates, rounded to the nearest 5 mg. For drip, the calculator estimates caffeine from the Brew Guide's dose and an assumed arabica content; espresso uses a separate 60–75 mg estimate per shot. The last column is calculated from the first two.

| Drink | Serving | Caffeine | Per fl oz |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single espresso | 1 shot, about 1 fl oz | 60–75&nbsp;mg | 60–75&nbsp;mg |
| Double espresso, or a latte made with two shots | 2 shots | 120–150&nbsp;mg | 60–75&nbsp;mg of espresso |
| Drip | 8 fl oz (237 ml) | 105–195&nbsp;mg | about 13–24&nbsp;mg |
| Drip | 16 fl oz (473 ml) | 215–390&nbsp;mg | about 13–24&nbsp;mg |

**Per ounce, espresso wins.** On the calculator's ranges, a shot carries about 2½ to 6 times as much caffeine per ounce as drip. The USDA's figures, 212 mg per 100 g for espresso and 40 mg for brewed coffee, put it at about five times (both calculated).

**Per drink, drip usually wins.** A single shot holds less caffeine than an 8 fl oz mug of drip. A double lands inside the 8 fl oz range, and a 16 fl oz drip carries more than either. A latte's milk adds volume, not caffeine.

Two things move these numbers: the dose and the bean. Brew with less coffee and you get less caffeine, in proportion, and a blend with robusta runs higher, since Jessica Easto notes robusta carries about twice the caffeine of arabica. A café's cup can also be bigger or stronger than these. If a chain publishes the caffeine for your drink, the calculator's "I know the mg" option takes that figure as it is. Our [caffeine guide](/blog/caffeine-in-coffee-guide/) covers more drinks, and what the FDA and EFSA say about daily amounts, in their own words.

## Crema

Crema, the foam on a shot, comes from the pressure. Hoffmann explains that water under very high pressure can dissolve more of the carbon dioxide that roasting leaves in the coffee; when the shot reaches normal air pressure, the gas comes out as tiny bubbles. Drip never pressurizes the water, so it makes no crema; the gas escapes in the bloom instead. Hoffmann adds that crema tells you only two things: roughly how fresh the coffee is, and how strong the shot is, from its color. It can't tell you whether the coffee is good.

## Which should you choose?

They answer different wants. Drip and pour-over make a mug or a pot from a medium grind, with a brewer and a burr grinder. Espresso makes a small, strong drink, on its own or under milk, and needs a machine that holds its temperature and pressure steady and a grinder that can grind fine and evenly. Because espresso pushes a little water through a lot of coffee in well under a minute, small changes show up in the cup, so expect to adjust shot by shot.
