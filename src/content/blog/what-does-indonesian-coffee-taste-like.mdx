If you've had a Sumatra, you know the reputation: heavy, low in acidity, earthy. That's real, but it describes one kind of Indonesian coffee, made one way. Indonesia grows coffee on many islands, mostly robusta, and its arabica ranges from the wet-hulled lots that made the name to fully washed coffees that taste quite different.

<LiteYouTube videoId="iuX5GJFl6Q0" title="What does Indonesian Coffee Taste Like?" />

Our October 2019 video gives the short version: a strong, robust flavor with mild acidity and a heavy body, generally earthy, with notes of spiced sweetness, wood, tobacco and leather. It calls Indonesia the world's fourth-largest coffee producer. In USDA's July 2026 figures, Indonesia was fourth for 2025/26 and is fifth in the forecast for 2026/27, behind Brazil, Vietnam, Colombia and Ethiopia.

## Most of it is robusta

USDA forecasts Indonesia's 2026/27 crop at 11.4 million 60&nbsp;kg bags, of which 10.0 million are robusta and 1.4 million arabica, so robusta is about 88 percent (calculated). USDA says about 75 percent of the robusta grows in the lowlands of southern Sumatra and Java.

History explains the split. Hoffmann writes that Indonesia grew only arabica until coffee leaf rust wiped out much of the crop in 1876; growers tried Liberica, which also suffered from rust, then switched to the disease-resistant robusta, which is still a significant part of the crop. Our [arabica vs robusta guide](/blog/arabica-vs-robusta-differences/) covers how the two species differ.

Almost all of it is grown on small farms. Hoffmann writes that most is grown by smallholders with 1 to 2 hectares, so a coffee is usually traceable only to a washing station or a region. Jati Misnawi of the Indonesian Coffee and Cocoa Research Institute, writing in 2013, put smallholders' share at 95.5 percent of the 1.3 million hectares planted, from 2010 data.

## What wet-hulling does to the taste

Indonesia's traditional process, *giling basah*, is a hybrid of the washed and natural methods. Hoffmann describes it: the coffee is depulped and dried only part of the way, hulled out of its parchment while still moist, then dried again as a bare bean. It's the one exception to the usual practice of keeping the parchment on the beans until just before shipping.

Hoffmann lists what it does to the cup: lower acidity and more body, with flavors such as wood, earth, mustiness, spice, tobacco and leather. He calls the taste deeply divisive. Many in the industry consider the process a form of defect, yet plenty of drinkers love the heavy, intense cups, and the market has come to associate those flavors with Indonesia. Many also feel those flavors dominate, so that, as he puts it, "we rarely get to find out how coffees from Indonesia really taste."

That's why the process on the bag matters as much as the island. Specialty buyers have been encouraging Indonesian producers to try the washed process, so that the variety and the land come through. Our [processing guide](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/) explains wet-hulling beside the other methods, and our [Sumatra taste test](/blog/7-sumatra-coffees-reviewed-best-and-worst/) covers how it shapes a Sumatran cup.

## Island by island

Hoffmann's atlas gives these growing areas, altitudes and harvests for Indonesian arabica:

| Island: main areas | Altitude | Harvest |
|---|---|---|
| **Sumatra:** Aceh (Gayo), Lake Toba, Mangkuraja | 1,100–1,600 m | Sept–Dec |
| **Java:** around the Ijen volcano in the east; some in the west | 900–1,800 m | July–Sept |
| **Sulawesi:** Tana Toraja, Mamasa, Gowa | 850–1,800 m | May–Nov |
| **Flores:** Bajawa | 1,200–1,800 m | May–Sept |
| **Bali:** Kintamani | 1,250–1,700 m | May–Oct |

Gowa's 850 m is Hoffmann's average for that area. The tasting descriptions below come from Hoffmann and from Misnawi's chapter in *Coffee: A Comprehensive Guide*. They describe what each area is known for, not what any one bag will taste like.

### Sumatra

Sumatra grows most of the country's coffee, about three-quarters of production in Misnawi's 2013 chapter, with robusta in the southern lowlands and the best-known arabicas from the highlands of Aceh and around Lake Toba in the north. Many of those arabicas are the wet-hulled cups the country is known for, but they aren't all alike. Misnawi describes Gayo coffee from Aceh as having a strong aroma and balanced body; Mandheling as complex in body, low in acidity and bitterness, spicy, slightly earthy and with a small-fruit flavor; Lintong as clean with good body; and Sidikalang as strong and sharp, with high acidity and a slightly grassy, green note. Our [Sumatra taste test](/blog/7-sumatra-coffees-reviewed-best-and-worst/) has the island's regions, what "Mandheling" and "Grade 1" mean, and seven coffees scored.

### Java

Java has more large estates than anywhere else in Indonesia, a legacy of Dutch colonial rule; Hoffmann notes that the four largest, once government estates, cover over 4,000 hectares between them. Misnawi describes the "Java" arabica from the government-owned PTPN XII estates in East Java as medium-bodied, chocolaty, flowery and balanced, wet-processed with 24 to 36 hours of fermentation and then sun-dried, from around Mount Ijen at 900 to 1,400 m. He also notes that until 1921, coffee from all over the Dutch East Indies was sold as Java; that year the US Department of Agriculture required the "Java Coffee" name to be used only for arabica from Java itself.

One Javanese style is the opposite of clean. Some estates age their coffee for up to five years before export, as **Old Brown Java**. Hoffmann writes that the green beans turn from blue-green to a muddy brown, and once roasted the coffee has no acidity left at all, with an intense pungency and woodiness: "if you like your coffee sweet, clean and lively, you may well hate it."

### Sulawesi

Most of Sulawesi's coffee comes from smallholders; Hoffmann counts seven large estates making about five percent of production. Most of its arabica grows high up around Tana Toraja, and the town of Kalosi to the south became a brand name of its own, so you'll see both "Toraja" and "Kalosi" on bags. Misnawi describes Toraja coffee as highly complex, with chocolate, sweetness and spicy herb notes. Wet-hulling is still common here, but Hoffmann writes that some of the island's most interesting coffees are fully washed and can be exceptionally enjoyable, and recommends seeking them out.

### Flores

Flores, east of Bali, came late to coffee and to a reputation for it, and much of its crop used to be sold within Indonesia or blended rather than exported as Flores coffee. Hoffmann credits its volcanoes with improving the soils, and Misnawi describes the Bajawa area's andosols, dark, porous volcanic soils he calls ideal for arabica, and its coffee as chocolaty, sweet and fruity with a slightly citrus aftertaste. Hoffmann writes that wet-hulling is still extremely common there, with some fully washed coffee too.

### Bali

Coffee came late to Bali, on the Kintamani highland plateau, and suffered a severe setback when Mount Agung erupted in 1963. Hoffmann writes that despite the government handing out arabica seedlings in the late 1970s and early 1980s, around 80 percent of the island's production was robusta in his 2018 account. Misnawi describes Kintamani arabica, grown among older orange trees, as fruity with mild acidity, and notes it was the first agricultural product in Indonesia to receive a Geographical Indication certificate. He gives Kintamani's altitude as 900 to 1,500 m, lower than Hoffmann's range for Bali.

## The varieties

Indonesian bags rarely name a variety, and Hoffmann writes that most Sumatran coffee is probably a mixture of unknown varieties. The names you may see:

- **Typica** in local forms. Hoffmann notes that much of the arabica first brought to Sumatra descended from the Typica taken from Yemen, often called Djember Typica there, while "Djember" in Sulawesi names a different, lesser variety.
- **Hybrids with robusta in their ancestry,** which Hoffmann calls common in Sumatra. The best known is the Timor Hybrid, a parent of Catimor, called TimTim in Sumatra.
- **Ateng, S795 and USDA,** which Hoffmann lists among the varieties on different islands.

## Reading an Indonesian bag

- **The island and region,** not just "Indonesia". Regional coffees vary widely in quality, and Hoffmann calls them something of a gamble.
- **The process.** "Washed" or "fully washed" means a cleaner cup; wet-hulled, semi-washed or *giling basah* means the classic heavy, earthy profile.
- **A farm or washing station,** if it's named. Hoffmann writes that traceable, fully washed lots are rare but definitely worth trying.
- **Skip kopi luwak.** Hoffmann calls it abhorrent: forgery is commonplace, civets are caged and force-fed, and a quarter of the price of a bag would buy a stunning coffee instead. Our [kopi luwak guide](/blog/kopi-luwak-truth-ethical-fraud/) covers the evidence.

## Brewing it

No special recipe is needed; brew it as you would any coffee and change the grind by taste. If it's the heavy body you're after, a French press keeps more of it: Hoffmann notes that its metal mesh lets a little of the coffee's oil and tiny particles into the cup, which gives a bigger, richer body and texture.
