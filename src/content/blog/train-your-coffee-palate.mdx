A trained palate isn't a gift. It's mostly practice at comparing. In *The World Atlas of Coffee*, James Hoffmann asks how professional tasters develop their skills so much faster than coffee drinkers, and answers that it isn't the cupping bowls, the spoons or the score sheets: it's regular opportunities for comparative tasting. Taste one coffee on its own and you're comparing it with your memory of other coffees, which he calls patchy and inaccurate. Taste two side by side and the differences start to show.

## What you're learning to notice

Hoffmann lists five things that almost every coffee score sheet assesses, and they're a good place to start:

- **Sweetness:** how much the coffee has. Generally, the more the better.
- **Acidity:** how much, and how pleasant. A lot of unpleasant acidity reads as sour; a lot of pleasant acidity gives a crisp or juicy cup.
- **Mouthfeel:** light, delicate and tea-like, or rich, creamy and heavy. More isn't necessarily better.
- **Balance:** whether the parts work together, or one of them is too loud.
- **Flavor:** the aromas and flavors, and how pleasant you find them. He notes this is the part new tasters find most frustrating.

Hoffmann's own home method starts with mouthfeel, sweetness and acidity and leaves flavor until last, and the plan below does the same.

## The tools: a flavor wheel and a lexicon

**The Coffee Taster's Flavor Wheel.** The Specialty Coffee Association (then the SCAA) first published its wheel in 1995 and redrew it in 2016 with World Coffee Research (WCR). The new wheel is built from WCR's Sensory Lexicon: at UC Davis, 72 coffee experts sorted the lexicon's attributes into groups in an online exercise, without tasting anything, and statistical clustering of their sorting, with some adjustments by the SCAA and the researchers, gave the wheel its nine main categories and three tiers.

The SCA's 2016 guide to using it says to start at the center, where the most general words are, and work outward, stopping wherever you like: fruity, then citrus fruit, then perhaps lemon or grapefruit. Then go back to the center and look for the next flavor. Cells that touch were judged closely related by the tasters in the research; a gap means they were judged less closely related. The colors were chosen to match the words, so "something red" can send you to the red fruits.

**The WCR Sensory Lexicon.** The lexicon is the dictionary behind the wheel. It was developed at Kansas State University's Sensory Analysis Center and validated by a second panel at Texas A&M University, from 105 arabica samples from 13 countries. It names 110 attributes of flavor, aroma and texture, and gives each one a definition and at least one reference you can smell or taste, most of them with an intensity on a 0-to-15 scale. Jasmine extract on a cotton ball, for example, is its reference for jasmine aroma at an intensity of 8.5. The second edition (version 2.0, October 2017) added shelf-stable references for 24 attributes from the UK company FlavorActiV.

Two things about the lexicon are worth knowing before you use it. It's purely descriptive: it has no "good" or "bad" words and doesn't rank coffee, and WCR says it isn't a replacement for cupping. And many of its references are American grocery products, such as a named brand of canned blueberries. WCR's own trained panels, typically five to seven tasters, train for six to nine months to agree with the lexicon and with each other; that's for scientific measurement, a different goal from learning what you like.

## An 8-week plan

This plan is ours. It puts Hoffmann's at-home method and the SCA's and WCR's tools in an order that goes from big differences to small ones. Two weeks per step is a suggestion: stay on a step until its differences feel obvious, however long that takes.

### Weeks 1–2: two coffees, brewed the same way

Hoffmann's home method, step by step:

1. Buy two very different coffees. He suggests asking your local roaster for help choosing them.
2. Brew a small cup of each the same way. He uses two small French presses.
3. Let them cool a little; flavors are easier to pick out in warm coffee than in hot.
4. Taste them alternately, a couple of sips of one, then the other.
5. Start with texture, sweetness and acidity: which feels heavier, which is sweeter, which has the cleaner acidity? Write words down without reading the bags.
6. Don't worry about naming flavors. Any words that describe what you taste are useful.
7. Then read the roasters' descriptions and see what they were trying to say.

Once that feels easy, add one more comparison of our own: the same coffee through two filters. Hoffmann writes that paper filters give the cleanest cup, because they hold back the suspended particles and the oils, while metal filters like a French press's let both through and add body. A [French press](/blog/how-to-use-a-french-press-the-most-amazing-coffee-youve-ever-had/) next to a paper-filter [pour-over](/blog/ultimate-pour-over-coffee-guide-and-best-v60-technique/) of the same beans is a clear lesson in mouthfeel.

### Weeks 3–4: one coffee, two roasts

If a roaster sells the same coffee at two roast levels, or two roasters sell the same origin at different roasts, taste them side by side. Hoffmann describes roasting as a trade-off between three curves: acidity falls the longer a coffee is roasted, bitterness rises, and sweetness peaks somewhere in the middle. In his buying guide he puts it simply: the lighter the roast, the more acidity; the darker, the more bitterness and the less acidity. Researchers at the University of Southern Denmark found that even across light and very light roasts, every acid they measured but one rose or fell steadily as the roast went darker: citric and malic acid fell, while acetic and phosphoric acid rose.

Write three words for each cup before you look anything up. Our guide to [roast levels](/blog/coffee-roast-levels-explained/) explains what changes and why.

### Weeks 5–6: origins side by side

Now taste origins against each other, ideally three in one session, all brewed the same way, or [cupped](/blog/how-to-cup-coffee-at-home/). Pick origins whose coffees are usually described very differently. Treat those descriptions as tendencies, not promises: how a coffee was processed often matters more than its country, and a natural Ethiopian can taste very different from a washed one.

### Weeks 7–8: blind, then triangles

Ask someone to brew two or three coffees you've tasted before and number the cups without telling you which is which. For each, write down your guess at the origin, the roast and the process, and a few words, before the answer comes out. Then move on to triangle tests.

## Triangle tests: is the difference real?

A triangle test asks one question: can you tell two coffees apart? It's a standard method in sensory science (ISO 4120): you get three cups, two of them the same, and you pick the odd one out. You have to choose, even if you're guessing.

Set it up with a helper. They brew coffee A in two cups and coffee B in one (or the other way round), shuffle the cups and mark the answer underneath. Taste each cup, pick the odd one, check, and repeat with a fresh set.

Guessing picks the odd cup one time in three, so a single right answer proves little. Over several rounds, this many right answers would happen by luck less than 1 time in 20 (calculated from the odds of guessing):

| Rounds | Right answers needed | Chance of that by guessing |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | 5 | About 2% |
| 10 | 7 | About 2% |
| 12 | 8 | About 2% |

Four right out of six happens by guessing about 10% of the time, so it's encouraging but not convincing.

The test tells you whether there's a difference, not how big it is or which coffee is better. ISO also notes it's less suited to products with strong, lingering flavors, which coffee can be, so rinse with water between cups and keep the rounds short. A progression that gets harder (our suggestion):

- **Two origins:** two coffees usually described very differently.
- **Two roasts:** the same coffee roasted lighter and darker.
- **Two grind settings:** the same coffee brewed one step apart on your grinder.
- **Two waters:** the same coffee and recipe, made with two different [water recipes](/blog/coffee-water-recipes-compared/).

## Build a reference kit

The lexicon's references are the most direct way to learn a word: smell or taste the reference, then look for the same thing in your coffee. Many are kitchen items. Among them:

- **Sweet:** brown sugar to smell, or two teaspoons dissolved in a cup of water to taste; a tablespoon of honey dissolved in 250 ml of hot water; two teaspoons of molasses in 250 ml of water.
- **Nutty and cocoa:** freshly roasted peanuts; a 90% cocoa dark chocolate.
- **Fruity:** the syrup from canned blueberries, to smell; applesauce, to taste.
- **Tea:** plain black tea, its leaves smelled with a splash of hot water over them and the tea tasted brewed.
- **Off notes, for smelling only:** a 2-inch square of cardboard soaked in half a cup of water; a few rubber bands; an adhesive bandage, which is the lexicon's reference for "medicinal".

Put each one in a small covered glass so the smells don't mix, as the lexicon does with snifters. The SCA's guide to the wheel adds one rule: references marked for aroma are for smelling and must never be eaten or drunk. Flavor references can be tasted.

The SCA's own descriptive assessment uses broader category examples, such as jasmine green tea for floral. Both the lexicon and the SCA also cite numbered vials from Jean Lenoir's Le Nez du Café aroma kit; it's an option, not something you need.

## Acids: learn the sourness, not the label

Start with the basic tastes, using the SCA's own references from its descriptive standard. Each is a solution in water; the amounts per liter are calculated:

- **Sour:** 0.04% citric acid, which is 0.4 g in a liter of water.
- **Sweet:** 0.52% sucrose (white sugar), 5.2 g in a liter.
- **Salty:** 0.20% table salt, 2 g in a liter.
- **Bitter:** a dark-roasted coffee, about Agtron #35.

A 0.1&nbsp;g scale makes the small amounts easy. Taste each next to plain water, then taste a coffee and look for the same sensation.

The lexicon goes further, with references for the sourness of citric, malic and acetic acid at set intensities. Its citric acid reference is 0.05% in water, its malic acid references are 0.5 and 1.0 g per liter, and its weakest acetic acid reference is 0.5%, which with an ordinary white vinegar labeled 5% acidity is about 1 part vinegar to 9 parts water (calculated). The lexicon has no phosphoric acid entry.

Be careful about what this teaches. In a 2023 study, researchers measured the acids in five light and very light roasted coffees from Brazil, Bolivia and Kenya and tested how much of each people could detect. For malic, acetic and lactic acid, the detection threshold was above the amounts in the coffees, and for phosphoric acid it was only just below; only citric acid was clearly detectable at the levels measured. When coffee experts tasted coffees with the acids added at their average measured levels, none of the acids was correctly recognized. The SCA's own guide to its new forms warns that organic acids make misleading references: a coffee with a so-called phosphoric profile may contain very little phosphoric acid, and acidity that reminds you of green apple is probably not malic acid. Use the solutions to learn how strong and what shape sourness can be, not to name the acid in your cup.

## Is it the coffee or the brew?

Sourness in a cup can come from the coffee or from a brew that didn't extract enough. The quickest way to find out is to brew the same coffee again with one change, or taste it next to a coffee you know well.

## Mistakes that slow you down

- **Too many coffees at once.** The SCA's cupping standard caps a session at six coffees, and WCR's panels usually taste four to six in a session of 1½ to 2 hours to avoid fatigue.
- **Tasting too hot.** The SCA starts tasting cupped coffee at about 158°F&nbsp;(70°C) and goes on as it cools; Hoffmann finds flavors easier to discern in warm coffee than in hot.
- **Talking first.** WCR's panels usually taste in silence, and the SCA notes that information about a coffee can change what tasters perceive. Write your notes before anyone says "blueberry".
- **Forcing a word.** The SCA's guide says you can stop at any tier of the wheel. "Fruity" that you're sure of is more useful than "jasmine" that you aren't.
- **Not writing it down.** Hoffmann's method ends with comparing your words with the roaster's, which only works if you wrote them.

## What Q graders train

The SCA calls the Q Grader the leading license for specialty coffee cuppers. For about 20 years the Coffee Quality Institute ran it as a six-day course of 20 exams over 9 modules, testing cupping, smell and taste, green-coffee defects, roast identification and matching pairs of coffee acids; its robusta course also tested triangulation. Since October 1, 2025 the SCA has run an evolved program built on its Coffee Value Assessment, and CQI is no longer involved. Our guide to [coffee scores](/blog/coffee-scoring-system-explained/) has the details. You don't need the license, but several of the exercises here come from the same toolbox.

## What to expect

No source we found says how quickly a home taster improves, and we won't guess. Hoffmann's encouragement is that describing coffee gets easier and easier, though even industry veterans are still working on it. The goal here is smaller than a license: knowing which coffees you like, why, and which words on a bag point to them.
