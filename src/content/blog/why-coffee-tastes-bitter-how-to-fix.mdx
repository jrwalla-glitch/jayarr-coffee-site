A good cup has some bitterness: Jessica Easto, in *Craft Coffee: A Manual*, describes a balanced cup as a pleasant mix of acidic, sweet and bitter flavours. The trouble starts when bitterness covers everything else, and the fix depends on where it comes from: no grind setting will fix a carafe that needs a scrub.

## Four kinds of bitter

| Where it comes from | What you might notice | First change |
|---|---|---|
| Over-extraction | A drying, harsh finish; the brew ran longer than the recipe | Grind one step coarser |
| The roast | Dark or oily beans; smoky, carbon-like notes, even when the brew ran fast | Slightly cooler water; a lighter roast next time |
| Too strong | Heavy and intense, but not drying | More water for the same coffee |
| Old oils in the equipment | A stale-oil, rancid edge, even with fresh beans | Wash the brewer, filter basket and carafe |

Two more are worth ruling out. A cup that's sour and bitter at once, hollow in the middle, usually points to uneven extraction (our guide to [channeling](/blog/fix-channeling-uneven-extraction/) covers it). And coffee that has cooled and been reheated can taste bitter whatever the recipe.

## Over-extraction: too fine, too long, too hot

Coffee's compounds don't all dissolve at once. Easto describes fruity acids as among the first to dissolve, browning sugars as taking a little longer, and what she calls dry distillates, the most roasted, burnt-leaning compounds with tobacco, smoky and carbon flavours, as the slowest. Even at low levels, she writes, they can mask the other flavours so the whole cup tastes bitter. Leave the water with the grounds too long and you get more of them.

Anything that speeds extraction or stretches it pushes that way: a finer grind, a longer brew, hotter water. The brew time is the easiest clue, measured against the recipe you followed:

- **Pour-over.** The Brew Guide's V60 recipe takes 3 to 4 minutes from the first pour to the end of the drawdown. Much slower than that, together with a bitter, drying cup, is a common sign the grind is too fine for that recipe, not a rule. Grind one step coarser.
- **Drip machine.** The machine sets its own brew time, so the clue is in the basket: the Grind Guide notes that a grind that's too fine can make it flood or overflow, and the coffee taste bitter.
- **French press.** The Brew Guide adapts Easto's eight-minute French press, and Fix My Coffee treats 4 to 8 minutes as on target. Easto notes that the fines that get through the mesh keep extracting, so pour all of it off the grounds as soon as you press.
- **Espresso.** The Brew Guide aims for 25 to 30 seconds for 18 g of coffee into a 36 g shot. A shot well past that at the same dose and yield is likely over-extracted; grind coarser. (Scott Rao's own range for a normale runs from 25 to 40 seconds, depending on the machine's pressure profile.)
- **Moka pot.** Take it off the heat as soon as it starts to sputter. Our [moka pot guide](/blog/moka-pot-brewing-guide/) covers the rest.

If a bitter cup ran on time, Fix My Coffee still suggests one step coarser first, then slightly cooler water as a separate change if it's still bitter. Our guide to [brewing temperature](/blog/whats-the-ideal-coffee-brewing-temperature/) has the Brew Guide's starting temperatures.

An average can also hide two extremes. Jonathan Gagné, an astrophysicist who studies brewing, explains that if some grounds extract much further than others, a cup can show a normal average extraction and still carry the bitter taste of the over-extracted part. More even grinding and pouring help; our [extraction yield guide](/blog/coffee-extraction-yield-explained/) explains the numbers.

## Bitterness from the roast

Roasting creates bitter compounds. Simone Blumberg, Oliver Frank and Thomas Hofmann, food chemists at the Technical University of Munich, measured the bitter compounds that roasting forms from the bean's chlorogenic acids. In their 2010 study, light to medium roasting mainly produced compounds with what they call a coffee-typical bitter taste, and more severe roasting broke those down into compounds with a harsh bitter taste, matching the change in the kind of bitterness found in sensory tests. Easto makes the practical point: darker roasts carry more dry distillates and have less sweetness left to balance them.

So a dark roast can taste bitter even when it's brewed well. If a dark roast tastes bitter after a fast brew, Fix My Coffee suggests slightly cooler water: for a V60, about 190°F&nbsp;(88°C), a little below the Brew Guide's dark-roast kettle of 196°F&nbsp;(91°C). The bigger change is a lighter roast. Our guide to [roast levels](/blog/coffee-roast-levels-explained/) explains what changes along the way.

## When the cup is just too strong

Strength and bitterness travel together. In a UC Davis study of drip coffee, Frost, Ristenpart and Guinard brewed one coffee at three roast levels across a grid of strengths and extractions, and bitterness changed with strength and extraction at all three roasts. A cup that tastes heavy and intense but not drying is often too concentrated rather than over-extracted.

The fix is the ratio: use more water for the same coffee, one step lighter (1:16 to 1:17), or add hot water to the cup. Our [ratio guide](/blog/coffee-to-water-ratio-guide/) has the amounts, and our guide to [strength and extraction](/blog/coffee-strength-vs-extraction/) explains why the two are different.

The opposite happens too. Easto points out that coffee can be weak and over-extracted at once, which she says describes typical diner coffee. Using less coffee would only make it thinner. The Extraction Map's moves for a bitter, thin cup are to grind coarser and use more coffee; make them one at a time, grind first.

## Old oils, stale beans and reheated coffee

**Clean the equipment.** The National Coffee Association advises washing your brewer's parts with hot, soapy water, and checking that there's no build-up of coffee oil, which it says can make future cups taste bitter. Easto says the same of thermal carafes: lingering oils quickly go rancid and give fresh coffee an off flavour. For a French press, Easto recommends a thorough wash after each use, with the plunger taken apart. For an espresso machine, follow the maker's cleaning and descaling instructions; our [espresso machine cleaning guide](/blog/espresso-machine-cleaning-maintenance/) covers the routine.

**Drink it fresh.** Easto writes that once coffee drops below about 175°F&nbsp;(79°C), its chemistry starts to change, giving increasingly sour or bitter flavours as it cools, and that reheating gives a sour, bitter, astringent, woody-tasting cup. Brew what you'll drink, and keep any extra in a preheated, insulated carafe rather than reheating it.

**Stale beans taste flat more than bitter.** James Hoffmann, in *The World Atlas of Coffee*, describes stale coffee as tasting flat, woody and vaguely of cardboard, and notes that darker roasts go stale faster. If your cup is dull rather than harsh, check the roast date; our [freshness guide](/blog/coffee-freshness-roast-to-stale/) covers the typical windows.

## Cold brew

Cold water dissolves coffee slowly. Easto explains that the compounds linked to overly bitter flavours dissolve last, so many of them stay undissolved even after 12 to 15 hours of cold brewing. The Brew Guide's cold brew steeps for 12 hours. If yours still tastes bitter, Fix My Coffee suggests grinding one step coarser or steeping a few hours less, one at a time, and a lighter roast if it stays bitter.
