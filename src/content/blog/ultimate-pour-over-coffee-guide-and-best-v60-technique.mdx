A V60 is a cone with one big hole, so it does almost nothing on its own: how fast and where you pour decides how the water moves through the coffee. That makes it the most rewarding pour-over to learn and the easiest to get wrong. This guide gives one complete recipe, then explains what each step is doing, so you can tell what to change when a cup misses.

## What you need

- **A V60 and its paper filters.** The cone's spiral ribs keep the wet paper off the wall so air and coffee can move.
- **A burr grinder.** Even particles extract evenly. A blade grinder makes dust and chunks in the same batch.
- **A scale with a timer.** Everything below is by weight.
- **A gooseneck kettle.** A thin, steady stream lets you pour where and as gently as you mean to.
- **A server or mug** to brew into.

## A complete V60 recipe

This is Jonathan Gagné's V60 method from his *Coffee ad Astra* blog, with his numbers. Gagné is an astrophysicist who studies coffee brewing; his recipe is a good teacher because each step has a stated reason.

- **Coffee:** 22 g, ground medium-fine (about the texture of table salt)
- **Water:** 352 g, a 1:16 ratio, brought to a boil
- **Time:** about 2 minutes of setup, then about 2½ to 3½ minutes from the first pour to the last drip
- **Makes:** about 310 g of coffee (the grounds hold back roughly 2 g of water per gram of coffee)

## What the bloom is for

The first small pour wets every particle so that the water that follows reaches all of the coffee at once. Freshly roasted coffee also gives off carbon dioxide as it wets, and that gas can hold water away from parts of the bed if it isn't let out first. Gagné uses three times the coffee's weight for the bloom, enough to soak every ground, and swirls straight after pouring so no dry pockets survive.

If the bed barely rises, the coffee may simply be older or very lightly roasted: darker and fresher coffees foam more. It isn't a reliable test of freshness on its own, but a dry patch after the bloom is always worth fixing with a swirl.

## Water temperature: the kettle is not the brew

The coffee in the cone, the slurry, is always cooler than the water in your kettle. The water loses heat to the air, the paper, the cone and the room-temperature grounds. Gagné reports that even when he brews with water straight off the boil, the slurry in his V60 "will hardly ever exceed 93°C" (199°F). It is easy to fall below 91°C (196°F), which can leave the cup under-extracted and sour.

That is why he brews with boiling water and keeps each pour generous: small, slow pours and a bed that drains between pours both let the slurry cool.

The Brew Guide starts at a lower kettle temperature, by roast:

| Roast | Brew Guide kettle temperature | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Light | 96°C (205°F) | Dense beans that extract slowly need the heat |
| Medium | 94°C (201°F) | The middle of the usual brewing range |
| Dark | 91°C (196°F) | Darker roasts extract easily and turn bitter sooner |

Both approaches work. If light-roast cups taste sour at 96°C, try Gagné's boiling water before you grind finer. The same strength at a different temperature also tastes different, so change one or the other, not both. Our [brewing temperature guide](/blog/whats-the-ideal-coffee-brewing-temperature/) goes further.

**The cone's material matters a little.** Gagné brews in the plastic V60. He notes that plastic holds heat in better than ceramic or glass, which take heat out of the first pours. A ceramic or glass V60 brews fine if you preheat it well with the rinse water. Ceramic gets hot to hold, which makes swirling awkward.

## Swirling, and why it beats stirring

A swirl is a small, gentle circle of the whole V60. Gagné credits it to the brewer Scott Rao, and it is often called the Rao spin. It does three things:
- it knocks dry grounds off the paper back into the slurry;
- it lets the bed settle flat;
- it closes any channels the pour has started.

A flat bed at the end of the brew, with no crater or high ring of grounds on the walls, is the visible sign the swirls are working.

A few habits keep water flowing evenly through the coffee rather than around it:

- **Pour into the coffee, not onto the paper.** Water that hits the filter can run down between the paper and the cone without passing through the grounds.
- **Keep the stream thin and steady.** A heavy pour digs a hole in the bed, and water follows the hole.
- **Don't let the bed run dry between pours.** It cools, and the next pour hits a compacted surface.

## Grind: the one dial to turn

For a V60, start medium-fine, about the texture of table salt. The Brew Guide's illustrative band for V60 is about 450–650 µm. A particle size only becomes a setting on a specific grinder, though. For example, Baratza's own chart puts the Virtuoso+ at setting 15 for a V60, and the [Grind Calibrator](/deep-brew/calibrator/?grinder=baratza-virtuoso-plus&method=pourover) shows the published settings for 21 grinders.

Then change the grind first, and only the grind, until the cup tastes right. It moves strength and extraction together, and you can repeat a grinder setting exactly. Gagné dials in the way Scott Rao suggests: grind finer until the cup turns drying and astringent, then go back one step.

Grind uniformity matters as much as the setting. A blade grinder's dust over-extracts and slows the drawdown while its boulders under-extract, and no pouring technique evens that out. Our guides to [manual grinders](/blog/top-10-best-manual-coffee-grinders/) and [electric grinders](/blog/top-5-best-electric-coffee-grinders/) compare burr grinders.

## Reading the cup

The draining time is a clue, not a verdict: the same time can taste great with one coffee and flat with another. Read it together with the taste, and change one thing.

| What you taste | Often seen with | One change to try |
|---|---|---|
| Sour, thin | A fast drawdown | Grind one step finer |
| Bitter, drying | A slow drawdown | Grind one step coarser |
| Sour and bitter at once, hollow | A crater or channels in the spent bed | Make sure the bloom wets everything; swirl after each pour |
| Flat, papery | Unrinsed filter or old coffee | Rinse the filter well; check the roast date |
| Too strong or too weak, but balanced | The ratio, not the grind | Change the ratio (1:15 stronger, 1:17 lighter) |

## Choosing your dripper: V60 or flat-bottom

The V60's cone and single large hole leave the flow rate to you, so pour speed and swirling change the cup. Flat-bottom drippers such as the Kalita Wave hold the coffee in an even layer and drain through small holes, so the brewer, not your pouring, sets more of the pace. That makes them more forgiving while you learn. The V60 rewards practice with more control.

For a hands-off cup, an immersion brewer like the [Clever Dripper](/blog/the-clever-dripper-better-coffee-than-pour-over/) steeps the coffee before it drains.

## Filters: white or brown

Rinse either kind. In Gagné's comparison, unbleached brown filters gave a "wet cardboard" taste, while bleached white filters gave less. After a thorough pre-rinse he could barely tell them apart. He advises against cloth filters.

Paper also changes what reaches the cup. It holds back most of the coffee oils, including cafestol and kahweol. These are the compounds behind the rise in LDL cholesterol from unfiltered coffee such as French press and boiled coffee (Urgert and Katan, 1997). Paper also catches fine particles, which is why a V60 cup tastes clean and light-bodied.
