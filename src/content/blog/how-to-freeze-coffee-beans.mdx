The old advice never to freeze coffee came from a real problem: a big bag going in and out of the freezer every morning. The cold was never the trouble. Moisture was. Seal the beans, portion them so you never reopen a cold bag, and the freezer becomes, in Gagné's words, one of the most powerful ways to keep coffee fresh for a long time.

Our April 2026 video shows the method, including vacuum-sealing a single dose. This page has since been checked against the sources, so where the two differ, follow the text.

## Why freezing works, and where it goes wrong

Cold slows staling. In his video on freezing coffee, Hoffmann explains that freezer temperatures slow oxidation and the loss of aromatic compounds, and that most home freezers run at about −4°F&nbsp;(−20°C), cold enough to slow staling "quite dramatically." Scott Rao, in *The Professional Barista's Handbook*, puts the oxidation rate in the freezer at about fifteen times lower than at room temperature, and adds that the coffee's oil congeals, which slows the movement of aromas.

The problem is moisture. Hoffmann points out that freezers are very dry places, because moisture comes out of the air as ice. Gagné explains in *The Physics of Filter Coffee* that every time the freezer door opens, moist air gets in and condenses on cold surfaces, forming ice crystals on anything that isn't properly sealed. Beans also stay cold for hours after they come out. If they meet room air while they're still cold, moisture condenses on them. That's harmless if you grind and brew them straight away, but Gagné warns it can lead to rapid degradation if the beans then sit in a bag with that extra humidity.

So the habit to avoid is the one Hoffmann describes: a big bag in the freezer that you take out every morning, scoop from and put back, letting humid air in each time. Everything below is about never doing that.

## When to freeze, and when not to bother

Freeze coffee you won't use soon. Our [storage guide](/blog/how-to-store-coffee-beans/) and the Freshness tool both say to freeze what you won't use within about two weeks. Hoffmann's rule is similar: freeze the excess if you've bought more than you'll use in the next week or two. A single retail bag you'll finish in a couple of weeks isn't worth freezing, he says, and freezing coffee that's already stale won't bring it back.

That makes the freezer most useful when you've bought more than you can drink in time: a kilo at once, or two or three bags of a seasonal coffee you'd like to keep drinking after it sells out.

When you freeze matters, because the freezer holds the beans where they are. The Freshness tool counts freezer days as paused, for up to a year: beans frozen on day 10 come out at day 10. Its usual best window is days 7 to 21 after roasting, and its "still good" stage runs to day 30. Gagné often leaves beans in the roaster's bag for a week or so before opening it, and splits them into small bags as soon as he opens it if he plans to keep them for more than a few weeks. So freeze within the first two or three weeks, ideally before the bag has been opened and resealed many times.

## Portions: two-week bags or single doses

Two approaches work, and they differ in how much effort and packaging you're willing to spend.

**Hoffmann's: one- to two-week portions.** Divide a large purchase into portions of about 250 to 350 g, enough for a week or two, and freeze those. Get as much air out as you can. He suggests freezer bags, freezer-proof Mason jars or ordinary retail bags.

**Gagné's: single doses.** Gagné suggests single-dosing beans as soon as a roaster's bag is first opened, into small containers with little headspace, so each dose is exposed to air and moisture only once. For coffee he wants to keep for several months, he vacuum-seals it, either as single doses or in larger bags, and freezes it.

Single doses mean you never open a cold container until you're about to grind. Weigh each one for the brew it's meant for:

- **Espresso:** 18 g, the Brew Guide's starting dose for a double shot.
- **One pour-over mug:** 15 to 20 g, enough for 240 to 320 g of water at the Brew Guide's 1:16 V60 ratio (calculated).
- **A larger brew:** 30 to 36 g; the Brew Guide's Chemex recipe uses 31 g with 500 g of water.

Label each portion with the coffee, the roast date and the dose. Six identical pouches are hard to tell apart in three months' time.

## Sealing: vacuum, bags and jars

The aim is the same whatever you use: as little air around the beans as possible, and a seal that holds at freezer temperatures.

**Vacuum sealing.** Hoffmann calls vacuum packing "probably the best possible way to do it," but finds it a little extreme because of the plastic waste, and wouldn't vacuum-seal individual portions unless the coffee was very expensive and the freezer very cold. Gagné uses it for coffee he keeps for months, with three cautions:

- **Use soft bags, not rigid containers.** In a soft bag, outside air pressure presses the bag against the beans; in a rigid one, a vacuum can draw aroma out of the beans over time.
- **Seal once.** Pulling the vacuum probably sucks out some aroma, so avoid vacuum-sealing the same beans more than once.
- **Expect a trade-off.** Over years with a relatively cheap vacuum device, he found it helped keep coffee for long periods but sometimes caused a noticeable loss of aroma, most of all with Ethiopian coffees. Chamber vacuum sealers pull a much stronger vacuum than cheaper FoodSaver-type machines.

I use an Inkbird automatic vacuum sealer. Most machines come with a roll of vacuum-bag material: cut pouches of about 4 by 5 in for an 18 g espresso dose, larger for filter doses.

**Bags and jars.** Hoffmann is happy with freezer bags or freezer-proof jars for two-week portions. Gagné is warier: he warns that ice crystals can form on anything not properly sealed, including most plastic containers and Ziploc bags. If you use bags, press out as much air as you can; if you use jars, choose a size the beans nearly fill, since Gagné's rule is a small headspace.

**Not the roaster's valve bag.** Gagné explains that the one-way valves on coffee bags aren't designed for the cold: many rely on a thin layer of oil whose properties change at low temperature. Silicone dome valves, like those on Weber Workshops' single-dose cellars, can work in the freezer.

**Hoffmann thaws sealed.** Take a portion out the night before, don't open it, and let it come to room temperature on the counter overnight. Opening it while the beans are cold lets condensation form on them.

**Gagné grinds frozen when he can.** He notes that grinding beans while they're still cold is thought to improve the taste, citing a 2016 study by Uman and colleagues. It likely produces slightly more fines (particles below 100&nbsp;µm), he says, but in his experience grinding frozen beans still seems to improve filter coffee, probably because more aroma survives the grinding.

That study, published in *Scientific Reports* by a team that included the chemist Christopher Hendon (then at the University of Bath and MIT) and Maxwell Colonna-Dashwood of the Bath café Colonna & Small's, kept 20 g of whole beans covered for two hours at 68°F&nbsp;(20°C), −2°F&nbsp;(−19°C, a freezer), −110°F&nbsp;(−79°C, dry ice) and −321°F&nbsp;(−196°C, liquid nitrogen), then ground them on a Mahlkönig EK43. The colder beans gave a narrower spread of particle sizes and a smaller average size. The authors note the minimal difference between dry ice and liquid nitrogen. They suggest the more uniform grind should waste less of the bean, and that cold grinding could potentially bring more aroma or flavor to the cup, but taste wasn't part of the study.

In practice, both methods work. Grinding from frozen skips the wait and may help the grind; thawing sealed suits anyone who'd rather grind beans at room temperature. Either way, the beans don't meet room air while they're cold and then go back into storage.

## Can you refreeze?

The sources differ on how strict to be. Rao's rule in *The Professional Barista's Handbook* is never to let beans be defrosted and refrozen, and the Freshness tool says not to refreeze either. Gagné writes that freezing likely doesn't damage the internal structure of the beans, and that he isn't aware of any data showing that repeated freezing cycles harm coffee. What all of them guard against is moisture: beans exposed to room air while cold pick up condensation. Portion the coffee so that you never need to refreeze it, and the question doesn't come up.

## How long frozen beans keep

Gagné writes that freezing, done properly, can preserve coffee freshness for up to a year or even more. The Freshness tool follows that: it pauses the clock for up to a year in the freezer and counts the days again after that.

Rao has two data points. In *The Professional Barista's Handbook* (2008), he describes finding Kenya AA beans he had roasted six years earlier in his mother's freezer, brewing them, and finding them "really good." He adds that this doesn't mean they wouldn't have tasted better six years earlier, only that freezing works. In a 2023 blog post, he writes that freezing vacuum-sealed beans slows aging so much that it would probably take months or years for them to reach the condition of beans rested for a week or two at room temperature.

Freeze whole beans rather than ground coffee. Ground coffee releases much of its trapped gas within minutes of grinding, and its aromas go the same way; our [freshness timeline](/blog/coffee-freshness-roast-to-stale/) has the measurements.
