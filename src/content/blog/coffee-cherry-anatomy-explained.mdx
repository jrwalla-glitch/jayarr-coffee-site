A coffee bean isn't a bean. It's the seed of a fruit, a cherry about the size of a small grape, and most of the cherry's volume is the seed itself, wrapped in a thin layer of fruit. Knowing the layers explains what the words on a bag mean: "washed", "natural" and "honey" describe what happened to them.

| Layer | What it is | In processing |
|---|---|---|
| Skin (exocarp) | The outer peel | Pulped off in washed and honey coffees; dried on in naturals |
| Pulp (outer mesocarp) | Sugary fruit flesh | Removed with the skin, or dried on |
| Mucilage (inner mesocarp) | Sticky, pectin-rich inner flesh | Washed off, partly or wholly left on (honey), or dried on (natural) |
| Parchment (endocarp) | A hard hull around each seed | Kept on through drying; hulled off before export |
| Silver skin (perisperm remnant) | A thin film on the seed | Flakes off as chaff in roasting |
| Seed (endosperm and embryo) | The bean | Roasted, ground and brewed |

## The skin (exocarp)

The skin stays green for most of the fruit's development. As the cherry ripens, the chlorophyll disappears and red pigments (anthocyanins) build up, so the skin turns briefly yellow and then red. Some arabica cultivars ripen yellow instead, and Hoffmann notes that a cross between a yellow-fruited and a red-fruited tree can give orange fruit.

Color matters at harvest because ripeness is tied to the sugar in the fruit. Hoffmann explains that red fruit is easier to pick ripe, because it passes through a yellow stage before it turns red, while a yellow cherry gives the picker less to go on.

Dried, the skin and pulp become cascara, which is brewed like a tea. In January 2022 the European Union authorized dried coffee cherry pulp as a traditional food from a third country, citing its history of food use in Yemen, Ethiopia and Bolivia. Our [cascara guide](/blog/coffee-vs-coffee-tea-taste-test/) covers how it tastes and how to brew it.

## The pulp and mucilage (mesocarp)

Under the skin is the mesocarp, which De Castro and Marraccini call the "true pulp": a layer about 0.5 to 2 mm thick, rich in sugars (sucrose and the reducing sugars) and water. Its inner part is the mucilage, the sticky layer that clings to the parchment. Hoffmann describes ripe coffee fruit as surprisingly delicious, sweet like honeydew melon with a little acidity, though not very juicy and hard to separate from the seeds.

The mucilage is the layer that processing argues about. Hoffmann explains that the fruit flesh contains a lot of pectin and is firmly attached to the seed, which is why washed coffees go through a fermentation tank: it breaks the flesh down enough to be washed away. The other methods leave some or all of it on:

- **[Washed](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/):** skin and most of the flesh come off in a depulper; fermentation and washing remove the rest before drying.
- **Natural:** the whole cherry dries intact, and the dried husk of skin and fruit is removed afterward.
- **[Honey](/blog/honey-process-colors-explained/):** the skin and pulp come off, and some or all of the mucilage is left on the seed as it dries. Yellow, red and black honey aren't a standard measure: the importer Café Imports reports that some mills set the color by how much mucilage they remove, while others leave all of it on and change the drying.

What happens in the fruit can reach the seed. In a 2017 case study of arabica processed wet and dry, De Bruyn and colleagues found that lactic acid bacteria dominated wet processing, and several of their products formed in the mucilage, including lactic acid, acetic acid and mannitol, were also found in the bean's endosperm. Dry processing was dominated by acetic acid bacteria and yeasts, with their products building up in the drying outer layers. Both methods changed the composition of the finished green beans.

## The parchment (endocarp)

Each seed sits in its own hull, the parchment. Botanically it's the endocarp: a hard, lignified layer, around 150 µm thick in the measurements De Castro and Marraccini cite. One proposed purpose is to protect the seed from the digestive enzymes of the animals that eat the fruit.

For a producer, the parchment is packaging. Hoffmann describes freshly picked beans at around 60 percent moisture being dried to about 11 to 12 percent, still in their parchment, and the parchment staying on through storage until the coffee is hulled at a dry mill, as late as possible before shipping. Traditionally the coffee rests in parchment for 30 to 60 days first, a step called *reposo*.

The exception is Indonesia's wet-hulled process, *giling basah*. There the coffee is dried only to 30 to 35 percent moisture, hulled out of its parchment, then dried again. Hoffmann writes that this gives the beans a deep swamp-green color, and that the cups tend to have lower acidity and more body, with flavors such as wood, earth, spice, tobacco and leather that divide the industry. ## The silver skin

Inside the parchment, a thin film covers the seed: the silver skin. It's what's left of the perisperm, the tissue that fills the young seed before the endosperm grows and takes its place. At maturity it's a pellicle about 70 µm thick. Older writers called the perisperm the "integument" or "spermoderm", which is why you'll see those names too.

Most of it comes off in the roaster. Hoffmann describes the beans expanding early in the roast as their thin papery skins flake off as chaff, which the roaster's airflow carries away to be collected.

## The seed (the "bean")

The bean is the endosperm, a storage tissue. As the cherry ripens, it fills with storage proteins, sucrose and complex polysaccharides and hardens; those reserves are what the embryo inside it would use to germinate. Hoffmann describes what happens when a bean is planted: the shoot lifts the bean out of the soil, and these seedlings, which look like a coffee bean on a thin green stem, are called "soldiers".

Most cherries hold two seeds that face each other, which is why each bean is flat on one side. When only one seed develops, it grows round: a peaberry. Hoffmann puts peaberries at around 5 percent of the crop, and notes that some buyers believe they have special qualities or roast differently; it isn't settled that they taste better.

The seed holds the raw materials that roasting turns into flavor:

- **Sucrose:** about 6 to 8.5 percent of arabica beans and 0.9 to 4.9 percent of robusta, in the figures De Castro and Marraccini cite. They describe it as a major contributor to cup quality, because its breakdown in roasting produces a wide range of aroma and taste compounds. Our [roasting guide](/blog/how-is-coffee-roasted/) explains what happens.
- **Caffeine and chlorogenic acids:** both higher in robusta than in arabica, and responsible for bitterness in the cup. Robusta has about twice the caffeine of arabica, Hoffmann notes; our [arabica vs robusta guide](/blog/arabica-vs-robusta-differences/) compares them.
- **Coffee oil:** it carries cafestol and kahweol, the compounds behind unfiltered coffee's effect on LDL cholesterol. Paper filters hold back most of them (Urgert and Katan, 1997).

Brewing dissolves only part of a roasted bean. Jonathan Gagné notes that average extraction can't go above about 30 percent, because the rest doesn't dissolve in water; our [extraction yield guide](/blog/coffee-extraction-yield-explained/) explains what that means for your cup.

## From cherry to cup

A bag's processing note is a record of which layers were on the seed while it dried, and when the parchment came off:

| Process | Fruit on the seed while it dries | Parchment removed |
|---|---|---|
| Washed | None: skin, pulp and mucilage removed first | After drying, at the dry mill |
| Honey | Some or all of the mucilage | After drying, at the dry mill |
| Natural | The whole fruit | With the dried husk, after drying |
| Wet-hulled | Skin and pulp removed first | Part-way through drying, at 30–35% moisture |

Each method tends to push the cup in a direction: Hoffmann finds washed coffees tend toward more acidity and a cleaner cup, naturals often toward fruit, and wet-hulled coffees toward body and earthy flavors. Those are tendencies, not promises; our [processing guide](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/) explains each method.
