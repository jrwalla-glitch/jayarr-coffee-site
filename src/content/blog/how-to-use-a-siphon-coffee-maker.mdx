A siphon, also called a vacuum pot, is two glass chambers stacked on a stand over a flame or heater. Heat pushes the water up from the bottom bulb into the top chamber, where it steeps with the coffee; take the heat away and the brewed coffee drains back down through a filter, leaving the grounds behind. Jessica Easto, in *Craft Coffee*, calls it an immersion method, but quite unlike the others, and one of the most consistent ways to make coffee, since the device does most of the work.

It's an old idea. Easto traces it to a patent filed in Berlin in 1830 by S. Loeff, and to a Frenchwoman, Marie Fanny Amelne Massot, who improved and patented the design in the 1840s as Mme. Vassieux. The first siphons made and sold in the United States, from 1910, were called the Silex. Today, Easto writes, many come from Hario, Yama and Bodum.

## How a siphon works

Everything happens because the bottom bulb is sealed.

1. **Up.** As the water in the bulb heats, the air and steam above it expand. Hario's manual puts it simply: "The heat causes the air to expand and pushes the water into the upper bowl." The tube doesn't reach the bottom of the bulb, so a little water always stays behind.
2. **Steep.** The water in the top chamber looks as if it's boiling. Easto explains that it isn't: the bubbling is air coming up through the tube, which keeps the slurry moving.
3. **Down.** When you take the heat away, the bulb cools, its steam condenses back to water and the pressure inside drops below the air pressure on the coffee above. That difference pushes the coffee down through the filter. It's the low pressure left in the bulb that gives the vacuum pot its name.

The heat source stays on while the coffee steeps, which is what sets a siphon apart. In most brewers the coffee cools as it brews: Jonathan Gagné finds a V60 loses about 5°C (9°F) the moment the water lands. In *The Physics of Filter Coffee* he notes that a siphon is one of the few brewers that can hold the slurry above 205°F&nbsp;(96°C), because the heat makes up for what's lost, and that brewing that hot has, in his experience, given acrid or bitter cups. That's why Easto turns the heat to its lowest once the water has risen and brews at 202°F&nbsp;(94°C), and why Hario's manual warns against overheating the nearly empty bulb.

Easto's 202°F sits inside the 195–205°F&nbsp;(91–96°C) range Scott Rao gives for drip and French press brewing in *The Professional Barista's Handbook*; his book doesn't cover siphons.

## A complete siphon recipe

This is the Three-Cup Siphon Method from Jessica Easto's *Craft Coffee: A Manual*, which she adapted from the siphon method of Blue Bottle Coffee in San Francisco. You'll need a thermometer.

- **Coffee:** 22 g, ground medium-fine (15 on a Baratza Virtuoso, in Easto's book)
- **Water:** 300 g, about 1:14 (22 to 300 is 1:13.6, calculated). Easto suggests starting with water boiled in a kettle: the burner will get there eventually, but it takes much longer.
- **Temperature:** 202°F&nbsp;(94°C) in the top chamber when the coffee goes in, with the heat at its lowest
- **Time:** about 5 minutes to prepare the filter and set up, a few minutes for the water to rise, then 1:55 from adding the coffee to the last of the drawdown; allow about 10 minutes in all
- **Makes:** about 255 g, or about 8½ US fl oz (255 ml). That's calculated: 300 g of water, less about 44 g held back by the grounds at roughly 2 g per gram of coffee. Easto lists the recipe as making 300 g, the water you start with.

## The maker's way: Hario's method

Hario's manual for its Technica siphon is simpler. It uses a level spoonful, about 10 g, of medium-ground coffee and 120 ml of water per cup, about 1:12 (calculated). The coffee goes in first. Once the water has risen, you stir gently and leave it on the heat for another 40 to 60 seconds, then move the siphon off the heat and let the coffee drain. Where your siphon's manual differs from Easto's recipe, especially on heat sources, follow the manual.

## Grind

Easto grinds medium-fine for the siphon, 15 on a Baratza Virtuoso, which sits between the settings she gives for her two V60 recipes (12 and 16). Hario's manual says medium, "not too fine," and our 2020 video uses a medium grind.

## Heat sources and safe handling

A siphon is hot glass over an open flame or a heater, so the maker's rules matter. From Hario's Technica manual:

- **Use an approved heat source.** Hario lists only an alcohol burner, a "pie-torch" burner, a halogen heater or a gas table made for siphons. Over a gas stove, it says to keep the flame low, so the stand and glass don't overheat.
- **Dry the glass first.** Wipe any water off the outside before heating.
- **Keep your face and hands away from the bulb** while it heats: hot water can spurt out.
- **Mind the burner.** Use only fuel-type alcohol, never gel alcohol, gasoline, benzene or kerosene. Don't move or refill the burner while it's lit, and put it out by covering it with its cap.
- **Hot parts.** The stand conducts heat, so carry it with a glove or a thick cloth.
- **No sudden cold.** Don't touch the hot glass with a wet cloth or set it on a wet surface until it has cooled; the sudden change in temperature can break it.
- **Check for damage.** Don't use glass with a crack or chip, and keep the siphon away from children.

Stovetop siphons work differently. Easto recommends a heat dispersion plate between a stovetop model and the burner. Whatever you own, the manual for that model decides which heat sources are safe.

## Filters: cloth, paper or metal

Most siphons come with a small round cloth filter on a chain. Our sources disagree about what it does to the cup:

- Scott Rao, in *The Professional Barista's Handbook*, generalizes that cloth filters give a lot of body and moderate clarity, paper the least body and the most clarity, and metal a great deal of body with poor clarity.
- Easto writes that cloth traps solids and oils almost as well as paper, and that she personally can't tell the difference.
- Gagné finds cloth filters give fast, cloudy brews, much like metal ones.
- Hario sells its Next siphon with both, and says the cloth gives a cleaner, lighter-bodied cup and the stainless steel filter a fuller mouthfeel.

They agree that cloth needs care. Boil a new filter before you first use it. After brewing, rinse it well (Hario suggests a toothbrush) and store it in clean water in the fridge, changing the water regularly. Easto boils hers every so often too; neglect it, she warns, and "your coffee will taste like a dirty sock."

Gagné goes further. Cloth absorbs coffee oils, which soon turn rancid, and a filter that isn't cleaned and dried quickly can grow bacteria or fungi. He recommends paper filters for the siphon, which need an adapter sold separately. If you stick with cloth, his routine is to keep a dozen, wash each straight after use, freeze it in a bag, then boil the batch with an odorless detergent, and two or more times again in clean water, before hanging them to dry. He admits it's impractical.

## When the cup isn't right

On Easto's recipe, these are common signs, not rules:

- **Bitter or harsh.** Check that the heat was at its lowest once the water rose; a slurry kept above 205°F&nbsp;(96°C) can taste acrid, in Gagné's experience. Then try a grind one step coarser.
- **Sour or thin.** Grind one step finer, and give the coffee the full 1:20 before the heat goes off.
- **Slow to drain, or a stalled drawdown.** The grind may be too fine; Hario's "not too fine" is the warning.
- **Musty, stale or rancid.** That's usually the cloth filter. Boil it, or replace it.
- **Water boiling over, or bubbling but not rising.** Hario's fix: take the heat away and let the water cool, refit the top chamber, and reheat with the chamber tilted so its filter chain makes small bubbles.

Change one thing at a time, so you know which one helped.
