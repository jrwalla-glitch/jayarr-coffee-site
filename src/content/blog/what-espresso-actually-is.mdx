If you don't make espresso, the routine looks odd. Someone weighs 18 grams of coffee, grinds it into a small metal basket, stirs it with needles, tamps it flat, starts a timer and stops the shot when a scale reads a number. Most of that fuss is control. Espresso pushes a little water through a lot of coffee in well under a minute, so small changes show up in the cup.

## A working definition

Hoffmann defines espresso broadly: a small, strong drink brewed from finely ground coffee under high water pressure, with crema on top, usually at about 1 part coffee to 2 parts drink by weight. He prefers an open definition to a strict one, and the published references show why. They describe different drinks:

| Reference | Coffee → in the cup | Shot time |
|---|---|---|
| Certified Italian Espresso (INEI, 2006) | 7&nbsp;g, ±0.5 → 25&nbsp;ml, ±2.5, crema included | 20–&#8288;30&nbsp;s |
| Scott Rao's espresso *normale* (2013) | 14–&#8288;22&nbsp;g, matched to the basket → 1.5 to 2 times the dose, by weight | 25–&#8288;40&nbsp;s |
| Our Brew Guide's starting double | 18&nbsp;g → 36&nbsp;g | 25–&#8288;30&nbsp;s |

For pressure and water, INEI gives 9&nbsp;bar, ±1, with the water leaving the machine at 187–&#8288;194°F&nbsp;(86–&#8288;90°C); Rao gives 196–&#8288;203°F&nbsp;(91–&#8288;95°C) at the group head; and our Brew Guide starts at 199°F&nbsp;(93°C) for a medium roast.

The Italian single is a small shot from about 7 g of coffee. The modern specialty double uses more than twice that, and it is measured by weight rather than volume. Neither is wrong. What they share is the method: hot water forced through a tightly packed bed of fine coffee, fast enough to stay small and concentrated.

## Crema: what it does and doesn't tell you

Crema is the foam on top. Hoffmann explains it this way: water under high pressure can hold more of the carbon dioxide that roasting leaves in the beans, and when the coffee reaches normal air pressure on its way to the cup, the gas comes out as tiny bubbles.

By his account, crema tells you two things. It tells you roughly how fresh the coffee is, because older coffee holds less gas and makes less foam. And its color tells you how strong the shot is, since crema is just a foam of the liquid underneath. It can't tell you whether the coffee is good, well roasted or brewed on clean equipment.

Plenty of things other than quality make more crema. Rao lists fresher beans, grinding just before the shot, a bottomless portafilter and adding robusta. Pendergrast notes that early espresso bars found cheap robusta blends made a richer crema. A thick head is not a score.

## Why espresso is weighed, not measured by volume

Crema makes volume unreliable. Rao gives an example: a 30 g shot from beans roasted the day before can fill almost 60 mL, while the same weight from beans 15 days old fills just over 30 mL. That's why modern recipes give the shot in grams.

The timer matters for a related reason. With the dose and the yield fixed, the time tells you how hard the water had to work to get through. If the same recipe suddenly runs much faster or slower than usual, something changed: the grind, the dose, the beans or the prep.

## Where espresso came from

The first espresso machines used trapped steam to push water through coffee ground finer than gravity alone could drain. Hoffmann notes that they made ordinary-strength coffee faster, but steam alone can't safely give much pressure. Pendergrast credits Luigi Bezzera with the first commercial espresso machine in 1901; Desiderio Pavoni bought his patent and built steam machines that spread through European cafés by the 1930s.

The change that made modern espresso came from Achille Gaggia. Gaggia's own history records a 1938 patent for a system that used hot water under pressure instead of steam, and a second patent, in 1947, for a lever-piston mechanism: the operator's lever loads a spring, and the spring drives the piston. (Hoffmann and Pendergrast date Gaggia's spring invention to 1945.) Hoffmann describes the result as a sudden jump in pressure that allowed a much finer grind and a smaller, stronger, well-extracted cup, topped with what Gaggia's company called *crema naturale*.

## The puck is the resistance

Rao describes espresso as pressurised hot water percolating through a tightly packed bed of fine coffee, the puck. How fast it flows depends mainly on three things: the pressure, the weight of coffee and the fineness of the grind. More pressure raises the flow only up to a point. Beyond it, Rao writes, pressure compacts the bed more than it pushes water through, and he puts the peak somewhere between 7 and 9 bar at the group head.

So the machine supplies hot water and force, and the puck decides what that force becomes. Hoffmann compares it to pouring water through sand and through pebbles: the finer the coffee, the harder it is for the water to pass, and the slower the shot.

Water also takes the easiest route. Rao explains that a channel, a path of fast flow through a weak spot, over-extracts the coffee along it and dilutes the shot, while the denser parts of the bed are under-extracted. That's how one shot can taste sour and bitter at once. A level, evenly dense bed that seals against the basket wall gives the water fewer easy routes; our guide to [channeling](/blog/fix-channeling-uneven-extraction/) covers the fixes.

Tamping hard isn't the answer. Rao notes that some or all of the tamping pressure is released as soon as the grounds get wet, and that the pump's push dwarfs a barista's. His footnote calculates it: 9 bar is about 130.5 psi, and over the 4.09 square inches of a 58 mm basket that's about 534 lb, against roughly 50 lb from a firm tamp. Our [tamping guide](/blog/tamping-pressure-doesnt-matter-myth/) goes further.

## Why the grinder matters so much

Rao calls the grinder the most important piece of equipment in an espresso bar, and he advises putting a grinder ahead of a machine when you budget. The reason is in the puck.

A grinder never makes one particle size. At espresso settings it makes a spread with two peaks: a great many tiny fines, which add up to little weight, and a larger peak of particles near the intended size. In a 2024 study, Smrke, Eiermann and Yeretzian added measured amounts of fines to espresso grounds and found that, in practical terms, the fines mainly changed how easily water could pass through the bed. Together with the main particle size, the share of fines predicted the shot time.

Cameron and colleagues (2020) found the other side of it. As they ground finer, extraction first rose, then fell and became more variable, which they read as water flowing unevenly through parts of the bed. Rao adds that dull burrs make more fines and more large pieces at once, which slows the shot and lowers extraction.

The machine still matters: it has to hold the temperature and pressure steady. But a better machine can't make uneven grounds behave. More of the [grinder's role](/blog/why-grinder-matters-more-than-machine/) is in our grinder guide.
