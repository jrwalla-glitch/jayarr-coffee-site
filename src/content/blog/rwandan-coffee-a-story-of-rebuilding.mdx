Rwanda grows only a small share of the world's coffee, but its coffee has a story behind it: decades of low-grade bulk exports, an industry wrecked in 1994, then a deliberate rebuild around quality. Here's that story, then what's in the cup today and how to buy it.

## What Rwandan coffee tastes like

James Hoffmann, in *The World Atlas of Coffee*, writes that great Rwandan coffees often have a fruitiness and freshness reminiscent of red apples or red grapes, and that berry flavors and floral qualities are fairly common.

Our 2019 video describes a cup that can be full-bodied, with a heavy, syrupy mouthfeel; bright notes of lime with fruit or florals; a clean, crisp acidity on the higher side; and a caramel aftertaste. Depending on the lot, you might also find white chocolate, nuttiness, mandarin orange, cinnamon, clove, dates or melon. These are what a Rwandan coffee *can* taste like, not a promise for every bag:

| What to look for | What it can be like |
|---|---|
| Fruit | Red apple, red grape, berries, lime, mandarin |
| Acidity | Bright, clean and crisp |
| Body | Full and syrupy in some lots |
| Sweetness and finish | Caramel, sometimes white chocolate |
| Florals | Floral notes are common |

Importers and roasters describe it in similar terms. Coffee Origins quotes one importer's 2026 washed Rwandan offering as chocolate, stone fruit, citrus and tea, and in its sample of 21 Rwandan lots that roasters described from January to March 2026, orange, black tea and apple came up most often. Roast, processing and the washing station all change the cup, which is why the station's name on the bag tells you more than the country.

## From bulk coffee to washing stations

Hoffmann writes that German missionaries first brought it in 1904, planting the first trees at the Mibirizi mission, and that Rwanda didn't grow enough to export until 1917. After World War I, Belgium took over; in the 1930s, coffee became a compulsory crop for many producers. The Belgians strictly controlled exports and taxed growers heavily, which pushed Rwanda toward high volumes of low-quality coffee sold at low prices. There was little infrastructure for quality: not even a single washing station.

By the 1990s, coffee was Rwanda's most valuable export. Then the 1994 genocide, which claimed nearly a million lives, devastated the industry, at a time when world coffee prices were also very low.

The recovery made coffee a symbol of something hopeful, Hoffmann writes. With foreign aid and interest, the sector turned toward quality: washing stations were built, the government opened up the coffee trade, and specialty buyers took notice.

- **The first stations.** The rebuilding was under way by 2001: the Alliance for Coffee Excellence records that the Maraba cooperative (Abahuzamugambi ba kawa Maraba) built its Cyarumbo washing station that year, the first built by a cooperative. Hoffmann dates the first station to 2004, with support from the US Agency for International Development; the cooperative's record is earlier.
- **Training.** Hoffmann credits the PEARL project (Partnership for Enhancing Agriculture in Rwanda through Linkages), later SPREAD (Sustaining Partnerships to enhance Rural Enterprise and Agribusiness Development), with spreading knowledge and training young agronomists, with a focus on the Butare region.
- **Growth.** By Hoffmann's 2018 edition, about 300 washing stations were in operation.
- **Recognition.** The Alliance for Coffee Excellence lists Rwandan Cup of Excellence competitions in 2008, 2010 to 2015 and 2018. In 2008, the top lot, from Huye in the south, scored 92.07.

Hoffmann adds that high world prices around 2010 made quality harder to sustain, since even low-quality coffee was profitable then, but that Rwandan coffees had recently been excellent.

## Rwandan coffee today

The recovery story isn't the whole picture. Rwanda's coffee is grown by very small farms: the National Agricultural Export Development Board (NAEB) says about 400,000 smallholder farm families produce it, and Hoffmann reports an average of just 183 trees per producer. That's why Rwandan coffee is traced to a washing station, cooperative or farmer group rather than to a single farmer.

USDA's production figures, in 60 kg bags, all arabica:

| Marketing year | Production (bags) |
|---|---|
| 2021/22 | 270,000 |
| 2022/23 | 400,000 |
| 2023/24 | 280,000 |
| 2024/25 | 260,000 |
| 2025/26 | 350,000 |
| 2026/27 | 350,000 (forecast) |

That's about 0.2 percent of the 189.7 million bags USDA forecasts worldwide for 2026/27 (calculated).

Most of it is fully washed. The importer Café Imports lists washed, natural, honey and anaerobic lots, grades Rwandan coffee by screen size (High Grade is screen 16), and gives September to December as the usual arrival in the US. ## Where it grows

Hoffmann writes that coffee is grown across the whole country without specific geographic zones, and that roasters may name a district along with the washing station or farmer group. His tables group it into two regions:

| Region | Areas Hoffmann names | Altitude | Harvest |
|---|---|---|---|
| Southern and Western | Huye, Nyamagabe, and Nyamasheke on Lake Kivu | 1,700–2,200 m | March–June |
| Eastern | Ngoma, and Nyagatare in the far northeast | 1,300–1,900 m | March–June |

NAEB puts coffee in most provinces at under 1,900 m, below the top of Hoffmann's southern and western range (2,200 m); the sources differ. NAEB also says there's one crop a year: picking starts in late February on the shores of Lake Kivu and in early March elsewhere, and runs as late as August in some parts of the Northern Province. Café Imports' list adds that province (Rulindo) and names Lake Kivu washing stations such as Kabrizi and Kigeyo. The Cup of Excellence lots above came from both the south and the north: in 2008, the top five included lots from Huye, Rulindo and Gakenke.

## Varieties

NAEB describes Rwanda's coffee as mainly Bourbon-type arabica. These are varieties World Coffee Research (WCR) describes as common or important in Rwanda, with its ratings:

| Variety | What WCR says about it | Yield | Quality potential | Leaf rust and berry disease |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bourbon Mayaguez 139 | Bourbon line; in Rwanda since the early 1950s | Medium | Very Good | Susceptible to both |
| Bourbon Mayaguez 71 | Sister selection; very similar in the field | Medium | Good | Susceptible to both |
| Jackson 2/1257 | Bourbon-like; common in Rwanda and Burundi | High | Good | Susceptible to both |
| Mibirizi | Likely Rwanda's oldest; drought tolerant | Medium | Exceptional | Highly susceptible |

WCR says the two Bourbon Mayaguez selections are believed to have come from Central America by way of the USDA's collection at Mayaguez, Puerto Rico, and a collection at Mulungu in the Congo, before reaching Rwanda in the early 1950s.

The two sources disagree about Mibirizi. Hoffmann describes it as a natural variant of Bourbon that appeared at the mission. WCR says its origin is poorly documented, that it was probably introduced from Guatemala in the 1910s, and that recent genetic tests confirm it is related to the Typica group.

Every variety in that table is susceptible to coffee leaf rust and coffee berry disease, by WCR's ratings. Our [variety guide](/blog/coffee-varietal-guide/) explains where Bourbon and Typica come from.

## The potato defect

Rwandan coffee has one well-known risk. The potato taste defect makes a coffee smell like freshly peeled raw potato once it's roasted and ground. It isn't a health risk, but a single affected bean can spoil the cup it's in.

- **Where it's found.** Hoffmann's 2018 edition describes it in Rwandan and Burundian coffee. World Coffee Research's 2023 review finds it mainly in the Great Lakes region: Rwanda, Burundi, Congo and Uganda, and less often Tanzania and Kenya.
- **What causes it.** Hoffmann describes an unknown bacterium entering the cherry through damaged skin and producing the taint. WCR ties the defect to damage from the antestia bug and to two odor compounds, IPMP and IBMP. The exact mechanism isn't agreed, it says, but the compounds are thought to come from bacteria that colonize the damaged beans.
- **Why it's hard to catch.** Hoffmann writes that it can't be detected once processing is finished, and is hard to find even after roasting until an affected bean is ground. Producers fight it in the field, by controlling the bug, and at the washing station, by floating off damaged cherries and sorting out damaged beans; WCR notes that UV lights are used in Rwanda to find insect-damaged beans.

If you grind a dose that smells of raw potato, throw that dose away. WCR suggests grinding smaller amounts when you suspect the defect, and purging the grinder with other coffee afterward. The rest of the bag isn't contaminated: Hoffmann notes that finding it doesn't mean the whole bag is spoiled unless it has all been ground, which is one more reason to grind only what you brew.

## Buying and brewing Rwandan coffee

- **Look for the washing station or cooperative.** It's the most specific trace Rwanda offers, and stations' coffees differ.
- **Washed is the classic.** Naturals and honeys exist but are less common.
- **Check the harvest and roast dates.** Our [freshness guide](/blog/coffee-freshness-roast-to-stale/) explains what they tell you.
- **Taste it next to another origin.** Our guide to [single-origin coffee](/blog/what-is-single-origin-coffee/) shows how; a [Kenyan](/blog/kenyan-coffee-flavor-notes-and-best-coffees/) or [Ethiopian](/blog/ethiopian-coffee-flavors-and-varieties/) makes a good comparison.

A clean, fruity washed Rwandan suits a paper-filter pour-over; our [V60 guide](/blog/ultimate-pour-over-coffee-guide-and-best-v60-technique/) walks through a full recipe, and a [French press](/blog/how-to-use-a-french-press-the-most-amazing-coffee-youve-ever-had/) gives you more body.
