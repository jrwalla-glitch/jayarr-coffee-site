A micron (µm) is a thousandth of a millimeter. Jessica Easto, in *Craft Coffee: A Manual*, points out that there are no universal grind sizes: one grinder set to 14 might not produce the same size grounds as another set to 14, and measuring particle size takes equipment most people don't have. This page is the technical reference: what the numbers mean and where they come from. For the practical question of which grind to use for your brewer, see our [grind size guide](/blog/coffee-grind-size-guide-what-grind-for-every-brew-method/).

## The micron chart

These are the bands our Grind Guide and Brew Guide use, finest first, with the Brew Guide's recipe time for each brewer.

| Brewer | Texture | Band (µm) | Brew Guide time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Turkish coffee | Extra fine, like flour | 75–125 | No recipe yet |
| Espresso | Fine, like powdered sugar | 200–300 | 25–30 seconds |
| Moka pot | Medium-fine, like fine sand | 300–450 | 4–5 minutes |
| Phin | Fine, like sand | 300–450 | About 5 minutes |
| AeroPress | Medium-fine, like fine sand | 350–500 | 50–90 seconds |
| V60 | Medium-fine, like table salt | 450–650 | 3–4 minutes |
| Kalita Wave | Medium, like sand | 500–700 | 3–4 minutes |
| Chemex | Medium, like coarse sand | 550–750 | 3½–4½ minutes |
| Drip machine | Medium, like sand | 600–900 | 4–8 minutes |
| Cold brew | Coarse, like kosher salt | 800–1,100 | 12 hours |
| French press | Extra coarse, like coarse sea salt | 900–1,200 | 8 minutes |

The bands are where most of the coffee should sit. Turkish coffee's is the one that names its statistic: it's the average particle size in Türkiye's registered description of Turkish coffee (TÜRKPATENT, registration G8). The others don't say which statistic they are, and the phin's is our estimate, the moka pot's band, because Nguyen Coffee Supply likens the two grinds. No one band is a measurement of your grinder's output. The Brew Guide narrows each band by roast, putting light roasts in the finer half and dark roasts in the coarser half: for a V60, about 450 to 550&nbsp;µm for a light roast and 550 to 650&nbsp;µm for a dark one.

## What a micron number means

**A grind is a spread, not a size.** Scott Rao, in *Espresso Extraction: Measurement and Mastery*, explains that coffee beans fracture into a variety of shapes and sizes, and that grinding, particularly at espresso's fine settings, gives a two-peaked (bimodal) distribution: a small peak made up of a large number of fines that add up to little mass, and a large peak holding most of the coffee near the size you were aiming for.

**Percentiles describe the spread.** D10, D50 and D90 are the sizes below which 10%, 50% and 90% of the coffee falls. Jonathan Gagné, an astrophysicist who studies brewing, defines D10 this way: sort every particle from lightest to heaviest, and D10 is the size of the particle you reach once you've counted 10% of the dose's weight. D50 is the median.

**The weighting changes the answer.** Counting particles and weighing them give very different pictures. Gagné notes that on a plot by number the coarser, "nominal" peak can be impossible to see, because tiny fines vastly outnumber everything else, while a plot by volume shows it clearly; volume tracks weight if the coffee's density is even. A 2024 espresso study from the Coffee Excellence Center at the Zurich University of Applied Sciences, for example, reported each grind's volume-weighted median and its volume share of particles under 100&nbsp;µm.

**Fines are usually the particles under about 100&nbsp;µm.** That's the cut-off in that study, in Gagné's grinder analysis and in our Grind Guide.

## How grinds are measured

There are three common ways, and each reports its own kind of number.

- **Sieves.** Screens with known openings sort the grounds by size. The SCA's cupping protocol defines its grind this way: 70 to 75% of the grounds should pass through a US No. 20 sieve, which has 850&nbsp;µm openings, a grind it describes as slightly coarser than typical paper-filter drip. That's a point near the top of the spread, not a median: 850&nbsp;µm is the size that about 70 to 75% of the grounds are smaller than.
- **Laser diffraction.** An analyser sizes the particles from the way they scatter a laser beam. Uman and colleagues used this method in a 2016 study of coffee ground on a Mahlkönig EK43. Plotted by surface area, their grinds were clearly bimodal, because the fines carry most of the surface water can reach.
- **Image analysis.** A camera images the dispersed particles and sizes each one from its projected area. The Zurich study used this method.

Because the methods and weightings differ, compare two micron figures only when you know which statistic each one is and how it was measured.

## Why the fines matter

Fines slow the water down. In the Zurich study, Smrke, Eiermann and Yeretzian added sieved fines to espresso grounds and found that a larger share of fines lowered the bed's permeability, slowing the flow and lengthening the shot. Gagné has written that D10, rather than the median, drives an espresso puck's resistance, because fines have such a large effect on it. When he later analyzed 300 particle-size distributions from 24 espresso grinders, though, he found the dialed-in grinds didn't follow the rule from soil science that ties flow to the 10th percentile, and he suggested several reasons why. So fines clearly matter, but no single percentile yet predicts how a bed will flow.

Fines also extract fastest. A particle a tenth the size has about ten times the surface per gram (calculated for spheres). Rao, in his account, says fines rapidly over-extract, adding bitterness and astringency, while the largest pieces, the boulders, under-extract. That's why a narrower spread is easier to brew well, and why our [grind size guide](/blog/coffee-grind-size-guide-what-grind-for-every-brew-method/) and [extraction yield guide](/blog/coffee-extraction-yield-explained/) both come back to even grinding.

## Microns per click isn't grind size

Some makers publish how far the burr moves per click. 1Zpresso's J-Max manual says each click "moves the burr by 0.0088mm/8.8 microns", and its JX-Pro manual gives 40 clicks per turn and 0.5&nbsp;mm of burr movement per turn, which works out to 12.5&nbsp;µm per click (calculated). Those figures describe the burr's travel. They don't tell you the size of the grounds, for several reasons:

- **The grounds are a spread.** Even at one setting, a grinder makes fines as well as a coarser main peak, as above.
- **The same gap gives different grinds on different grinders.** In his analysis of espresso grinders, Gagné found that different grinders have very different positions for their coarse peak at dial-in, that changing the burr gap changes the amount of fines as well as the average size, and that a grinder's speed and feed rate can have a large effect on the distribution.
- **Zero isn't a fixed point.** 1Zpresso defines the JX-Pro's zero as where the handle starts to turn with resistance, not the tightest position, and warns that the J-Max's starting point can differ by up to 10 clicks either way depending on how firmly the nut and dial are tightened.

Makers that do quote particle sizes give a range, not a size per setting. Baratza's product sheet for the Virtuoso+ says its 40 settings cover a grind range of 250 to 1,200&nbsp;µm, as published by the maker, without saying which setting gives which size or which statistic the figures are. So don't multiply clicks by microns per click to get a particle size; use your grinder's own chart and your taste.

## From a micron band to your dial

Start from the maker's chart. Baratza's chart for the Virtuoso+, for example, suggests setting 8 for espresso, 12 for the AeroPress, 15 for a Hario V60, 18 for an automatic brewer, 20 for a Chemex and 28 for a French press, as published by the maker. Baratza adds that the setting will need adjusting for the recipe, the roast, the coffee and your taste.

The [Grind Calibrator](/deep-brew/calibrator/?grinder=baratza-virtuoso-plus&method=pourover) shows published starting settings for the grinders it covers, with each setting's source named.

Then adjust by taste, one step at a time. A few things move the right setting:

- **Roast.** The Brew Guide starts light roasts finer and dark roasts coarser within each band.
- **Batch size.** The Brew Guide warns that a pour-over batch much bigger than its recipe drains more slowly, and suggests grinding a touch coarser.
- **Burr wear.** Rao writes that as burrs dull they make more fines and more boulders.
- **Bean temperature.** Uman and colleagues found that grinding colder beans gave a narrower distribution and a smaller average size.
