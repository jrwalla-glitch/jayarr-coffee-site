Take a six-dollar latte, a round example price; what you pay depends on the city and the shop. This page works out what's physically in it, what those ingredients cost at published prices, and where published café accounts say the rest goes. Every price has a date and a source, and every per-cup figure is calculated, with the arithmetic shown.

## What's in a latte

Recipes vary, so these are the reference amounts this page uses:

- **Coffee:** a double shot. Our Brew Guide's espresso recipe, from Scott Rao's parameters, starts at 18 g of ground coffee for 36 g in the cup.
- **Milk:** a Spanish specialty recipe in our Coffee Drinks tool uses 40 g of espresso, close to the Brew Guide's 36 g, and 180 to 240 g of milk. An Italian latte from illy uses a single shot and 120 ml of whole milk, so the milk in a café latte varies a lot.

## What the coffee costs

Coffee is priced at several points on its way to your cup. Two have published, dated figures: green coffee on the world market, and ground coffee in US stores. We found no published, dated index of what cafés pay roasters for roasted beans, so this page doesn't guess it.

**Green coffee.** The ICO's composite indicator price, which blends arabica and robusta, averaged 262.18 US cents a pound in September 2026. Its arabica groups ranged from 290.19 cents (Brazilian naturals) to 356.15 cents (Colombian milds), and robustas averaged 167.49 cents.

Beans lose weight in roasting, so a roaster needs more green coffee than the roasted coffee it sells. An example from the Specialty Coffee Association of America, in a chapter Robert Thurston adapted for *Coffee: A Comprehensive Guide*, allows for an 18 percent "shrinkage loss" and calls such a loss typical, since some of the green beans' weight is water that escapes as steam; the roaster's cost example in Mark Pendergrast's *Uncommon Grounds* uses the same 18 percent. On that figure, 18 g of roasted coffee started as about 22 g of green coffee (18 ÷ 0.82, calculated). A pound is 453.6 g, so:

| Price basis, September 2026 (checked October 18, 2026) | Price per pound | Coffee in an 18 g double (calculated) |
|---|---|---|
| ICO composite, green | 262.18 cents | about 13 cents |
| ICO Brazilian naturals, green | 290.19 cents | about 14 cents |
| ICO Colombian milds, green | 356.15 cents | about 17 cents |
| ICO robustas, green | 167.49 cents | about 8 cents |
| BLS US average, ground coffee | $9.33 | about 37 cents |

The green rows are 22 g × the price per gram; the ground row is 18 g × the price per gram, with no roasting loss because the coffee is already roasted. That's the arithmetic behind the old "10 cents of coffee": it works only at green prices of about 207 cents a pound or less (calculated). At September 2026 prices, only the robusta indicator comes in below it. For why prices rose and where they are heading, see our [coffee price explainer](/blog/why-is-coffee-so-expensive-now-the-9-coffee-crisis/), which uses the same ICO and BLS series.

## What the milk costs

The US Bureau of Labor Statistics put a gallon of whole milk at $4.11 on average in September 2026 (checked October 18, 2026). The USDA weighs a quart of whole milk at 976 g, so a gallon is about 3,904 g (calculated). At that price, the Spanish recipe's 180 to 240 g of milk costs about 19 to 25 cents (calculated). A café buys milk in bulk and may pay a different price.

So at US retail prices the coffee and milk in a double-shot latte come to about 56 to 62 cents, about 9 to 10 percent of six dollars (calculated). Cups, lids and syrup aren't counted, because we found no dated public price for them.

## Where the rest goes

No public source itemizes one latte, but two published accounts show how a café's money divides.

**A café example (Specialty Coffee Association of America, 2010).** Thurston's chapter "Where Does the Money Go in the Coffee Supply Chain?" adapts an SCAA example of a shop selling 16-ounce brewed coffees, using certified Fair Trade organic beans. Working from its figures, the roasted beans were about 26 percent of the shop's sales, and labor about 20 percent, "the biggest single expense of all." The lease was about 7 percent, and the shop's net profit after taxes about 6 percent (all calculated from the example). Thurston adds that the retailers he interviewed said beans were usually less than 20 percent of their costs and that "everywhere labor is the greatest expense." The example is for brewed coffee, not lattes, and it is dated; treat the shares as an illustration.

**A chain's accounts (Starbucks, fiscal 2025).** Starbucks' annual report for the year to September 28, 2025, reports these shares of total net revenue:

- **Product and distribution costs:** 31.4 percent.
- **Store operating expenses:** 45.9 percent. The company attributes part of the year's rise to "additional labor."
- **Operating margin:** 7.9 percent, down from 15.0 percent the year before.

These cover the whole company, including its licensed stores and packaged-coffee business, not a latte on its own. On a six-dollar drink, a 7.9 percent margin would be about 47 cents (calculated).

## Plant milk

Starbucks used to charge extra for non-dairy milk. It announced on October 30, 2024 that, from November 7, customers at its company-operated stores in the US and Canada would no longer pay extra for non-dairy milk. Independent shops set their own prices. For how oat and dairy milk behave in a latte, see our [oat milk versus dairy guide](/blog/oat-milk-vs-dairy-in-coffee/).

## The farmer's share

The green-coffee prices above are world market prices for exported coffee. The farmer is paid part of that, after processing, transport and export costs, and we couldn't find a current, published figure for the share that reaches farms, so this page doesn't give one. Certification schemes such as Fairtrade set a minimum price as a floor for the years when the market falls; with market prices above that floor, as they have been, the market price applies. Our [coffee certifications guide](/blog/coffee-certifications-decoded/) has the current minimums and what each label checks.

## Is it cheaper at home?

For the ingredients, yes. At September 2026 US retail averages, the coffee and milk for a double-shot latte cost about 56 to 62 cents (calculated above). That leaves out the espresso machine, the grinder, energy and your time, which is what the café's price mostly pays for. If you want to try, our guide to [steaming milk for lattes](/blog/how-to-steam-milk-for-lattes/) covers the milk, and our [coffee-to-water ratio guide](/blog/coffee-to-water-ratio-guide/) covers the brewing numbers.
