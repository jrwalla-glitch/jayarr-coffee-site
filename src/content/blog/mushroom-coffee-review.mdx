This is a review of the published research, not a taste test. It looks at what one brand's label lists for a serving, then at the human studies of each mushroom: who was studied, how many, at what dose and for how long. Lab and animal studies are left out, because they can't tell you what a drink does in a person.

## What's in a serving

Harvard Health describes mushroom coffee as medicinal mushrooms, such as chaga, lion's mane, reishi and cordyceps, blended with coffee, cacao or tea. Take RYZE's label as an example. As published by the maker (checked October 18, 2026), a 6 g serving contains:

- **Organic instant coffee,** listed first;
- **a 2,000 mg mushroom blend** of six species: cordyceps, reishi, turkey tail, king trumpet, shiitake and lion's mane;
- **a prebiotic fiber blend** (acacia, inulin and tapioca fiber);
- **about 50 mg of caffeine.**

The label doesn't say how much of each mushroom is in the blend. If it were split evenly, each species would be about 333 mg (calculated), but that's an assumption, and the real split could be quite different.

The caffeine comes from the coffee. RYZE describes its blend as having "less than half the caffeine of regular coffee," and our Caffeine Calculator agrees: it estimates an 8 fl oz (237 ml) drip coffee at the Brew Guide's dose at about 105–195 mg, so a 50 mg serving is roughly a quarter to a half of that (calculated). If less caffeine is what you're after, a smaller cup, a half-caf blend or [decaf](/blog/how-is-coffee-decaffeinated/) does the same job.

## What the human studies tested

Here are the trials for the mushrooms that mushroom coffees are sold on, with their designs and sizes. None of them gave people mushroom coffee.

| Study (mushroom) | Who | Dose and length | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mori, 2009 (lion's mane) | 30 adults aged 50–80 with mild cognitive impairment | 3 g of tablets a day, 16 weeks | Better than placebo; scores fell after stopping |
| Docherty, 2023 (lion's mane) | 41 healthy adults aged 18–45 | 1.8 g a day, 28 days | Mixed: faster on one task, poorer word recall than placebo |
| Surendran, 2025 (lion's mane) | 18 healthy adults aged 18–35 | One 3 g dose of extract | No overall effect |
| Hirsch, 2017 (cordyceps blend) | 28 young adults; 10 went on to week 3 | 4 g a day, 1 to 3 weeks | No significant change at 1 week; aerobic capacity up at 3 weeks in the small group |
| Chen, 2010 (Cs-4) | 20 healthy adults aged 50–75 | About 1 g a day, 12 weeks | Two exercise thresholds up; maximal aerobic capacity unchanged |
| Parcell, 2004 (Cs-4) | 22 trained male cyclists | 3 g a day, 5 weeks | No effect |

All six compared the mushroom with a placebo. Mori, Docherty, Surendran and Hirsch describe randomized, double-blind designs; Chen's abstract says double-blind but not randomized, and Parcell's abstract doesn't describe blinding or how people were assigned.

### Lion's mane

Lion's mane is marketed for focus. In the study with the clearest positive result, Mori and colleagues gave 30 older adults with mild cognitive impairment, 15 per group, four 250 mg tablets three times a day. That's 3 g of tablets, or about 2.9 g of dried mushroom (calculated from the tablets' 96%). The lion's mane group scored higher than the placebo group at weeks 8, 12 and 16, and its scores fell significantly four weeks after the tablets stopped.

In healthy young adults the picture is thinner. Docherty and colleagues gave 41 people 1.8 g a day for 28 days. After the first dose, the lion's mane group was faster on the Stroop task but less accurate on immediate word recall; after 28 days, the placebo group did better on delayed word recall, and the lion's mane group showed a trend toward lower stress that just missed significance (p = 0.051). The authors call their positive findings tentative, given the small sample; Sempera Organics, the capsules' maker, supplied them. Surendran and colleagues' single 3 g dose of a concentrated extract, in 18 people, made no overall difference to cognition or mood.

### Cordyceps

Cordyceps is marketed for energy and endurance, and its trials measure exercise. Hirsch and colleagues gave 28 young adults 4 g a day of a mushroom blend containing *Cordyceps militaris*, or a placebo, for a week. Their main analysis found no significant change, though a confidence-interval analysis showed longer time to exhaustion on the blend. Ten of them carried on for two more weeks, and in that small group maximal oxygen uptake rose by 4.8 ml/kg/min on the blend against 0.9 on placebo. Because the product was a blend, the result can't be credited to cordyceps alone.

The other two trials used Cs-4, a fermented product of *Cordyceps sinensis*. In 20 healthy adults aged 50 to 75, about 1 g a day for 12 weeks (calculated) raised two exercise thresholds but didn't change maximal aerobic capacity (Chen et al., 2010). In 22 trained cyclists, 3 g a day for five weeks made no difference to aerobic capacity or time-trial performance (Parcell et al., 2004).

### Chaga

Chaga is marketed for its antioxidants. Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center's herb database says that "the safety and efficacy of chaga have yet to be evaluated in clinical studies." What is on record in people is a handful of case reports of kidney damage, such as a 72-year-old woman with liver cancer who had taken 4 to 5 teaspoons of chaga powder a day for six months (Kikuchi et al., 2014), and a 69-year-old man who had taken 10 to 15 g a day, with vitamin C, for three months (Kwon et al., 2022). Both developed oxalate nephropathy, kidney injury from oxalate crystals. The man's 10 to 15 g a day is five to seven times the whole 2 g mushroom blend in a RYZE serving (calculated), and RYZE's blend doesn't list chaga at all. The reports show chaga isn't harmless in large amounts, not that a cup is risky.

### Reishi

Reishi is marketed for calm and sleep. We couldn't find a published trial of reishi on its own, in healthy adults, testing either claim, so this review makes none. Memorial Sloan Kettering's reishi page lists cautions instead: talk to your healthcare provider if you take blood thinners, because "reishi mushroom can increase your risk of bleeding," or immunosuppressants, because "reishi may not be safe for you"; and it lists liver injury among reported side effects.

### Turkey tail

Turkey tail is marketed for immune support. The National Cancer Institute's summary of medicinal mushrooms describes its human research in people with cancer, using a purified extract called PSK alongside standard treatment. That research doesn't tell you what a pinch of turkey tail powder does for a healthy adult. The same summary states that "the FDA has not approved the use of medicinal mushrooms as a treatment for cancer or any other medical condition."

## The dose gap

Compare the blend with the trials, even on the generous assumption of an even split. About 333 mg of lion's mane is about a ninth of the dried mushroom in Mori's tablets and a fifth of Docherty's 1.8 g (calculated). About 333 mg of cordyceps is a third of Chen's daily Cs-4 and a ninth of Parcell's 3 g, a dose that made no difference in trained cyclists (calculated). The forms differ too: whole powder, concentrated extract and fermented mycelium aren't interchangeable gram for gram, and the label doesn't say which form each mushroom takes. So you can't tell from the jar how close a serving comes to anything that was studied.

## Is it safe?

Harvard Health's view is that "you probably won't do any harm by swapping your normal cup of joe for a mug of mushroom coffee." Its caffeine counts toward your day like any other coffee's. Memorial Sloan Kettering's cautions are about specific situations: reishi with blood thinners or immunosuppressants, and chaga with blood thinners and, in its notes, diabetes medicines (a lab finding whose clinical significance, it says, is unknown). If you take medicines, are pregnant, or have a condition you're treating, ask your clinician or pharmacist before adding mushroom products. Our [caffeine in coffee guide](/blog/caffeine-in-coffee-guide/) has the agencies' caffeine guidance in their own words.

## The coffee in the jar

RYZE's coffee is instant. Its label says organic, but not the species, the origin or the roast date. Instant coffee is coffee that has already been brewed and dried; our [supermarket instant coffee comparison](/blog/we-bought-every-instant-coffee-at-the-supermarket/) shows how widely instant varies.

Price is the other trade-off. RYZE's US store listed it at about 80 cents to $1.20 a serving, depending on bag size and subscription, when checked October 18, 2026. Brewing at home, an 8 fl oz drip-machine cup at the Brew Guide's dose uses about 16 g of coffee, which at the US average of $9.33 a pound for ground coffee in September 2026 (Bureau of Labor Statistics, checked October 18, 2026) costs about 33 cents (calculated). The mushroom coffee's extra cost buys the mushrooms, at doses the label doesn't spell out.

Harvard Health's summary of the evidence is a fair one to end on: "there is very little research on medicinal mushrooms that includes humans," and "none of that research was conducted on mushroom coffee." If you enjoy the taste, that's reason enough to drink it. If you're buying it for a health effect, the studies above are what that effect rests on.
