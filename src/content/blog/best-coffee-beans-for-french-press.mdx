A French press shows you a lot of the coffee. Its metal mesh lets the bean's oils and fine particles into the cup, so you get body and texture, and less of the clarity a paper filter gives. That changes which coffees taste best in it.

Here's what I've learned after years of immersion brewing: most bean advice treats every brewing method the same, and it shouldn't. A delicate light-roast Ethiopian that shines in a V60 can disappoint in a French press, while a medium-dark blend designed for espresso can make one of the most satisfying cups a press will give you. This guide explains what to look for, then groups eight widely sold coffees by the kind of cup they make. They aren't ranked. If you're still choosing a brewer, our [pour-over vs French press](/blog/pour-over-vs-french-press/) comparison helps.

## What a French press does to a coffee

Scott Rao, in *The Professional Barista's Handbook*, explains the trade-off. The French press's coarse mesh lets in a lot of insoluble particles and oil, which gives "a tremendous amount of body but poor flavor clarity." Those insoluble bits add body but can dull the acidity and muddle the flavor. Jonathan Gagné adds that immersion brews in general have more body, because fine particles stay suspended in the coffee.

So, in our view, a French press flatters coffees whose appeal is sweetness, body and depth: chocolate, caramel, nuts, dried fruit. It's harder on the delicate floral and tea-like notes that a paper filter shows off. That's a matter of taste, not a rule, and if you want a bright coffee's clarity with a press's ease, Rao's tip is to pour the finished press through a paper filter.

## Roast level: read the roaster's own label

There's no universal standard for light, medium or dark, as our [roast levels guide](/blog/coffee-roast-levels-explained/) explains: one roaster's medium can be another's light. The coffees below show it. Intelligentsia calls its House Blend "our benchmark for a light roast blend," yet its own three-step scale (Bright, Comforting, Traditional) puts House in the middle. Kicking Horse blends light, medium and dark roasts into Three Sisters and labels the result medium. Compare roast words within one roaster's range, and read its tasting notes.

The usual advice is that light roasts are hard to extract and suit a press poorly. Jessica Easto gives the standard explanation in *Craft Coffee*: lighter roasts are less soluble. Gagné's own measurements complicate it: across his filter brews, darker roasts gave lower average extraction yields, not higher. What's clearer is the taste. James Hoffmann describes acidity falling the longer a coffee is roasted and bitterness rising with darkness, so lighter roasts keep more of a coffee's own character and darker ones trade it for roast flavor.

Our Brew Guide adjusts for roast: its French press starts light roasts at 208°F&nbsp;(98°C) and the finer half of its grind band, medium at 205°F&nbsp;(96°C), and dark at 199°F&nbsp;(93°C) and the coarser half.

## Freshness: buy whole beans and grind just before

Whole beans usually taste best about 7 to 21 days after roasting and stay good to about day 30, as our [freshness guide](/blog/coffee-freshness-roast-to-stale/) explains, though those are typical windows, not a forecast for your bag. Ground coffee runs on a much faster clock: grinding exposes far more of the coffee's surface, and in one study up to 75 percent of a fresh coffee's trapped gas was gone within about 90 seconds of grinding. A coarse French press grind exposes less surface than a fine one, but it's still far more than a whole bean.

So buy whole beans with a roast date and grind them just before you brew. If you buy pre-ground, buy smaller bags and keep them airtight.

## A starting recipe for any of these

Our Brew Guide's French press follows Jessica Easto's eight-minute method at 1:15. For a press holding 500 g of water, that's about 33 g of coffee (calculated: 500 ÷ 15), ground extra coarse, like coarse sea salt (about 900–1,200&nbsp;µm). Pour all the water, stir gently at 0:30 to sink the crust, leave the lid on with the plunger up, and press gently at 8:00, then pour it all out straight away. It makes about 433 g, or about 14.7 US fl oz (433 ml); that's calculated, 500 g of water less about 67 g held back by the grounds at roughly 2 g per gram of coffee.
