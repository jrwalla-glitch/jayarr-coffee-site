Single origin is one of the most common phrases on specialty bags and café menus. It tells you something real, but how much depends on how specific the bag gets.

<LiteYouTube videoId="97Km9NktaFE" title="What's Special About Single Origin Coffee?" />

## What "single origin" means

Jessica Easto, in *Craft Coffee: A Manual*, puts the line simply: a bag with beans from different origins is a blend, and one with beans from one location is a single-origin coffee. A single origin is usually named for at least its country, and usually a growing region or washing station too. Many roasters go further and print the most specific details they have.

So "single origin" covers a range. From broadest to narrowest, a bag might name:

- **A country.** "Colombia" alone could mean beans from many farms mixed together.
- **A region.** Yirgacheffe in Ethiopia, Huila in Colombia or Tarrazú in Costa Rica.
- **A washing station or cooperative.** Where farms are small, many growers' coffee is processed together. Easto's example is Halfwit Coffee Roasters' Rwanda Kanzu, named for the cooperative that produced it.
- **A farm or estate.** Sometimes the estate is better known than its region, as with Hacienda La Esmeralda's Geisha in Panama.
- **A single lot.** Some farms separate small lots by plot, variety or process. Hoffmann notes that many Salvadoran farms make micro-lots by process and variety, and Hacienda La Esmeralda's famous 2004 Geisha came from the first time the family separated parts of the farm into individual lots.

How far down a bag can go depends on the country. James Hoffmann's advice in *The World Atlas of Coffee* is to buy as traceable a coffee as possible, but he's clear that this isn't possible everywhere:

- **Costa Rica:** about 90 percent of producers own small or medium farms, so coffees can often be traced to a farm or cooperative.
- **Ethiopia:** single estates exist but are relatively rare; traceability more often leads to a cooperative.
- **Rwanda:** the average producer has just 183 trees, so coffee is traced to washing stations and farmer groups rather than to one farmer.

Easto adds that a coffee you can't trace to a farm isn't automatically worse: areas with few washing stations simply process the surrounding farms' coffee together.

Two label traps:

- **Colombia's "Supremo" and "Excelso."** Hoffmann explains that these refer only to bean size and have nothing to do with quality. A coffee sold that way may come from many farms, blended before it's sieved.
- **Brazil's "Santos."** It means the coffee shipped from the port of Santos, Hoffmann writes, not where it grew.

## Single origin or blend?

Neither is better; they're built for different things. Easto describes the trade this way:

| | Single origin | Blend |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Show one place's character | A balanced, consistent cup |
| Over the year | Changes with each harvest and lot | Kept close to one profile |
| On the label | Country, often region, farm or station | Usually the countries; sometimes only a name |
| Price | Often higher | Often lower, since blends can use less expensive beans well |

A good blend takes real skill to hold steady month after month, Easto writes. She's wary of roasters who won't disclose at least the countries in a blend, and she notes that "Mocha-Java" is often a generic marketing name rather than coffee from Yemen and Java. Single origins, meanwhile, vary from year to year and even from one corner of a farm to another, so it's hard to say all coffees from one country taste one way.

## What shapes a single origin's flavor

Three things do most of the work, and each has its own guide:

- **Where it grew.** Easto counts soil, climate, sunlight, weather and elevation as the coffee's terroir, and World Coffee Research notes that the best altitude for a variety depends on how close the farm is to the equator. Bags sometimes print an altitude grade, and those cutoffs vary by country and by source: Hoffmann gives El Salvador's Strictly High Grown as above 1,200 m (3,900 ft), for instance. Our [altitude guide](/blog/coffee-altitude-guide/) explains what elevation does and doesn't tell you.
- **The variety.** Most arabica descends from two old lines, Typica and Bourbon, plus Ethiopian landraces such as Gesha. A named variety tells you someone kept track of the trees. Our [coffee varietal guide](/blog/coffee-varietal-guide/) maps the family tree.
- **The processing.** Hoffmann writes that washed coffees tend to have more acidity, more complexity and a cleaner cup; naturals often taste of fruit, sometimes in wild or fermented ways; and pulped-natural and honey coffees gain sweetness and body. The same farm's coffee can taste very different processed two ways. Our [processing guide](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/) explains each method.

## How to read a single-origin bag

- **Country and region.** The more specific, the better you can match it to what you liked before.
- **Producer, farm, cooperative or washing station.** This is the traceability.
- **Variety and process.** Washed for clarity; natural or honey for more fruit, sweetness and body.
- **Altitude.** A useful clue, read with the latitude in mind.
- **Roast date.** Our Freshness tool treats days 7 to 21 after roasting as typical peak for filter coffee and about a month as the usual best-before. Those are typical windows, not a forecast for your bag.
- **Tasting notes.** What the roaster found, and what the coffee *can* taste like, not a promise.

Our guide to [reading a coffee bag label](/blog/how-to-read-coffee-bag-label/) covers the rest.

## Four origins to start with

Our 2020 video covers these four origins. The written sections below use figures checked in October 2026.

<LiteYouTube videoId="CYkMdsrMwf4" title="4 More Single Origin Coffees You Need to Know!" />

These four make a good first flight because they're so different from each other. The tasting tendencies are Hoffmann's summaries for each country; any one bag can differ.

| If you like | Try | Why (Hoffmann's summary) |
|---|---|---|
| Low acidity, heavy body, chocolate and nuts | Brazil | Better Brazilian coffees tend to be low in acidity, heavy and sweet |
| Floral, citrus, tea-like | Ethiopia, washed | The best washed coffees can be "incredibly elegant" |
| Big, wild fruit | Ethiopia, natural | Naturals can be "wildly fruity" |
| Clean and sweet | Costa Rica | Typically very clean and sweet, often light-bodied |
| Bright fruit like red apple or grape | Rwanda | Often fruity and fresh, with berry and floral notes |

### Brazil: comfort in a cup

Brazil grows more coffee than any other country. USDA forecasts a record 71.9 million 60 kg bags for 2026/27, and says Brazil accounts for nearly 40 percent of world production. Hoffmann sums up the better Brazilian coffees as low in acidity, heavy in body and sweet, often with chocolate and nutty flavors.

- **Varieties.** Hoffmann lists Mundo Novo, Yellow Bourbon, Caturra and Catuaí among the varieties developed or evolved in Brazil. "Bourbon Santos," a name once listed here, isn't a variety: Santos is the port.
- **Processing.** Much of Brazil's coffee has long been dried in the whole cherry on patios. Hoffmann credits the pulped natural process, introduced in the early 1990s, with improving quality: the skin comes off, but enough fruit stays on to add sweetness and body.
- **Region.** The Cerrado of western Minas Gerais, at about 850 to 1,250 m (2,800 to 4,100 ft), is dominated by large mechanized farms, Hoffmann writes.
- **Traceability.** High-quality Brazilian coffees are usually traceable to a farm, Hoffmann notes, but Brazil breaks the usual rule: some farms there grow more coffee than all of Bolivia, so a traceable Brazilian coffee isn't necessarily a better one.

Hoffmann notes that a French press's metal mesh lets oils and fine particles into the cup for a rich body, which plays to Brazil's strengths. Our guide to [Brazilian coffee](/blog/is-brazilian-coffee-any-good/) goes further.

### Ethiopia: where arabica began

Arabica evolved in Ethiopia's forests, and Hoffmann describes its coffees as notably diverse: from citrus, often bergamot, and florals to candied or even tropical fruit. The best washed coffees, he writes, can be incredibly elegant and complex, and the best naturals wildly fruity. USDA forecasts a record 12.1 million bags for 2026/27, with 5.0 million drunk at home, about 41 percent (calculated).

Three of Hoffmann's regions to know:

| Region | Altitude | Harvest |
|---|---|---|
| Yirgacheffe | 1,750–2,200 m (5,750–7,200 ft) | October–January |
| Harrar | 1,500–2,100 m (4,900–6,900 ft) | October–February |
| Limu | 1,400–2,200 m (4,600–7,200 ft) | November–January |

He calls many of Yirgacheffe's great washed coffees "explosively aromatic," full of citrus and floral notes with a light, elegant body. Harrar is one of the oldest growing regions and its coffees are quite distinct. Limu lacks Yirgacheffe's reputation but still produces some astonishing coffee. Most Ethiopian bags list "heirloom" varieties; our guides to [Ethiopian coffee](/blog/ethiopian-coffee-flavors-and-varieties/) and [heirloom varieties](/blog/ethiopian-heirloom-vs-named-varietals/) explain why.

Hoffmann writes that paper filters give the cleanest cup, which suits a delicate washed Ethiopian.

### Costa Rica: clean and sweet

Hoffmann describes Costa Rican coffees as typically very clean and sweet, though often very light-bodied, and notes that the micro mills that spread from the mid-to-late 2000s have widened the range of styles. USDA forecasts 1.2 million bags for 2026/27.

Costa Rica is also where the honey process began, according to the importer Café Imports. Hoffmann explains how it works: the skin is stripped off by machine, and the depulpers can be set to leave a chosen share of the sticky fruit on the bean as it dries, so a coffee is sold as, say, 100 percent or 20 percent honey. Mills label the styles differently, Café Imports adds. Hoffmann groups honey coffees with pulped naturals, which gain sweetness and body from the fruit left on. Our guide to [honey processing](/blog/honey-process-colors-explained/) explains the color names.

Tarrazú, at 1,200 to 1,900 m (3,900 to 6,200 ft) in Hoffmann's table, has the country's highest farms and its best-known name, so well known that coffee from outside the region has been sold as Tarrazú. Our [Costa Rican coffee guide](/blog/what-does-costa-rican-coffee-taste-like/) covers all eight regions.

### Rwanda: fruit and freshness

Hoffmann writes that great Rwandan coffees often have a fruitiness and freshness reminiscent of red apples or red grapes, with berry and floral notes fairly common. Most of its output is fully washed arabica, largely Bourbon and its local variant, Mibirizi. USDA puts the 2026/27 crop at 350,000 bags.

The quality is recent. Hoffmann describes how Belgian rule pushed Rwanda toward high volumes of low-quality coffee, and how the 1994 genocide, which killed nearly a million people, devastated the industry. In the recovery, washing stations were built with foreign aid and a push for quality. The rebuilding was under way by 2001, when the Maraba cooperative built its Cyarumbo washing station, the first by a cooperative after the genocide (Alliance for Coffee Excellence); Hoffmann's 2018 edition reports about 300 in operation. His regional tables put the harvest at March to June.

**The potato defect.** Rwanda and Burundi coffees can carry a defect that makes individual beans smell like freshly peeled raw potato when they're ground. Hoffmann describes it as caused by an unknown bacterium entering a damaged cherry skin; it isn't harmful, and it affects single beans rather than a whole bag, unless the whole bag has been ground. Hoffmann's 2018 account describes it as hard to detect in roasted beans until they're ground, and finding it doesn't mean the whole bag is spoiled, as long as you grind only what you brew. Our [Rwandan coffee guide](/blog/rwandan-coffee-a-story-of-rebuilding/) goes further.

## Taste the difference

Tasting two coffees side by side makes their differences much easier to notice. Brew two single origins from different regions, say a washed Ethiopian and a Brazilian, the same way: same dose, ratio, grind and water. Once you can tell them apart, compare within one country: two Ethiopian lots, or a washed Costa Rican against a honey one.
