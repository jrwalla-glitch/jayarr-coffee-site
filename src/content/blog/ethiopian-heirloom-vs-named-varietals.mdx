You pick up a bag of Ethiopian coffee. Under "Variety," it says "Heirloom." It sounds romantic: ancient, rare, passed down through generations. And it's not wrong, exactly. But it isn't telling you much.

Our video predates this revision; where the two differ, follow the text.

## What "heirloom" means

Jeff Koehler, in *Where the Wild Coffee Grows*, gives the clearest description: what's usually called Ethiopian heirloom is "a wily mix" of trees from wild, old-growth forest, raised over time in gardens, with seeds from the best-producing trees selected and passed around. Most Ethiopian coffees, he writes, come from small gardens with a range of different trees, "a blend of 'heirloom' Arabica varieties that get further mixed at the local cooperative."

Coffee people call these trees landraces. World Coffee Research (WCR) defines a landrace as a domesticated, locally adapted, traditional variety that has developed through adaptation to its environment and isolation from other populations. Most coffee landraces, it writes, come from the forests of Ethiopia, and they're "generally associated with very high cup quality and lower yields." The importer Sucafina says exporters and importers frequently use "heirloom" for Ethiopian varieties, and that Ethiopia's varieties fall into two groups: those developed by the Jimma Agricultural Research Center (JARC) and local landraces tied to particular areas, of which it counts at least 130 that are widely grown.

So "heirloom" on a bag can mean either of two things, and the label won't tell you which:

- **A mix of local landraces**, the trees in a garden or forest that nobody has identified one by one.
- **Landraces mixed with research selections**, because JARC hands its selections to farmers and cherries from many farms meet at the washing station. The improved share is growing: USDA reports that over the last four years more than half of Ethiopia's cultivated coffee area was replanted with higher-yielding varieties, which helps explain its record forecast.

James Hoffmann, writing in 2018, noted that little work had yet been done to catalog the genetic diversity and cup quality of Ethiopia's indigenous trees. Koehler made the same point about JARC's own collection: apart from resistance to coffee berry disease, little assessment had been done on its trees. The label is honest about how little anyone can tell you.

## How much diversity is there?

More than anywhere else, but less than the romance suggests. A 2020 genome study of 736 arabica accessions, led by World Coffee Research with Italian and French partners, found that Ethiopian material has by far the widest diversity of any group, including diversity not found in Yemen or in cultivated varieties. The same study, by Scalabrin and colleagues, also found that the whole species has "extremely low levels of variation," most likely because it arose from a single polyploidization event, which the authors write makes its internal diversity less useful for breeding than in most crops.

Two more findings put that in perspective:

- **Cultivated coffee is a narrow slice.** A 2021 study by Montagnon and colleagues found the wild Ethiopian accessions formed their own genetic cluster, clearly separated from Yemeni coffee and from varieties grown around the world. WCR notes that in Brazil, which grows about 40 percent of the world's coffee, 97.55 percent of cultivars derive from Typica and Bourbon, two old lines that came out of Yemen.
- **The counts run into the thousands.** UNESCO describes Kafa, nominated as a biosphere reserve in 2010, as the birthplace of wild arabica, with "close to 5,000 wild varieties." JARC's field genebanks held 5,853 accessions from 6,923 original collections, according to a 2019 JARC review.

Most of what the world knows about Ethiopia's coffee genetics comes from collections. The largest outside Ethiopia, Labouisse and Kotecha of CIRAD wrote in 2008, came from an FAO mission in 1964–65 and a French ORSTOM mission in 1966: 682 accessions, mostly from forests. WCR adds that those missions didn't collect much east of the Rift Valley or around Harrar.

## The JARC selections: where the numbers come from

JARC was set up in 1967, Koehler writes, with a national mandate to improve coffee quality and productivity and to work on disease resistance. In 1971, he writes, Ethiopia had an outbreak of coffee berry disease, a fungus that blackens and mummifies the cherries and can cut production by up to 80 percent in wet years. JARC launched a program to find resistant trees, starting with accessions from the forests of Jimma and Kafa. Its collections for the disease program ran from 1973 to 1987, and it released 18 resistant selections in that first phase, three of which were later withdrawn, according to a 2019 review by Demelash Teferi of JARC.

These are selections, not crosses: trees picked from Ethiopian coffee, tested and multiplied. The numbers you see on bags, such as 74110 and 74112, are JARC's names for them. By 2019 it had released 42 arabica varieties in all, 35 pure-line selections and 7 hybrids.

A 2015 study by Elsa Tirfe and colleagues, one of them a JARC researcher, reproduces ratings for seven of the early releases from reports covering 1996 to 2004:

| Selection (released) | Tree shape; cup rating (1996–2004 reports) | Research-station yield |
|---|---|---|
| 74110 (1978/79) | Compact; good | 1,910 kg/ha |
| 74112 (1978/79) | Compact; good | 1,810 kg/ha |
| 74140 (1978/79) | Compact; average | 1,970 kg/ha |
| 74158 (1978/79) | Compact; fairly good | 1,910 kg/ha |
| 744 (1979/80) | Open; average | 1,660 kg/ha |
| 75227 (1980/81) | Open; fairly average | 1,780 kg/ha |

All are rated resistant to coffee berry disease. The yields are research-station figures, converted from quintals (100 kg) per hectare; the same table gives farmers' yields of about 800 to 1,000 kg/ha. The table also lists 74165, another compact, disease-resistant selection rated "fairly good" in the cup.

The authors then cupped all seven, grown at three JARC stations, Gera (1,940 m), Jimma (1,753 m) and Metu (1,550 m), from the 2012 harvest. 74158 scored highest, statistically level with 74110, 74112 and 74140, and 744 lowest. The compact selections did better than the open, large-bean ones, with 74112, 74140 and 74158 showing the most acidity, and the authors rank the stations Gera, Jimma, Metu: best at the highest, weakest at the lowest. That's one harvest, scored on the Ethiopian Commodity Exchange's cupping protocol rather than the SCA's, so read it as a tendency.

In 1991, JARC adopted a new strategy: breeding for each region from that region's own landraces, so that varieties would suit local conditions and keep each area's character. Eleven of those selections were released in 2010 for Sidama, Yirgacheffe, Wollega and Harrarghe.

## What a name on the bag tells you

A named selection tells you someone tracked which trees went into the lot, which usually means care elsewhere in the chain too. It doesn't tell you the coffee will taste better: in the ratings above, 74110 and 74112 rate "good," not exceptional, and the cup still depends on where and how it was grown and processed.

Names can also mislead, because trees travel and their names stick:

- **"Harar" outside Ethiopia.** WCR's Harar entry is a variety grown in Rwanda, introduced there from Congo in 1956, with a Typica-like genetic background, not an Ethiopian landrace. It notes that many varieties share the name Harar "but are not necessarily the same."
- **"Geisha."** WCR warns that the name is often applied to coffees that don't share the distinct genetics of Panamanian Geisha.
- **Local names.** In Yemen, a 2022 study found farmers' variety names don't track the trees' genetics. Our [Yemen guide](/blog/yemen-coffee-guide-mocha-origin/) covers it.

Some importers now list local landrace names: Café Imports' Ethiopia profile, for instance, gives its common varieties as heirloom types "including Kudhome, Gesha, Djimma, and others." Treat names like these as you would "heirloom": a clue to where the coffee came from, not a guarantee of what's in it.

## The Gesha connection

The most famous Ethiopian landrace made its name somewhere else. Koehler traces the route: seeds imported around 1931 from the Geisha forest in southwest Ethiopia were planted at a nursery in Kitale, Kenya; seeds from those trees went in 1936 to research stations in Uganda and at Lyamungu, Tanzania; in 1953 Geisha seeds from Lyamungu reached Costa Rica as a disease-resistant strain; and in 1963 a farmer took some to Boquete, Panama. It was planted for disease resistance, never for flavor, and took about 75 years to be noticed, until Hacienda La Esmeralda's lot won the 2004 Best of Panama competition.

Its early identity was loose. Koehler quotes a botanist who worked with the historical Lyamungu Geisha material: it "is not a variety in the sense that it is a genetic entity," only trees that came from near Geisha Mountain. The Panamanian line is different: WCR traces it to CATIE's T2722 and describes it as genetically distinct and uniform. WCR rates Panama's Geisha "Exceptional" for quality potential at high altitude, with low yields. Our [Panama guide](/blog/panama-coffee-guide-geisha-boquete/) and [Gesha guide](/blog/gesha-coffee-worth-the-price/) tell the rest.

Geisha's success set off a hunt for the next one in Ethiopia's forests. Koehler argues that certainly many fine, unknown varieties exist there, but that finding them needs a long, systematic search and decades of evaluation, and Ethiopia punishes anyone trying to take coffee seed out of the country.

## Other Ethiopian landraces you'll see abroad

Geisha isn't the only one. WCR's catalog classes three entries as Ethiopian landraces, and an importer's DNA tests have added a fourth name:

| Name on the bag | What it is | WCR quality potential |
|---|---|---|
| Geisha (Panama) | Collected in Ethiopia in the 1930s; reached Panama via Tanzania and Costa Rica | Exceptional |
| Java | Taken from Ethiopia to Java by the Dutch in the early 19th century; later selected in Cameroon | Very Good |
| AB3 | Selected in Ethiopia and taken to Indonesia in 1928; released there in 1995 | Good |
| Pink Bourbon | Not in WCR's catalog; Café Imports' DNA tests (2017 and 2023) found an Ethiopian landrace, not a Bourbon | Not rated |

Café Imports' 2023 test covered five samples from producers in Colombia and Costa Rica, four sold as Pink Bourbon and one as Orange Bourbon: one was pure, two were mixed with true Bourbon and two with Catimor, though the importer couldn't say whether the mixtures came from mixed beans. Our [coffee varietal guide](/blog/coffee-varietal-guide/) puts all of these on the family tree.

## How to actually buy Ethiopian coffee

Here's the practical advice: don't choose Ethiopian coffee by variety. Choose by region and process. We've [reviewed seven Ethiopian coffees side by side](/blog/7-ethiopian-coffees-reviewed-best-and-worst/) if you want specific recommendations.

- **The region tells you the flavor direction.** Yirgacheffe for floral brightness, Sidama for stone fruit, Guji for dense fruit, Harrar for blueberry funk. Our guide to [Ethiopian coffee flavors and regions](/blog/ethiopian-coffee-flavors-and-varieties/) goes region by region.
- **The process tells you the intensity.** A washed Ethiopian is clean and bright; a natural is fruitier and heavier, sometimes dramatically so. The difference between a washed and a natural from the same area can be bigger than the difference between two regions. Our [processing methods guide](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/) explains why.
- **"Heirloom" is neutral information.** It doesn't make a coffee better or worse. Sucafina makes the useful point that because landrace trees are so varied, flavors differ even between farms with similar conditions. Pay for the region, the washing station or producer, and a roaster whose coffees you like, not for the word.
- **A named selection is a traceability signal, not a score.** If you want a score, look for one. Our guide to [what coffee scores mean](/blog/coffee-scoring-system-explained/) helps there.

## The bigger picture

Ethiopia's forests and gardens hold most of arabica's genetic diversity, and they're shrinking. Labouisse and Kotecha reported that closed high forest in Ethiopia's southwestern highlands fell from about 1,158,000 hectares in the early 1970s to about 556,700 by 1997, a loss of 52 percent in under 30 years. Climate models add to the pressure on wild arabica; our research note on [coffee and climate change](/deep-brew/research/coffee-climate-change-data/) sets out what they project for Ethiopia, and how much depends on whether coffee can move and the forests are kept.

The "heirloom" label, for all its vagueness, points to something real: a living collection of coffee genetics that has barely been read.
