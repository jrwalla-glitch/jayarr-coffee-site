<AnswerBox answer="The International Bartenders Association’s official espresso martini is 50&nbsp;ml of vodka, 30&nbsp;ml of Kahlúa, 10&nbsp;ml of sugar syrup and one strong espresso, shaken well with ice and strained into a chilled cocktail glass, with three coffee beans on top. Difford’s Guide uses 45&nbsp;ml of vodka, 30&nbsp;ml of hot espresso and 20&nbsp;ml of coffee liqueur, and says to shake the espresso while it’s hot: its crema is key to the drink, and letting it cool will “kill the cocktail.” Pull the shot just before you shake." />

The espresso martini is vodka, coffee liqueur and espresso, shaken hard enough to build a pale foam on top. It isn’t a true martini; Difford’s Guide calls it perhaps the best known of the contemporary classic cocktails to come out of the 1990s. Below are its published recipes, where it came from, and four more coffee cocktails, each from a named recipe with its own measures.

## The espresso martini, recipe by recipe

| | IBA | Difford’s | Bradsell |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vodka | 50&nbsp;ml | 45&nbsp;ml | 50&nbsp;ml |
| Coffee liqueur | 30&nbsp;ml Kahlúa | 20&nbsp;ml | 10&nbsp;ml Kahlúa, 5&nbsp;ml Tia Maria |
| Shot | 1 strong shot | 30&nbsp;ml, hot | 25&nbsp;ml, double strength |
| Sugar syrup | 10&nbsp;ml | To taste | 5&nbsp;ml, cane |

The IBA’s is its official recipe; Difford’s Guide’s is its own; and Bradsell’s is his later recipe, which Difford’s Guide prints from the 2010s.

In US measures, 50&nbsp;ml is about 1⅔&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz, 45&nbsp;ml about 1½, 30&nbsp;ml about 1, 25&nbsp;ml about ⅚, 20&nbsp;ml about ⅔, 10&nbsp;ml about ⅓, and 5&nbsp;ml about a teaspoon (all calculated). Difford’s gives its own recipe in ounces as 1½&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz of vodka, 1&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz of espresso and ⅔&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz of liqueur.

Difford’s leaves the sugar out of its main recipe. Depending on the coffee liqueur, it suggests adding 2.5 to 5&nbsp;ml of rich (2:1) sugar syrup, and says that with a very dry or bitter liqueur the sugar becomes more necessary than optional. Bradsell’s recipe ends with a test: “if you can see through it or it is brown, not black, it’s wrong.”

## How to make one

This follows the IBA’s recipe, with the espresso used hot, as Difford’s Guide advises.

- **Vodka:** 50&nbsp;ml (about 1⅔&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz)
- **Kahlúa:** 30&nbsp;ml (about 1&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz)
- **Sugar syrup:** 10&nbsp;ml (about ⅓&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz)
- **Espresso:** 1 strong espresso, freshly pulled
- **Garnish:** 3 coffee beans
- **Makes:** 1 cocktail

1. Put a cocktail glass in the freezer or fill it with ice water to chill.
2. Pull a strong espresso.
3. Pour the vodka, Kahlúa, sugar syrup and the hot espresso into a cocktail shaker.
4. Fill the shaker with ice and shake it hard.
5. Empty the glass if you chilled it with ice water, then strain the cocktail into it.
6. Float three coffee beans on the foam.

**The espresso.** The IBA asks only for “1 strong espresso.” Bradsell’s recipe uses 25&nbsp;ml of double-strength espresso, and Difford’s 30&nbsp;ml. Our Brew Guide’s Shorter setting, 18&nbsp;g of coffee to 27&nbsp;g in the cup, makes a short, strong shot of about that size; its classic double is 36&nbsp;g. If the shot tastes sour or bitter on its own, it will in the drink too, so it’s worth [dialing in](/blog/how-to-dial-in-espresso/) first.

**Why hot?** Difford’s Guide says the espresso’s crema is key to the drink’s success and look, and that if you let the coffee cool so the crema dies, “you’ll kill the cocktail.” It also strains the drink through a fine strainer, and expresses a lemon zest over the top and discards it before adding the beans.

**The three beans.** Difford’s traces the garnish to the Italian custom of serving sambuca with floating coffee beans, *con la mosca*, “with the fly,” where the three beans stand for health, wealth and happiness.

### Without an espresso machine

Difford’s Guide lists a ready-made espresso, Exprè Caffè Italiano, as an alternative to a fresh shot. Otherwise, a moka pot makes coffee James Hoffmann describes as very strong, though it isn’t espresso, so the drink won’t be quite the same. Brew it as in our [moka pot guide](/blog/moka-pot-brewing-guide/) and measure out what the recipe asks for while it’s hot.

## Where the espresso martini came from

The drink is credited to Dick Bradsell, a London bartender who died in 2016, and the details depend on who tells it.

- **Difford’s Guide** dates it to about 1983, at the Soho Brasserie on Old Compton Street, where Bradsell first made it as a Vodka Espresso served on the rocks. In the story, a customer said to be a model asked for a drink that would wake her up and then get her drunk. Bradsell told Difford’s Guide that the brasserie’s coffee machine stood right next to his station, so coffee was on his mind, and vodka was what everyone drank.
- **Punch** (Robert Simonson, 2021) reports that the first version had no coffee liqueur, only vodka, espresso and a little simple syrup, on the rocks, with three espresso beans. It adds that Bradsell’s daughter, Beatrice Bradsell, dates the drink to 1985, because her father told her it was when the David Bowie film *Absolute Beginners* was being filmed in Soho.
- **Match and Pharmacy:** both accounts then move to Match EC1 in Clerkenwell in 1997. *Punch* says that’s where the drink first appeared on a menu; Difford’s says a bartender there first served it straight up in a Martini glass, and Bradsell rebalanced it using the Brandy Alexander as a template. In 1998 he renamed it the Pharmaceutical Stimulant for the menu at Pharmacy, Damien Hirst’s restaurant in Notting Hill.

The IBA now lists the Espresso Martini among its official cocktails, in its “New Era” category.

## Four more coffee cocktails

Each of these follows one published recipe, with its own measures.

### Irish coffee

This is the IBA’s recipe.

- 50&nbsp;ml (about 1⅔&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz) Irish whiskey
- 120&nbsp;ml (about 4&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz) hot coffee
- 50&nbsp;ml (about 1⅔&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz) fresh cream, chilled
- 1 teaspoon sugar

1. Pour the hot black coffee into a warmed Irish coffee glass.
2. Add the whiskey and the sugar, at least a teaspoon, and stir until it dissolves. The IBA says sugar syrup can replace the sugar.
3. Pour the thick, chilled cream carefully over the back of a spoon held just above the coffee, so that it floats on top without mixing.

Use freshly brewed coffee; our [pour-over guide](/blog/ultimate-pour-over-coffee-guide-and-best-v60-technique/) makes a good cup. The Foynes Flying Boat & Maritime Museum in Ireland tells the drink’s origin: in the winter of 1943, a flight from Foynes to New York turned back in bad weather, and the chef, Joe Sheridan, put Irish whiskey in the stranded passengers’ coffee. Asked if it was Brazilian coffee, he joked that it was Irish coffee.

### Carajillo

This is Licor 43’s recipe; the liqueur’s maker calls it its most popular drink in Mexico.

- 50&nbsp;ml (about 1⅔&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz) Licor 43 Original
- 1 hot espresso
- Ice

1. Pour the Licor 43 into a cocktail shaker.
2. Add the hot espresso and some ice, shake, and strain into a glass full of ice.

Without a shaker, Licor 43 says, stir the ingredients in the glass. In Spain the word is broader: the Royal Spanish Academy’s dictionary defines a *carajillo* as hot coffee mixed with a high-proof alcoholic drink, so it isn’t always made with Licor 43.

### Cold brew negroni

This is Simon Difford’s Cold Brew Negroni, created in December 2021: a negroni with a splash of cold brew coffee.

- 30&nbsp;ml (about 1&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz) gin
- 25&nbsp;ml (about ⅚&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz) sweet red (rosso) vermouth
- 20&nbsp;ml (about ⅔&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz) red bitter liqueur, such as Campari
- 10&nbsp;ml (about 2 teaspoons) cold brew coffee
- Orange zest twist

1. Stir the gin, vermouth, bitter liqueur and cold brew with ice.
2. Strain into an ice-filled old-fashioned glass, over a large cube if you have one.
3. Express the orange zest twist over the drink and use it as the garnish.

Difford’s doesn’t say whether it means ready-to-drink cold brew or concentrate; our [cold brew concentrate guide](/blog/cold-brew-concentrate-guide/) shows how to make both.

### White Russian

The IBA lists this as a variation of its Black Russian.

- 50&nbsp;ml (about 1⅔&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz) vodka
- 20&nbsp;ml (about ⅔&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz) coffee liqueur
- Fresh cream; the IBA gives no amount

1. Pour the vodka and coffee liqueur into an old-fashioned glass filled with ice and stir gently. That’s a Black Russian.
2. For a White Russian, float fresh cream on top and stir it in slowly.
