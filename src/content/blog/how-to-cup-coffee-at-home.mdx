Cupping is how the coffee trade tastes coffee side by side. Every coffee gets the same simple brew: ground coffee and hot water in a cup, no filter and no technique, so a difference in the cup is a difference in the coffee. As James Hoffmann puts it in *The World Atlas of Coffee*, the idea is to keep the brewing process from changing the flavor and to treat every coffee as equally as possible.

The Specialty Coffee Association (SCA) wrote the method down. Its 2004 cupping protocol was replaced in November 2024 by a set of standards; the one that covers the cupping itself is **SCA-102-2024, *Sample Preparation and Tasting Mechanics***. The recipe below comes from it. The SCA defines cupping as tasting several cups of each coffee, each ground and brewed on its own. You can do it with kitchen equipment.

## What the SCA's standard says

SCA-102 is written for assessing green coffee, so it also says how to roast the sample. You'll be cupping coffee that's already roasted, which the standard allows for: when a roasted coffee is being described, the roasting step simply doesn't apply.

| Step | SCA-102 (2024) |
|---|---|
| Dose | 8.25 g of whole beans per 150 ml of cup capacity, measured to the rim, weighed for each cup on a 0.1&nbsp;g scale (±0.2&nbsp;g) |
| Grind | Each cup ground on its own, as close to cupping as practical, so 70–75% passes an 850&nbsp;µm sieve: slightly coarser than for paper-filter drip |
| Water | 194 to 205°F&nbsp;(93 ± 3°C), poured to the rim with a little turbulence |
| Crust | Left unbroken for 3 to 5 minutes, then broken by stirring about three times |
| Tasting | Once it cools to about 158°F&nbsp;(70°C), at least three rounds as it cools below body temperature |
| Cups | Five per coffee when scoring; three to five when only describing |

The water should have no chlorine, a calcium hardness of 50 to 175 ppm and an alkalinity at or near 40 to 70 ppm (both as calcium carbonate), and a pH of 6 to 8. Our guide to [coffee water recipes](/blog/coffee-water-recipes-compared/) explains those numbers. Cups should be glass or ceramic, all identical, holding 200 to 350 ml. A session usually has three to six coffees; the SCA recommends no more than six coffees per session.

## The dose for your cups

The dose is set by how much your cup holds, not by how much water you pour. To find out, put an empty cup on the scale, fill it to the brim with room-temperature water and read the grams: that's its capacity in milliliters. Multiply by 0.055 (8.25 ÷ 150) for the coffee. These doses are calculated:

| Cup capacity, to the rim | Coffee per cup |
|---|---|
| 200 ml (about 6¾ US fl oz) | 11.0 g |
| 250 ml (about 8½ US fl oz) | 13.8 g |
| 300 ml (about 10 US fl oz) | 16.5 g |
| 350 ml (about 11¾ US fl oz) | 19.3 g |

The dose is 55 g per liter of empty-cup capacity. This is not a measured coffee-to-water ratio: the grounds occupy part of the cup. Use the capacity-based dose above and fill to the rim.

Weigh whole beans for each cup separately, and grind each cup separately too. The SCA's reason for several cups per coffee is uniformity: if one cup of a coffee tastes different from the others, that coffee isn't consistent from bean to bean. Separate doses are what let you see it.

The SCA has every coffee on the table go through each step together: all the fragrances, then all the pours, then the tasting rounds, so you compare them at the same stage.

If you're cupping with other people, the SCA also describes a more hygienic version: each coffee gets its own serving spoon, which moves coffee from the cup to your own spoon or shot glass, so no one's spoon goes back into the shared cups.

## What to taste for

SCA-102 splits the experience into sections, in the order you meet them: **fragrance** (the dry grounds), **aroma** (the crust and the break), then in the mouth **flavor**, **aftertaste**, **acidity**, **sweetness** and **mouthfeel**, and finally an **overall** impression. Hoffmann's list of what almost every score sheet assesses is shorter and a good place to start: sweetness, acidity, mouthfeel, balance and flavor.

Since 2024 the SCA keeps describing a coffee and liking it apart. Its descriptive form has you rate how intense each section is (low, medium or high, on a 15-point scale) and tick the flavor families you find, the same families as the SCA and World Coffee Research [Coffee Taster's Flavor Wheel](/blog/train-your-coffee-palate/). How much you like it goes on a separate form. At home you can do the same on a sheet of paper: for each coffee, a few words, a low, medium or high for acidity, sweetness and body, and then, separately, whether you'd buy it again.

## Our shortcuts for cupping at home

These simplifications are our suggestions, not part of the SCA's standard:

- **Two coffees, two or three cups each.** The SCA scores with five cups per coffee and describes with three to five. Two or three is enough to notice whether a coffee's cups match, and two coffees are enough to compare. Hoffmann's advice for tasting at home starts with two very different coffees for the same reason: without a second coffee you're comparing against memory.
- **Matching mugs or glasses.** They only need to be identical; weigh one full of water to set the dose.
- **No roast to control.** You're tasting coffee as it's sold, so roast date and roast level are part of what you taste. Compare coffees roasted at about the same time, or the age difference may be what you notice.
- **Tasting blind.** Have someone label the cups underneath, or label them yourself and shuffle. Hoffmann suggests not reading the bags until you've written your notes, then comparing your words with the roaster's.
- **Or skip the crust.** For describing coffee, the SCA also allows a batch brew, such as a French press or a filter brewer at 55 to 60 g of coffee per liter, with no crust to break. Two small French presses side by side is Hoffmann's own at-home method.

## Mistakes that skew a cupping

- **Tasting too soon.** The SCA starts tasting at about 158°F&nbsp;(70°C), and Hoffmann notes it's much easier to pick out flavors in warm coffee than in hot. A thermometer in a spare cup tells you when to start.
- **Talking before you've written.** The SCA notes that information about a coffee can change what tasters perceive, and World Coffee Research's trained panels usually taste in silence. Write your notes, then compare.
- **Too many coffees.** The SCA recommends no more than six coffees per session, and World Coffee Research's panels usually assess four to six coffees in a session of 1½ to 2 hours to avoid fatigue.
- **Uneven doses or grinds.** The SCA allows only ±0.2&nbsp;g per cup. Cups brewed at different strengths are harder to compare, and the last coffee's leftovers in the grinder can make one cup taste unlike its neighbors. Weigh every cup and clear the grinder between coffees.
- **A dirty spoon.** Rinse it in hot water before it goes into each cup, or one coffee carries into the next.

A consistent burr grinder and a 0.1&nbsp;g scale are the two tools the method depends on. Our guides compare [electric](/blog/top-5-best-electric-coffee-grinders/) and [manual grinders](/blog/top-10-best-manual-coffee-grinders/), and [five coffee scales](/blog/5-coffee-scales-reviewed/).

## Where cupping leads

Cupping is a way to compare, and the comparison is the point. Put two coffees from different origins side by side and the differences are much easier to name than either coffee on its own.
