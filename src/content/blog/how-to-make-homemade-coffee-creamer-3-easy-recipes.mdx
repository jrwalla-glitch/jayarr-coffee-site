Homemade creamer is two ingredients and a flavoring. Sweetened condensed milk brings the sugar and the body, the milk thins it to pouring consistency, and the flavor is up to you. Below is the base, three flavors to make with it, what's in a tablespoon, and how long to keep it.

Our February 2020 video makes the maple-cinnamon version. It predates this revision in two places: its caption says a 16 oz can, where its own notes and this recipe use 14 oz, and it keeps the creamer for up to 14 days, which is longer than USDA's figures support. Follow the storage section below.

## The base

- **Sweetened condensed milk:** one 14 oz (397 g) can.
- **Milk or cream:** 1¾ cups, which is 14 US fl oz (414 ml). Our video's notes suggest anything from skim, 2 percent or whole milk to half-and-half or cream, or a plant milk.
- **Makes:** about 3 cups (about 740 ml, or 50 tablespoons), calculated from USDA's weight for a cup of condensed milk (306 g), so the can is about 1⅓ cups.

Whisk the two together in a bowl, or shake them in a jar with a tight lid, until the condensed milk has dissolved. Then add a flavor.

## Three flavors

Each one goes into the full base above.

| Flavor | Add to the base |
|---|---|
| Maple-cinnamon, the one in our video | 1 tbsp maple syrup, 1 tsp vanilla extract, ½ tsp ground cinnamon, ⅛ tsp ground allspice |
| French vanilla | 2 tsp vanilla extract |
| Hazelnut | 2 tsp hazelnut extract |

Here's the maple-cinnamon version, step by step, as our video makes it:

1. Pour the can of sweetened condensed milk into a large bowl.
2. Add the milk, maple syrup, vanilla, cinnamon and allspice.
3. Whisk until the condensed milk has dissolved and the spices are mixed in.
4. Pour the creamer into a clean bottle or jar with a lid, and refrigerate it straight away.
5. Shake it before each use, and stir 1 to 2 tablespoons into a cup of hot or iced coffee.

It takes about 5 minutes from start to finish. The other two flavors work the same way, with their own add-ins in step 2.

## More flavors from our video's notes

Our video's notes call the maple-cinnamon version gingerbread, and they list more combinations to try. A few of them, again for one batch:

- **Pumpkin spice:** 2 tbsp maple syrup, 1 tsp vanilla extract, 1 tsp pumpkin pie spice and ½ tsp cinnamon.
- **Vanilla chai:** 2 tbsp honey, 2 tsp vanilla extract, 1 tsp cinnamon and ½ tsp cardamom.
- **Dark chocolate:** 2 tbsp chocolate syrup and 1 tsp cocoa powder.
- **Peppermint mocha:** 1 tsp peppermint extract, 1 tbsp chocolate syrup and 1 tsp cocoa powder.

Ground spices, cocoa and honey can leave a creamer gritty, and the video's notes suggest warming them first with a little of the milk. The honey dissolves, but cocoa and ground spices only disperse, so whisk them in until evenly mixed. Let it cool, whisk it into the condensed milk and the rest of the milk, refrigerate promptly, and shake before use, since some particles settle. Extracts and syrups can go straight in. Start with the amounts above and adjust the next batch to taste.

## How much sugar is in it?

Almost all of the sweetness comes from the can. By USDA FoodData Central's figures, sweetened condensed milk is 54.4 percent sugar, so a 14 oz can holds about 216 g of it. Whole milk adds about 22 g of its own lactose and the tablespoon of maple syrup about 12 g. In all, the maple-cinnamon batch has about 250 g of sugar across about 50 tablespoons:

- **About 5 g of sugar per tablespoon,** and about 32 calories (calculated from USDA's values for each ingredient).
- **For comparison,** USDA's entry for a flavored liquid coffee creamer lists 33 g of sugar per 100 g, which is also about 5 g in its 15 g tablespoon.

With whole milk, this maple-cinnamon recipe has about the same total sugar per tablespoon as USDA's generic flavored liquid creamer. Individual products and other homemade recipes vary. What you control is what goes in and how much of it.

For a less sweet creamer, use less of the can. Half the can with the same 1¾ cups of milk and the maple-cinnamon flavorings works out to about 3.6 g of total sugars per tablespoon (calculated the same way). Or simply use less creamer in the cup.

## Dairy-free

Our video's notes suggest sweetened condensed coconut milk with a plant milk. <a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Nature%27s+Charm+Sweetened+Condensed+Coconut+Milk&tag=jayarrcoffee-20" rel="sponsored noopener">Nature's Charm Sweetened Condensed Coconut Milk</a> is one brand of it. Check the size of the can: if it's smaller than 14 oz, scale the milk down to match, using ⅛ cup of milk for each ounce of condensed milk (the base's 1¾ cups for 14 oz, calculated).

Plant milks behave differently in hot coffee, and some can separate or curdle. Our [plant milk guide](/blog/best-plant-milks-for-coffee/) compares them, including what a study of soy milk in coffee found about curdling.

## Storage and food safety

USDA's FoodKeeper has no entry for homemade creamer, so we go by the ingredients in it:

- **An opened can of condensed or evaporated milk:** 4 to 5 days in the refrigerator, according to FoodKeeper.
- **Milk:** FoodKeeper says to go by the use-by date on the package.

Treat the creamer like the shortest-lived thing in it: **use it within 4 to 5 days, or by the milk's date if that comes first.** That's shorter than the 14 days our 2020 video gives. FoodKeeper's 3 weeks for "coffee creamer, liquid refrigerated" counts from the day you buy a commercial creamer; it isn't a figure for a homemade mix. For a dairy-free batch, FoodKeeper lists 7 to 10 days for opened soy or almond milk, but it has no entry for condensed coconut milk, so follow that can's label and use the shorter time.

A few rules from the FDA apply too:

- Keep the refrigerator at or below 40°F&nbsp;(4°C).
- Don't leave the creamer out for more than 2 hours, or 1 hour if the room is above 90°F&nbsp;(32°C).
- "If you're not sure or if the food looks questionable, throw it out."

Shake the jar before each use, as our video does.

## Using it

Stir 1 to 2 tablespoons into a cup of coffee and adjust from there. It works hot or over ice, as our video shows, and the video also steams it like milk; our [milk steaming guide](/blog/how-to-steam-milk-for-lattes/) covers the technique.
