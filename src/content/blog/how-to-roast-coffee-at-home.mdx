James Hoffmann's advice is to treat home roasting as a new hobby, not as a way to save money on coffee. The equipment and the time cost something, so it's worth doing if you enjoy the roasting itself. What you get is the chance to roast small batches of many more green coffees than you could buy roasted, and to learn as you go. Expect some terrible failures and some surprise successes, he writes.

This page is the method. [How is coffee roasted?](/blog/how-is-coffee-roasted/) explains what happens inside the bean, and [coffee roast levels explained](/blog/coffee-roast-levels-explained/) covers what light, medium and dark mean.

## Buying green coffee

Hoffmann's suggestions for buying raw coffee:

- **Buy traceable coffee**, where the seller can tell you where it came from.
- **Don't buy in bulk.** Green coffee keeps longer than roasted coffee, but it fades too; he'd use it within 3 to 6 months.
- **Benchmark your roasts.** Now and then, buy a green coffee from a company that also sells it roasted, and compare your roast with theirs.

## What to roast in

You can roast coffee in almost anything that gets hot enough, Hoffmann writes, but the beans need to keep moving or they roast unevenly.

- **An oven.** Beans on a baking sheet brown, but he calls the results "pretty terrible": uneven, and scorched where they touch the tray.
- **A wok or pan.** It works, but the stirring it needs quickly gets tiresome.
- **A heat gun or a popcorn popper.** Many people start with a heat gun or a modified electric popcorn popper, Hoffmann writes. Poppers roast a small batch quickly, in around four or five minutes, but don't roast lighter roasts evenly, and some lack the power to roast coffee properly. Popper makers don't intend them for this: West Bend's manual for its Clear Air popper says it "is not intended for use with seeds, beans, or anything other than popcorn," and that a plugged-in popper shouldn't be left unattended. We don't give popper settings here for that reason.
- **A home roaster.** If you really want to roast well, Hoffmann writes, you need a machine designed for it. There are two main types: hot-air roasters and drum roasters.

The specs and instructions below are as published by the makers, checked October 18, 2026. Your own machine's manual comes first wherever it differs from this page.

### Hot-air roasters

Hot-air roasters work like powerful popcorn poppers: hot air both heats the beans and keeps them moving. You can usually set the heat and the fan speed, so you can speed a roast up or slow it down. They cost less than drum roasters, and Hoffmann calls them a great starting point.

**Fresh Roast SR540.** Its user manual, from the maker, says it roasts about 4&nbsp;oz (114&nbsp;g) at a time: four of the included 43&nbsp;cc scoops, never more. It starts at a factory setting of fan 5, heat 9 and a 6-minute timer, but the manual notes that these are only for starting up and need adjusting during the roast. It can show the temperature in °F, and it has a cooling cycle. The manual also says to empty the chaff basket and clean the top screen before every use, not to lift the glass chamber or chaff basket during a roast, to plug it into its own outlet on a dedicated circuit, to keep it off and away from stove burners, not to use it outdoors, and to wait at least 30 minutes between roasts. <a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Fresh+Roast+SR540&tag=jayarrcoffee-20" rel="sponsored noopener">Search for the Fresh Roast SR540 on Amazon</a>.

### Drum roasters

Home drum roasters tumble the beans in a heated drum, like commercial ones, though they aren't built as heavily. Some can be programmed, so you can vary the heat through a roast and repeat a profile you like.

**Behmor 2000AB Plus.** Behmor's manual gives a maximum batch of 1&nbsp;lb (454&nbsp;g) on a 120&nbsp;V supply, and says its smoke suppression can take a full pound to second crack without visible smoke. It goes into a cooling cycle automatically when the roast ends, and has a button to start cooling at any time. Behmor is emphatic about supervision: the roaster "is NOT a set and walk away device," and preset times and programs are only estimates. Its other rules include a properly ventilated spot with at least 18&nbsp;in (46&nbsp;cm) of clearance on all sides and above, no outdoor use, no high-chaff green coffee, and never going more than 10 seconds into second crack, because the beans could ignite. See <a href="https://behmor.com/behmor-2000ab-plus/">Behmor's product page</a>.

## Chaff, smoke and fire

Roasting coffee is roasting a food until it nearly burns, so the safety rules matter as much as the flavor ones.

- **Chaff.** The beans shed their thin, papery skin as they start to expand, around the time they turn yellow. Commercial roasters collect it and remove it because it's a fire risk, Hoffmann writes. At home, empty your machine's chaff collector every time: Fresh Roast says clearing it after each roast is required.
- **Smoke.** Smoke increases as the roast goes darker; Behmor describes light smoke as beans go into second crack, and smoke levels rising quickly from there. Hoffmann recommends a well-ventilated area even with machines that handle smoke well. Fresh Roast suggests roasting near an open window or under a range hood that vents outside.
- **Fire.** Coffee beans can catch fire. Hoffmann warns that going on past second crack usually ends with the beans catching fire, and Behmor's manual says to cool at once if you see heavy smoke. If you see a bean fire in a Behmor, its manual says to turn it off and unplug it, keep the door shut until it has cooled, and never use water on it.
- **Stay with it.** Never leave a roast unattended, whatever the machine.
- **Indoors or out.** Both the SR540 and the Behmor manuals say not to use them outdoors. Hoffmann notes that roasting outside in very cold weather can also slow a roast down. Follow your machine's manual.

## Your first roast, step by step

These steps follow the stages Hoffmann describes, with each maker's instructions where they apply.

1. **Set up.** Put the roaster on a stable, heat-resistant surface, with the clearance its manual asks for, near an open window or a range hood that vents outside, and away from stove burners. Empty the chaff collector.
2. **Weigh the green coffee.** Use the batch size in your manual: about 4&nbsp;oz (114&nbsp;g) for the SR540, up to 1&nbsp;lb (454&nbsp;g) for the Behmor. Too few beans and the roaster won't get up to temperature, Fresh Roast notes. Write the weight down.
3. **Start the roast and stay with it.** Watch the color, listen, and smell. For the first few minutes, while the beans dry, little seems to happen.
4. **Note yellowing.** The beans turn yellow and smell of basmati rice and a little bread, and chaff starts to flake off. Write down the time.
5. **Listen for first crack.** It sounds kind of like popcorn, as Jessica Easto puts it. Behmor's manual says the pops may be sporadic or continuous, and the first series lasts about 1 to 1.5 minutes, depending on the beans and how many there are. Write down when it starts. From here, the roast can end at any point.
6. **Decide when to stop.** For a first roast, stop somewhere between the end of first crack and the start of second crack, which comes as a quicker, higher-pitched crackle that Behmor likens to rice cereal in milk. Rob Hoos's starting points for light roasts on his drum roasters are about 1:00 to 1:30 of development after first crack for natural coffees, and 1:20 to 1:45 for washed ones. If you go on into second crack, Behmor's limit is 10 seconds.
7. **Cool the beans at once.** Start the machine's cooling cycle. Hoffmann writes that roasted beans must be cooled quickly to stop them over-roasting or tasting baked. On the SR540, don't remove the chamber or chaff basket until the cycle has finished and the machine has shut off.
8. **Weigh them again.** Weight loss is the difference between the green and roasted weights, as a share of the green weight. If 114&nbsp;g of green coffee comes out at 98&nbsp;g, it has lost 16&nbsp;g, about 14% (calculated). Hoos treats 11–13% as light and 14–16% as medium, and our [roast levels guide](/blog/coffee-roast-levels-explained/) covers the rest of his scale.
9. **Rest the beans.** Fresh Roast suggests letting them cool to room temperature, leaving them for 6 to 12 hours in a container that isn't airtight so they can give off gas, then storing them airtight. Most coffees taste better after a few days' rest.

Keep notes on every roast: the green weight, the times of yellowing, first crack and the end, the weight loss, and how the coffee tastes. Then change one thing at a time. If you have an SR540, wait at least 30 minutes before the next roast, as its manual says.

## Reading your first roasts

Taste is the test, and Hoffmann's rules of thumb are a good start:

- **Very bitter:** you may have roasted too dark.
- **Lacking flavor and sweetness:** you may have roasted too slowly.
- **Very sour, astringent and grassy:** you probably roasted too quickly.

Two more faults are worth knowing. Easto warns that a roast can be too light, without enough time in the heat to develop good flavors, so that it tastes like wood or bread, "and not in a good way." The Specialty Coffee Association describes roasts that are too fast, where the surface of the bean is much darker than the inside, and too slow, where the bean "bakes" and turns brittle, with subdued flavor and often grain notes.

## Planning roasts with Hoos's levers

Once a few roasts have gone well, Rob Hoos's framework is a useful next step. In *Modulating the Flavor Profile of Coffee* he breaks a roast into parts a roaster can steer: the drying phase, the stretch from yellowing to first crack (his MAI, for Maillard-to-first-crack interval), the development time after first crack, and the end temperature. In his cupping experiments, a longer MAI gave a heavier body in most of the coffees he tried, with exceptions he reports; he ties the development time mainly to the balance of acids and the end temperature mainly to caramelization. Our research note on [Maillard, caramelization and Strecker](/deep-brew/research/maillard-caramelization-strecker/) covers how far the evidence goes.

His later book, *Cultivar*, suggests a first-crack time for light roasts by cultivar group, sooner for Typica-group and Ethiopian-group cultivars than for the Bourbon group, and adds a rough rule of thumb for planning the drying phase: multiply your target first-crack time by 0.56 to get roughly when to aim for yellowing. He presents his planning as a recommendation, built on drum roasters, and our [cultivar roast profiles note](/deep-brew/research/cultivar-roast-profiles-hoos/) summarizes it.
