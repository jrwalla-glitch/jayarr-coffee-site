Malawi rarely comes up when people talk about African coffee, and the reason is mostly size. This guide covers how much it grows and who grows it, where, the varieties and processing, how the coffee tastes, and how to find a bag.

## How much coffee Malawi grows

Not much, and the figures are patchy:

- **Hoffmann (2018):** production peaked in the 1990s at 7,000 tonnes and has since shrunk back to around 1,500 tonnes a year. His table gives 18,000 bags for 2016.
- **USDA:** the downloaded series records 21,000–25,000 bags annually from 2010/11 to 2015/16 and 10,000 in 2019/20, its last nonzero entry. Entries for 2020/21 through 2026/27 are zero. Those entries do not establish that Malawi produced no coffee, and we do not use them as a current production estimate.
- **Importers:** Sucafina's Malawi page says the country produces only 1,500 tonnes a year, and Café Imports' page lists 25,000 bags exported annually. Neither page dates its figure.
- **The Mzuzu union:** its own website says its members harvest 400 to 500 tonnes a year, of which about 95 percent is exported.

At 60 kg a bag, 1,500 tonnes is about 25,000 bags, and the union's 400 to 500 tonnes about 6,700 to 8,300 bags (both calculated). An earlier version of this page gave "5,000 to 7,000 bags a year", a figure none of these sources supports.

## Who grows it: estates and smallholders

Malawi's coffee comes from two very different kinds of farm, Hoffmann writes: large commercial estates in the south, and smallholders in the central and northern regions. So a Malawian coffee can be traced either to a single estate or to a large group of producers, and he expects excellent coffees from both.

- **The estates.** Sucafina counts five large estates, mostly in the south, producing around two-thirds of the national crop, although most of them grow tea, macadamia and tobacco as their main crops. It names Sable Farms as the main producer and exporter of washed arabica among them.
- **The smallholders.** Sucafina puts them at 3,000 to 4,000 farmers in six cooperatives, producing the remaining third, and says the cooperatives formed the Mzuzu Coffee Planters Cooperative Union in 1999. The union says its 3,000 member farmers, a quarter of them women, have been growing coffee since the early 1930s.

The Mzuzu union's coffee did well in the African Fine Coffees Association's national Taste of Harvest competition in 2017. Malawi's *Nation* newspaper reported that coffees from eight of the union's zones were among the ten best of the 16 coffees cupped; the other two came from Sable Farming and Satemwa Tea Estate. The winning coffee, the union's smallholder lot, scored 85.33.

## A short history

Coffee seems to have arrived in the late 1800s, Hoffmann writes; one claim has a Scottish missionary, John Buchanan, bringing a single tree from the Edinburgh botanical gardens in 1878. (Café Imports dates the introduction to the 1890s, under British rule.) It first took root around Blantyre in the south, and by 1900 Malawi produced 1,000 tonnes a year. (Sucafina places the early commercial plantings in Thyolo and Mulanje, also in the south.)

Production soon collapsed, which Hoffmann puts down to poorly kept soils, pests and diseases, and competition from Brazil. Under British rule there was little African ownership of the large farms in the first half of the 20th century. A cooperative movement began in 1946 and production grew sharply in the 1950s, but the cooperatives were all dissolved in 1971 through political interference. Sucafina adds that the success of tobacco is one reason coffee never became a staple crop in Malawi, as it did in Kenya and Tanzania.

## Where it grows

Hoffmann writes that Malawian coffees are rarely labeled by region, and that the regions are best thought of as pockets of growing rather than areas with distinct flavors. In the north, those pockets match the Mzuzu union's six cooperatives, whose altitudes the union publishes:

| Cooperative (area) | Altitude, per the union | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Misuku Hills (Chitipa) | 1,700–2,000 m | Borders Tanzania; over half the union's coffee |
| Phoka Hills (Rumphi) | 1,300–2,500 m | Near Lake Malawi and Nyika National Park |
| Viphya Hills | 1,200–1,500 m | North Viphya Plateau |
| Nkhata Bay Highlands | 1,000–2,000 m | East of Mzuzu, above Lake Malawi |
| South East Mzimba | 1,200–1,700 m | South of the Viphya Plateau |
| Ntchisi East | 1,200–1,800 m | The union gives no further detail |

Hoffmann's district altitudes are close to the union's, with Rumphi at 1,200 to 2,500 m. He names Chitipa, home to the Misuku Hills cooperative, as having a reputation for some of the country's best coffee.

## Varieties and processing

Malawi's varieties cover both ends of the quality range, Hoffmann writes: quite a lot of Geisha, the variety behind Panama's auction records, but also quite a lot of Catimor, which resists disease but generally gives a much lower-quality cup. World Coffee Research's entry for Panamanian Geisha notes that "Geisha is also cultivated widely in Malawi"; see our [Gesha guide](/blog/gesha-coffee-worth-the-price/) for what the name does and doesn't guarantee.

The lists differ a little by source:

- **Hoffmann**, for every northern district: Agaro, Geisha, Catimor, Mundo Novo and Caturra. Sucafina gives the same five.
- **Café Imports:** Bourbon, Catimor, Caturra, Gesha/Geisha, Population 3 and SL-28.

Processing is mostly washed. Café Imports lists only washed coffee, and the Mzuzu union says its beans are wet-processed. For what washing involves, see our [processing guide](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/).

Grading has been simple, Hoffmann notes, Grade 1 and Grade 2, though Malawi has been moving toward the AA-style size grades used elsewhere in Africa.

## What Malawian coffee tastes like

Hoffmann's summary: the coffees "can be quite sweet and clean, though rarely as explosively fruity and complex as other coffees from East Africa." The Mzuzu union says the slow maturing in its cool hills gives its coffee a softer, creamier body than coffees from neighboring countries.

These are general descriptions, and what a Malawian coffee *can* taste like, not a promise for a particular bag; a Geisha lot and a Catimor lot from the same district can be very different cups. An earlier version of this page called Malawian coffee "tea-like" and tied that to the country's tea estates; none of the sources here makes that link, so it's gone.

## How to find Malawian coffee

Malawian coffee isn't a standard offering at most roasters, so look for it as a seasonal or limited release.

- **Timing.** Café Imports lists typical arrivals from November to February, a few months after the harvest ends.
- **The name.** A cooperative (Misuku, Phoka, Viphya) or an estate on the bag is a better sign than "Malawi" alone, and a Geisha should say so.
- **The process.** Expect washed coffee; a natural or honey lot would be unusual.
- **Freshness.** A small origin can sit on a shelf. Check the roast date, and our [Freshness tool](/tools/freshness/) shows where a bag sits, from very fresh to stale.

## Brewing it

A sweet, clean washed coffee suits a paper-filter pour-over. Hoffmann writes that paper filters produce the cleanest cup. Start with the Brew Guide's V60 recipe and adjust: if the cup tastes sour, grind a step finer; if it's bitter or drying, a step coarser.
