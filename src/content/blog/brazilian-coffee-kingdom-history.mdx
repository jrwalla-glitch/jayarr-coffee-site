"Coffee made modern Brazil," Mark Pendergrast writes in *Uncommon Grounds*, "but at an enormous human and environmental cost." This is that history, from the first seeds in 1727 to the end of the Vargas era in 1945, told from Pendergrast's book and James Hoffmann's *World Atlas of Coffee*. It explains why Brazil still dominates the world's coffee supply, and why its coffee tastes the way it does; for that side of the story, see our guide to [what Brazilian coffee tastes like](/blog/is-brazilian-coffee-any-good/).

## How coffee reached Brazil

The story usually starts in 1727. Pendergrast tells it this way: the governors of French and Dutch Guiana asked a Portuguese Brazilian official, Francisco de Melo Palheta, to settle a border dispute. Neither would let coffee seeds leave, but Palheta negotiated the border and, in Pendergrast's telling, seduced the French governor's wife, who gave him a farewell bouquet with ripe coffee berries hidden inside. He planted them at home in Pará, in the north. Hoffmann calls the seduction part of the story a myth; the planting in Pará is the part both tell. Either way, Hoffmann writes, the crop stayed small for decades, passed from garden to garden as it worked its way south.

It found its home near Rio de Janeiro. Pendergrast writes that a Belgian monk introduced coffee to the mountains near Rio in 1774, on red soils left unfarmed during the gold and diamond boom. When Napoleon's army took Lisbon in November 1807, the Portuguese royal family fled on British ships to Rio, where, in Pendergrast's account, King John VI declared Brazil a kingdom and handed out coffee seedlings grown at the Royal Botanical Gardens. Brazil became independent in 1822, and in 1840 Pedro II took the throne at fourteen. "Under his long rule," Pendergrast writes, "coffee would become king in Brazil."

## The coffee kingdom was built on slavery

Brazil's first commercial coffee farms, Hoffmann writes, were large plantations worked by enslaved people around the Paraíba River near Rio, where one enslaved worker might look after four to seven thousand coffee plants. When the soil wore out, the plantation moved on to fresh land. Production boomed between 1820 and 1830; by 1830 Brazil grew 30 percent of the world's coffee and by 1840 40 percent. Hoffmann puts the number of enslaved people brought to Brazil to work on the coffee plantations at more than 1.5 million.

Pendergrast traces how the coffee boom drove the slave trade:

- **1825 to 1828:** slave imports rose from 26,254 to 43,555 a year. Well over a million people were enslaved in Brazil by then, nearly a third of its population.
- **1831:** Brazil outlawed importing enslaved people, to placate Britain, and didn't enforce the law. By 1848 slavers were bringing in 60,000 people a year.
- **1850:** after British warships began capturing slave ships, Brazil's legislature truly banned the trade. Some 2 million people already in the country remained enslaved.

Pendergrast quotes a traveler in the Paraíba Valley who described the routine: prayers at four in the morning, work in the fields until seven at night, then household and mill work until nine, when men and women were locked in separate quarters for seven hours before seventeen more hours of almost uninterrupted labor. Beatings and murders went unexamined, children were often sold away from their parents, and owners went armed against retaliation. (An earlier version of this page applied Pendergrast's phrase "dying after an average of seven years" to the coffee plantations; he writes it of the sugar plantations of the 1600s and 1700s.)

The end came slowly. In 1871 Pedro II, who had freed his own slaves more than thirty years before, declared the "law of the free womb," making every child born to an enslaved mother free. Growers and politicians kept fighting abolition. "Brazil is coffee," one member of parliament declared in 1880, "and coffee is the negro." Once the planters concluded that immigrant labor cost them less than slavery, Pendergrast writes, they led the push for abolition themselves. On May 13, 1888, with Pedro II abroad, his daughter, Princess-Regent Isabel, signed the Golden Law, freeing the remaining three-quarters of a million enslaved people. Brazil kept slavery longer than any other country in the Western Hemisphere.

Freedom brought little else. Pendergrast writes that abolition did nothing to improve the lot of Black workers: the planters preferred European immigrants, whom they considered genetically superior, and Brazilians of African descent were pushed further to the margins. A year after abolition, the planters helped depose Pedro II in favor of a republic that the coffee growers of São Paulo and Minas Gerais would run for years. Hoffmann notes the period's name, *café com leite*, coffee with milk, for the power of São Paulo's coffee barons and Minas Gerais's dairy producers.

## A war on the forest

The plantations cost the land too. Pendergrast draws on the historian Warren Dean's *With Broadax and Firebrand*: gangs felled whole hillsides of Atlantic forest at once, let the timber dry and burned it, and planted coffee in the ash, in full sun, in rows running up and down the slopes. When the land was "tired," it was abandoned and more forest was cleared.

## Immigrant colonos after 1884

After the 1850 ban, planters tried other labor. At first, Pendergrast writes, they paid European immigrants' passage and made them work off the debt, and it was illegal to leave a plantation until it was paid, which typically took years: debt peonage. Swiss and German workers revolted in 1856. In 1884 the Paulista planters persuaded the government to pay the passage instead, and between 1884 and 1914 more than a million immigrants, mostly poor Italians, came to work on the coffee farms. Some saved enough to buy land; others earned just enough to go home. Conditions were poor enough that most plantations kept armed guards, *capangas*, and one much-hated owner, Francisco Augusto Almeida Prado, was killed by his workers. When coffee prices slumped after 1900, owners cut wages and took back workers' food plots, and in 1902 Italy banned subsidized emigration to Brazil.

Coffee built the infrastructure around it:

- **1867:** the first Santos railway to a coffee region was completed.
- **1874:** a submarine cable linked Brazil to Europe.
- **1874 to 1889:** track grew from 800 to 6,000 miles. Pendergrast notes the lines ran from the coffee lands to the ports of Santos and Rio, deepening Brazil's dependence on foreign trade rather than tying the country together.
- **1890 to 1901:** production rose from 5.5 million to 16.3 million bags. By 1900 more than 500 million coffee trees grew in São Paulo state, and one German immigrant of the 1880s, Francisco Schmidt, eventually owned twenty huge fazendas with 16 million trees.

## The 1906 Taubaté valorization

By 1901 Brazil was flooding the market. Pendergrast writes that the world grew nearly 20 million bags that year and drank about 15 million; the visible supply, the stocks held by the big trading houses, reached 11.3 million bags, over two-thirds of the year's consumption, and coffee fell to 6 cents a pound. A 1902 conference of producing countries at the New York Coffee Exchange agreed on almost nothing, and in 1903 Brazil effectively banned new planting with a tax.

The answer São Paulo chose was *valorização*: buy the surplus and hold it until prices rose. The steps, in Pendergrast's account:

- **February 25, 1906:** the presidents of the three coffee states, São Paulo, Rio de Janeiro and Minas Gerais, met in Taubaté and signed an agreement to buy surplus coffee and keep it off the market. The federal government declined to take part beyond stabilizing the exchange rate.
- **August 1, 1906:** the Disconto Gesellschaft bank of Berlin lent São Paulo £1 million for a year.
- **October 1906:** a syndicate assembled by the New York coffee broker Hermann Sielcken began buying at Santos at an average of 7 cents a pound, paying 80 percent while São Paulo paid 20 percent. By year's end it held about 2 million bags, a small part of a 20-million-bag crop.
- **December 14, 1906:** a £3 million loan from J. Henry Schroeder & Company of London and National City Bank of New York, whose side Sielcken represented; he reputedly covered 250,000 dollars of it himself.
- **Late 1908:** a £15 million consolidation loan. The remaining stock, nearly 7 million bags, went to a committee of seven with only one São Paulo member. Sielcken was one of the seven, and later called it, before Congress, "the best loan I have ever known."

Prices sat at 6 or 7 cents for a while, then climbed to nearly 11 cents by December 1910 and over 14 cents in 1911, peaking in 1912, the year a Brazilian delegate, J. F. de Assis-Brasil, had predicted at the 1902 conference.

## Sielcken before Congress

American consumers and politicians were furious. In March 1911 Representative George Norris of Nebraska asked the attorney general to investigate "a monopoly in the coffee industry." On May 16, 1912, Sielcken testified to the "Money Trust" subcommittee. Pressed by its counsel, Samuel Untermyer, he said that selling the valorized coffee held in the United States wouldn't make "that much difference," snapping his fingers, and when asked whether the rise from 5 to 14 cents had anything to do with keeping millions of bags off the market, answered "Not that much," snapping them again.

The day after, Attorney General George Wickersham sued Sielcken, the New York Dock Company and the foreign committee members. A court refused to impound the coffee, the State Department pressed for restraint, and in April 1913 the new attorney general, J. C. McReynolds, dropped the suit, despite a memo from the special assistant who had built the case urging him not to. The last valorized coffee in Europe was sold by 1916; nearly 2 million bags sold after the First World War began left their proceeds frozen in Berlin until 1921, when Germany paid Brazil over 125 million marks under the Versailles settlement. Sielcken died in October 1917, days after his US property, worth over 3 million dollars, was seized under the Alien Property Act; it was returned to his widow four years later.

Pendergrast's verdict: the scheme benefited Sielcken and his associates more than Brazil's farmers or government, though it did prevent mass bankruptcies and possibly a revolution. Its perceived success led Brazil to keep trying, and its domination of world coffee, nearly 80 percent in the early 1900s, slowly eroded as other countries planted more.

## More valorizations, and the 1929 crash

Brazil held coffee off the market again in 1917, 3 million bags of it, and in 1921, with a £9 million British-American loan secured on 4.5 million bags. In the 1920s holding coffee back became standing policy: President Artur Bernardes ordered eleven huge warehouses and a policy of sending only as much coffee to the ports as the market wanted, and the São Paulo Coffee Institute, with its own bank, lent against plantations and stored coffee. When a bumper crop of nearly 30 million bags arrived in 1927, the institute borrowed more.

It ended on October 11, 1929, when the institute's broker on the Santos coffee exchange sat silent all day and never bought. The institute was bankrupt. Coffee crashed, Pendergrast notes, two weeks before the New York stock market.

## Vargas and the burning of coffee

The crash ended Brazil's old republic. In October 1930 a military coup put Getúlio Vargas in power, and even the São Paulo coffee barons welcomed it. Coffee had fallen from 22.5 cents a pound in 1929, and would reach 8 cents by 1931; 26 million bags sat in Brazil's warehouses in 1930, a million more than the whole world had drunk the year before. Vargas banned new planting, and his government set out to burn the surplus so that, as Pendergrast puts the plan, the market could "revert to the time-honored law of supply and demand."

In Pendergrast's account:

- **The first year:** Brazil destroyed over 7 million bags, worth some 30 million dollars, and still had millions more.
- **Other uses:** inventors tried to turn coffee into bricks to fuel railroads, and into alcohol, oil, gas, caffeine and cellulose, and later a kind of plastic. Brazil also traded coffee for American wheat from 1931.
- **1937:** Brazil burned 17.2 million bags, when the whole world drank only 26.4 million. Only 30 percent of that year's Brazilian harvest reached the market. Meanwhile Colombia and Central America sold more: Brazil's share of US coffee imports fell from 65 percent at the start of the Depression to just over half by 1937.
- **November 1937:** Vargas declared his *Estado Novo* dictatorship and announced "free competition," opening the floodgates. Prices fell to 6.5 cents, and the burning resumed, more moderately.

Hoffmann puts the total at around 78 million bags of stockpiled coffee burned, and writes that it had little effect on prices. That's about three years of the world's consumption at the 1937 rate (calculated from Pendergrast's 26.4 million bags). Brazil's grip loosened all the same, as Pendergrast's figures show:

| Year | Brazil (million bags) | Rest of the world (million bags) | Brazil's share (calculated) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1906 | over 20 | 3.6 | about 85% |
| 1938 | nearly 22 | 10.2 | about 68% |

## The 1940 quota agreement

With Europe closed by war and coffee at 5.75 cents a pound in September 1940, the lowest price in history by Pendergrast's account, the United States backed a quota deal to keep Latin America's economies, and their politics, away from the Axis. Fourteen producing countries signed the Inter-American Coffee Agreement with the State Department's Sumner Welles on November 20, 1940. It let 15.9 million bags a year into the United States, and gave Brazil not quite 60 percent of the quota and Colombia just over 20 percent. Brazil no longer set the world price alone.

Vargas himself was forced out by the military on October 29, 1945, and in that crisis, Pendergrast writes, Brazil abolished its National Coffee Department.

## After the kingdom: the quota years

Brazil kept trying to manage the market, now with other countries:

- **1962:** after a UN conference in New York, the International Coffee Agreement set a basic export quota of 45.6 million bags, of which Brazil got 18 million and Colombia just over 6 million, Pendergrast writes. Hoffmann notes it came to include 42 producing countries. It wasn't fully in force until 1965.
- **The 1960s:** surpluses persisted; in 1966 Brazil held 65 million of the world's 87 million surplus bags. Researchers working for Nelson Rockefeller's IBEC found that, with zinc, boron, lime and fertilizer, coffee could grow on Brazil's barren *cerrado* lands, where regions such as Cerrado Mineiro grow coffee today.
- **1972 to 1980:** the quota system lapsed on December 11, 1972. After the Black Frost of July 1975 killed one and a half billion trees, well over half of Brazil's, quotas weren't needed again until the end of 1980, when Brazil's share was 25 percent, down from 40 percent in 1962.
- **1986 to 1989:** quotas were suspended in February 1986 when prices ran high, taken up again under a new agreement in October 1987, and ended on July 4, 1989, when the International Coffee Organization suspended all export limits. Hoffmann writes that the agreement broke down after Brazil refused to accept a smaller quota, believing it could prosper outside it; prices fell for years afterward, in the coffee crisis that helped inspire Fair Trade.

## Why Brazil still grows the most coffee

The kingdom left a template. Hoffmann describes Brazil today as the most advanced and industrialized coffee producer in the world, focused on yield, with some farms that grow more coffee than all of Bolivia. Its scale began on the slave plantations and the *colono* fazendas, and its newest regions, like the Cerrado, opened with the research of the 1960s. USDA forecasts Brazil's 2026/27 crop at 71.9 million bags, about 38 percent of the world's 189.7 million (calculated).

The geography also shaped the taste. Because 95 percent of Brazil lies below 3,000 feet, Pendergrast writes, its beans have always tended to lack acidity and body; Hoffmann describes the reputation its specialty producers have spent years fighting as low acidity with more body, best suited to espresso blends. Pendergrast credits the roaster George Howell with starting the Cup of Excellence, with a 2000 competition that put exceptional Brazilian coffees in front of international judges, and Brazil's best lots now sell on their own names.
