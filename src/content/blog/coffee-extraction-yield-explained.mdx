When hot water meets ground coffee, it dissolves part of it. Not all of it: Jonathan Gagné, an astrophysicist who studies brewing, notes that the average extraction can never go above about 30%, because the rest of the bean doesn't dissolve in water. Extraction yield is how much of that you actually got, as a percentage of the coffee you started with.

## How to calculate extraction yield

You need three numbers: the coffee dose, the weight of the drink, and its strength as total dissolved solids (TDS), which you read with a coffee refractometer.

**Extraction yield (%) = drink weight (g) × TDS (%) ÷ coffee dose (g)**

Two worked examples, calculated:

- **Espresso:** an 18 g dose gives a 36 g shot at 10% TDS. That's 36 × 10% = 3.6 g of dissolved coffee, and 3.6 ÷ 18 = **20%**.
- **Pour-over:** 20 g of coffee gives 300 g in the cup at 1.3% TDS. That's 300 × 1.3% = 3.9 g of dissolved coffee, and 3.9 ÷ 20 = **19.5%**.

A few things keep the number honest:

- **Immersion brews** (French press, AeroPress, cold brew): use all the water you added, not the drink weight, because the liquid left in the grounds is as strong as the cup. For example, 30 g of coffee steeped in 500 g of water that reads 1.25% has extracted about 20.8% (calculated: 500 × 1.25% = 6.25 g, ÷ 30). This simple version ignores the dissolved coffee's own weight, so it reads slightly low.
- **Diluted drinks:** for an immersion brew, take the reading before you dilute it. For a pour-over or espresso you dilute afterwards (an americano, bypass water), take the weight and the reading at the same point, either the shot or the whole drink. Adding water doesn't change how much coffee dissolved.
- **The sample:** read a cooled, well-mixed sample. The Extraction Map treats a result under about 10% or over about 26% as a likely mix-up in the weights or the reading, and doesn't place it on the map.

## Strength and extraction are different numbers

**Strength** is TDS: how concentrated the drink is. **Extraction** is how much of the coffee dissolved. They're linked by the ratio, since TDS works out as extraction × dose ÷ drink weight, so at a fixed ratio a better-extracted cup is also a stronger one. That's why the SCA chart draws each coffee-to-water ratio as a diagonal line.

They can still point different ways. Two calculated examples:

- 20 g of coffee giving 200 g of drink at 1.5% TDS has extracted 15%: strong, but below the reference extraction.
- 18 g giving 400 g at 1.05% TDS has extracted about 23.3%: weak, and above the reference extraction.

The SCA chart names those corners "Strong & Underextracted" and "Weak & Overextracted". As the Extraction Map puts it, grind, water temperature and contact time mostly move extraction, while the coffee-to-water ratio mostly moves strength.

Espresso has no SCA chart. The Extraction Map uses the same 18 to 22% for extraction and treats 8 to 12% TDS as a rule of thumb for strength.

## What the 18–22% range means

The SCA's *Coffee Brewing Control Chart*, revised in March 2019 (Version 3), marks an "SCA Ideal" region of 18 to 22% extraction and 1.15 to 1.45% TDS. The older Golden Cup standard, still printed in the SCA's 2018 *Coffee Standards*, uses the same extraction range with a narrower strength band: 1.15 to 1.35% TDS (11.5 to 13.5 g per liter). The Extraction Map uses the 2019 chart.

The chart dates back to the 1950s, and recent research from the UC Davis Coffee Center has found that taste is more complicated than one box:

- Frost, Ristenpart and Guinard (2020) brewed one coffee at three roast levels across a grid of strengths and extractions. Trained tasters found that 21 of 30 attributes changed significantly, including sourness, bitterness and sweetness, and that sweetness fell as strength rose.
- Cotter and colleagues (2021) had 118 mostly college-age black-coffee drinkers rate brews of one medium-roast coffee. They split into two groups whose preferences varied most with strength, and the authors concluded that the chart's single "ideal" should be reconsidered.
- Guinard and colleagues (2023) combined these studies into new charts that show the tasting trends across strength and extraction, and the two preference groups with their different likes and dislikes.

So read 18 to 22% as a reference for comparing brews. A cup outside it isn't a faulty cup if you like it.

## Evenness: an average can hide two extremes

Extraction yield is an average over every particle in the bed. In "The Dynamics of Coffee Extraction", Gagné explains that if some grounds extract much more than others, a cup with a normal-looking average can still carry the bitter, astringent taste of the over-extracted part. (He also suggests the under-extracted part adds vegetal, sour notes, but later called that link speculative.)

That's why he expects a grinder with a narrower spread of particle sizes to let a cup reach a higher average extraction, around 22 to 24% in his examples, before it turns bitter, and why he expects the preferred range to depend on the grinder. He presents those numbers as illustrations, not limits. Uneven flow through the bed does the same thing; our guide to [channeling](/blog/fix-channeling-uneven-extraction/) covers it.

## Without a refractometer

You can brew very good coffee without measuring anything. Under-extracted cups tend to taste sour and hollow, and over-extracted ones bitter and drying, but water, freshness and an uneven bed cause similar tastes, so treat taste as a hypothesis and the refractometer as the number.

These are the next moves the Extraction Map suggests when a measured cup tastes off to you; change one at a time. Without a reading, a sour, thin cup is a reason to try the first row, and a bitter, drying one the second.

| Your reading | Pour-over, drip or immersion | Espresso |
|---|---|---|
| Below the reference extraction | Grind finer, or use hotter water (pour-over or drip) or steep longer (immersion) | Grind finer, or pull a longer shot |
| Above the reference extraction | Grind coarser, or use slightly cooler water (pour-over or drip) or steep for less time (immersion) | Grind coarser, or pull a shorter shot |
| Below the reference strength | More coffee for the same water | A bigger dose for the same yield |
| Above the reference strength | More water for the same coffee, or dilute in the cup | A longer ratio, or dilute in the cup |
