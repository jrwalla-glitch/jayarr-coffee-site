The Wacaco Nanopresso is a portable espresso maker with no batteries and no heater: ground coffee and hot water go in, and a hand pump builds the pressure. In our July 2020 video we unboxed a single-shot Nanopresso, which cost about £70 in the UK at the time, brewed two shots with it and tasted them. This page keeps that review, adds Wacaco's own specifications and instructions, and explains what the pressure figure on the box means.

The base unscrews into a small cup, and the water tank sits inside it. The box holds the Nanopresso, its filter basket, a scoop that doubles as a tamper, a cleaning brush, a cloth pouch, an instruction book, a warranty card and two stickers. Our 2020 unit, in white, came with a zip-up case; Wacaco sells the Nanopresso with or without one.

## How we brewed it, and what Wacaco's manual says

In the video we ground about 10 g of coffee relatively fine, though the scoop holds about 8 g, tamped it hard with the back of the scoop, and filled the tank to the line, about 80 ml, with water at 91 to 95°C. The video converted that as 195 to 200°F; it's about 196–203°F&nbsp;(91–95°C), and the same range our Brew Guide gives for espresso machines. As the instructions said, the first eight pumps built the pressure; the coffee came out over about 20 more, and the second shot took about 25.

Wacaco's instruction book differs in places, and for this brewer the maker's method is the one to start from:

1. **Use one scoop, 8 g.** The manual says to use only one scoop of ground coffee and suggests weighing it on a scale.
2. **Grind just before brewing,** extra-fine on Wacaco's product page, and tamp hard with the back of the scoop.
3. **Preheat.** Run one cycle with hot water only to warm the Nanopresso and your cup.
4. **Add boiling water,** between the 50 ml and 80 ml lines, with the tank on a level surface. The Nanopresso can't heat water, and the manual says never to heat it in the tank.
5. **Pump about once a second.** The first eight strokes build the pressure; then pump slowly until all the coffee is out. Wacaco says most shots take around 30 seconds.

The manual also explains what happens at the top of the pump's range: a valve opens at 18 bar and sends water back into the tank, and Wacaco says that for a good shot you should keep the pressure under 18 bar so the valve doesn't open. Too much coffee, too fine a grind or fast pumping all raise the pressure. If little or no coffee comes out, it suggests slowing the pumping, grinding a little coarser, and with pre-ground coffee tamping more lightly or using slightly less.

## How the shots tasted

We liked it. The first shot was smooth, "buttery smooth," and "quite an enjoyable espresso experience." Reviews of the Nanopresso were mixed then, with some owners calling it closer to an espresso-light than real espresso; to us, it was "extremely close." We made a second shot in a glass to show it, and the crema on top looked great.

We were honest about the gap, too. Placed beside an expertly made coffee-shop espresso, we expected you could probably tell the difference and might prefer the café shot. But on the go, we said, it's "more than close enough." The shot also went too quickly for us, which is part of why Wacaco's double-shot Barista Kit interested us (more on it below).

## What 18 bar and crema do and don't tell you

Wacaco's product page says the Nanopresso can reach a maximum of 18 bar of stable pressure during extraction, "more than what most home espresso machines can deliver." That's the maker's claim about the pump's ceiling. More isn't better for espresso:

- **Machines brew lower.** James Hoffmann puts the desired pressure for espresso at around 9 bar (130 psi). He notes that makers of cheap machines often boast of 15 bar "as if more is somehow better," while an overpressure valve vents the excess.
- **Flow peaks below that.** Scott Rao explains that pressure also compresses the coffee bed: at some point between 7 and 9 bar at the group, flow peaks, and at much higher pressures it slows to a trickle or stops.
- **Wacaco agrees in practice.** Its manual says to keep the Nanopresso under 18 bar, and for its non-pressurized Picopresso it describes reaching 9 to 12 bar during extraction.

In the video we took the crema as a sign that the Nanopresso was making enough pressure. Hoffmann's account is more limited: crema tells you roughly how fresh the coffee is and how strong the shot is, not whether the coffee, the roast or the extraction is good. The Nanopresso also ships with a pressurized head, which Wacaco says helps extraction and consistency when the grind isn't precise; it lists "easy crema" among the features. Our [espresso explainer](/blog/what-espresso-actually-is/) covers what crema is.

So treat the taste, not the foam or the bar figure, as the test. For our part, we'd tried a few cheap espresso makers that cost under £100, and in our view the Nanopresso made a better espresso than they did.

## Accessories and Wacaco's other models

These are as listed on Wacaco's store, checked October 18, 2026:

- **Barista Kit ($32.90 listed):** two 16 g double baskets, an extra 8 g basket, a tamper, an adapter ring, a larger 140 ml water tank and a bigger cup. Wacaco's chart gives a double espresso from 16 g and 80 ml of water, about 60 ml in the cup, and a lungo up to 120 ml. Its manual recommends using the kit with the pressurized head removed, with a slightly finer grind. The video noticed the double-shot version on the box and suggested topping a double up with hot water for an americano.
- **NS Adapter ($21.90 listed):** for Nespresso Original capsules and most compatibles. It wasn't part of our 2020 review.
- **DG Kit ($32.90 listed):** for Nescafé Dolce Gusto capsules.
- **Picopresso ($129.90 listed):** Wacaco calls it its most pro-oriented portable: a non-pressurized 52 mm basket for 18 g, 350 g, 80 ml of water, and the same 18-bar maximum. It asks more of your grinding and technique.
- **Minipresso:** the original Minipresso GR is rated to 8 bar; the current Minipresso GR2, listed at $54.90 on sale, has an adjustable 8 to 12 g basket and an 18-bar maximum.

## Practical points

- **You need hot water.** Bring a kettle or a flask, or use a hotel kettle; the tank isn't for heating water.
- **A grinder helps.** Wacaco's instructions assume coffee ground just before brewing. A small hand grinder that can grind fine enough for espresso makes it a travel kit; our [manual grinder guide](/blog/top-10-best-manual-coffee-grinders/) compares the makers' specs.
- **Cleaning.** We found it a few more parts than we'd like to clean, though the grounds came out as a neat, compact puck. Wacaco says to rinse the basket, portafilter and cup under running water after each use, deep-clean the portafilter and nozzle about every 20 shots, and clean the filter mesh every six months or 300 shots. No part goes in the dishwasher.
- **Weight.** At 336 g it's light for an espresso maker, but not light for ultralight hiking.

## Who it suits

The Nanopresso's selling point is portability: espresso where there's no machine, from a few simple parts. We thought it well made, with just the right weight, though slightly on the pricier side. Reviews were mixed, so we suggested doing your own research first; if you want espresso that's quick, easy and portable, it's worth a look.

It doesn't replace an espresso machine at home, and it doesn't steam milk. If a kitchen setup is what you need, start with a machine and an espresso grinder together.
