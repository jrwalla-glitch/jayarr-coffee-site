In Central America, Nicaragua grows less coffee than Honduras or Guatemala but about twice as much as Costa Rica, on USDA's 2026/27 forecasts (calculated). For years it was known more for volume than for single farms: the importer Café Imports says Nicaragua has mainly been a source of container-sized Fair Trade and organic coffee for it, and that it is now looking for single-producer lots and even microlots.

## What Nicaraguan coffee tastes like

James Hoffmann, in *The World Atlas of Coffee*, writes that Nicaraguan coffees show a range of flavors and are typically quite complex, capable of pleasing fruit-like flavors and clean acidity.

Our 2019 video describes Nicaraguan coffee as balanced, with vanilla and nut flavors, a fruity sweetness and a low acidity. It adds that higher grades often lean toward bright, high-toned notes like floral and citrus, rather than the deeper apricot, chocolate and papaya notes of other lots. These are tendencies, not a promise for every bag:

| Kind of lot | What it can taste like |
|---|---|
| Top specialty lots | Bright floral, citrus, tropical fruit |
| Everyday specialty | Chocolate, cocoa, subtle stone fruit, caramel at a medium roast |
| Commercial grades | Nutty, mild, clean and straightforward |

Importers and roasters describe it in similar terms. Coffee Origins quotes one importer's washed regional blend from Jinotega and Matagalpa as dark chocolate, red fruit and tangerine, and caramel came up in four of the seven Nicaraguan lots in its sample of roasters' descriptions from January to March 2026.

## How much Nicaragua grows

USDA forecasts Nicaragua's 2026/27 crop at 2.44 million 60 kg bags, 12th among the producing countries on its table and about 1.3 percent of the world's coffee (both calculated from USDA's figures). Its Managua office expects the crop to dip, because a likely El Niño could bring drought and fertilizer costs rose about 25 percent in early 2026.

| Marketing year | Production (million 60 kg bags) |
|---|---|
| 2021/22 | 2.84 |
| 2022/23 | 2.59 |
| 2023/24 | 2.35 |
| 2024/25 | 2.66 |
| 2025/26 | 2.56 |
| 2026/27 | 2.44 (forecast) |

It is almost all arabica: 2.30 million of the 2.44 million bags in the 2026/27 forecast, about 94 percent (calculated); the rest is robusta. The government opened lowland areas on the Atlantic and Pacific coasts to robusta in 2013, but USDA reports that robusta has stagnated at around 140,000 to 160,000 bags a year.

About 45,000 growers farm about 143,000 hectares of coffee, and USDA reports the sector employs more than 330,000 people along the value chain. In the 2011 agricultural census, farms with less than 15 hectares of coffee were 71 percent of farms but grew 37 percent of the crop, while large farms over 70 hectares were 7 percent of farms and grew 36 percent.

Most of it is exported. The United States bought 35 percent of Nicaragua's coffee exports in 2024/25, mostly high-quality arabica for specialty roasters, USDA says; the European Union took about 32 percent, with European buyers particularly seeking organic and Fair Trade coffees.

## A short history

Hoffmann writes that Catholic missionaries brought coffee to Nicaragua in 1790, and that it gained economic importance around 1840, with the first commercial plantations around Managua. By 1870 it was the country's main export, and laws in 1879 and 1889 paid planters to plant more trees on large farms.

The late 20th century was hard on the industry. After the 1979 revolution, the US-backed Contra rebels targeted coffee, attacking vehicles carrying farm workers and sabotaging mills. Coffee was still Nicaragua's main export in 1992, but the 1999–2003 price crash did enormous damage: Hoffmann notes that three of the country's six largest banks collapsed because of their exposure to coffee. Hurricane Mitch in 1998 and a drought around 2000 made it worse. The importer Café Imports adds that the long revolutionary period and Mitch kept Nicaragua out of the specialty spotlight just as its neighbors were entering it, and that USAID and Fair Trade work helped unite small producers into cooperatives.

Leaf rust struck in 2013/14, an outbreak that USDA says decimated production; growers have replanted about 20,000 hectares since. Hoffmann writes that traceability, once poor, is now very high, and that more farmers are focusing on quality.

## Where it grows

USDA puts more than 85 percent of Nicaragua's arabica farms in three northern departments, Jinotega, Matagalpa and Nueva Segovia, at 365 to 1,500 m; the rest grow in Carazo and around Managua. Hoffmann's regional tables:

| Region | Altitude | Harvest | Varieties Hoffmann lists |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jinotega | 1,100–1,700 m | Dec–Mar | Caturra, Bourbon |
| Matagalpa | 1,000–1,400 m | Dec–Feb | Caturra, Bourbon |
| Nueva Segovia | 1,100–1,650 m | Dec–Mar | Caturra, Bourbon |

He also names smaller regions, including Madriz, Managua, Boaco and Carazo, that produce some excellent coffees. Coffee Origins draws a wider harvest, October to March, and a 600–1,500 m band from importers' figures.

### Jinotega

Hoffmann calls Jinotega the primary producing region, and its economy has long depended on coffee. Its coffees can be clean and balanced, with chocolate, vanilla and nut notes, good sweetness and a hint of citrus. That makes it a good first Nicaraguan coffee.

### Matagalpa

Matagalpa's coffee comes from a mix of estates and cooperatives, Hoffmann writes, and its capital has a coffee museum. Its coffees can be fruitier than Jinotega's, with stone fruit, berries and sometimes a wine-like note in lighter roasts.

### Nueva Segovia

On the northern border with Honduras, Nueva Segovia has begun to distinguish itself for some of the very best coffees in the country, Hoffmann writes, with a great deal of success at the Cup of Excellence. Café Imports also sees great potential there, citing its higher altitudes and producers' growing interest in quality varieties and experimental processing. Its coffees can be more nuanced, with floral notes and a livelier acidity.

### Madriz

One of the smaller northern regions Hoffmann names. Its coffees can be bold and full-bodied, with dark chocolate and brown sugar.

## Varieties

USDA reports that Caturra covers 72 percent of Nicaragua's arabica area. Other common varieties are Bourbon, Pacas, Catuai, Catimor, Maragogype and Pacamara, and growers have been getting access to higher-yielding varieties such as Marsellesa, Parainema, Costa Rica 95, IHCAFE 90 and Lempira. Here's how World Coffee Research (WCR) rates some of them:

| Variety | What it is | WCR ratings: yield; quality potential at high altitude; leaf rust |
|---|---|---|
| Caturra | Bourbon mutation; compact | Medium; Good; susceptible |
| Bourbon | Old line from Yemen via Réunion; tall | Medium; Very Good; susceptible |
| Catuai | Mundo Novo × Caturra; compact | Medium; Good; susceptible |
| Maragogype | Typica mutation with very large beans | Low; Very Good; susceptible |
| Pacamara | Pacas × Maragogipe, from El Salvador | Low; Exceptional; susceptible |
| Marsellesa | Sarchimor selected in Nicaragua by ECOM and CIRAD, released in 2009 | High; Good; intermediate resistance |
| Parainema | Sarchimor; compact | Very High; Good; highly resistant |

One variety has Nicaraguan roots. WCR describes Maracaturra as most likely a natural cross of Caturra and Maragogipe in Nicaragua: the Nicaraguan Institute of Agricultural Technology (INTA) began selecting it in 1976; the revolution interrupted the work, later selections continued privately, and the variety has never been stabilized.

You'll see these on bags from competition lots. In the 2026 Cup of Excellence, the top-placed coffees included Geisha, Maragogype, Maracaturra, Java, Parainema, Catuai, Caturra and Pacamara. Our [variety guide](/blog/coffee-varietal-guide/) explains how the families relate.

## Processing

Washed is the norm: it's the main method in Café Imports' profile, with other processes on occasion. At the top end, the 2026 Cup of Excellence ranked naturals and honeys separately from washed coffees. Our [processing guide](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/) explains how each changes the cup.

## Nicaragua at the Cup of Excellence

The Alliance for Coffee Excellence lists Nicaraguan Cup of Excellence competitions from 2002 to 2026, with a few years missing. In 2026:

- **Washed:** the winner was a Geisha from El Cambalache in Dipilto, at 91.44 points.
- **Natural and honey:** the winner was a natural Geisha from Los Alpes, also in Dipilto, at 92.00.
- **Where the winners came from:** Dipilto and Mozonte, both in Nueva Segovia, dominated both lists, with lots from Jinotega, San Rafael del Norte and Matagalpa among the washed winners.

That's Hoffmann's point about Nueva Segovia, in numbers. Our guide to [coffee scores](/blog/coffee-scoring-system-explained/) explains what those numbers mean.

## What to look for on a bag

- **A region, and ideally a municipality or farm.** "Nicaragua" alone tells you little; Jinotega, Matagalpa or Nueva Segovia (or a place like Dipilto) tells you more.
- **A producer, cooperative or farm.** Hoffmann says you should be able to find coffees traceable to single estates, producer groups or cooperatives.
- **The variety.** A Pacamara, Maragogype or Bourbon rates higher for quality potential in WCR's catalog than Catimor-type varieties such as Lempira or IHCAFE 90; it's a clue, not a verdict.
- **Certifications, if they matter to you.** Café Imports has bought mainly Fair Trade and organic coffee in Nicaragua, and USDA notes that European buyers seek both; our [certifications guide](/blog/coffee-certifications-decoded/) explains what each one checks.
- **Grades.** Café Imports lists SHG (Strictly High Grown) and HG (High Grown). Cutoffs vary by country, and a grade says less than a named farm.
- **The roast date.** Our [freshness guide](/blog/coffee-freshness-roast-to-stale/) explains what it tells you.

If you like [Colombian](/blog/single-origin-colombian-coffee-what-flavor-notes-to-expect/) or [Honduran](/blog/honduras-coffee-guide-marcala-comayagua/) coffee, a washed Nicaraguan from Jinotega makes an easy comparison; try them side by side, as our guide to [single-origin coffee](/blog/what-is-single-origin-coffee/) suggests.
