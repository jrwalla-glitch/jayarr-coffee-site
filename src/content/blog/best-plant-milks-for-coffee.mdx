## What to read on the carton

Three things on a carton tell you most about how it will behave in coffee:

1. **A steaming claim.** A barista carton's maker says it froths, foams or steams. That promise is the best single guide, because the same base milk can come in a plain carton and a barista one.
2. **Fat and gums.** The oat barista cartons on our oat pages and Ripple's pea blend add a vegetable oil; the almond, coconut and macadamia ones below get their fat from the nuts or coconut cream. All four non-oat barista cartons below add thickening gums, such as gellan, guar or xanthan gum, or carrageenan. In the lab comparison below, most plant milks were more viscous than cow's milk, which the authors put down to added polysaccharide thickeners; some also used lecithin or saponin as emulsifiers.
3. **A phosphate.** Several barista cartons add one. Oatly says it adds dipotassium phosphate to its Barista Edition "as an acidity regulator" that keeps it from separating in hot drinks; our [oat milk label test](/blog/your-6-dollar-oat-milk-is-wrecking-your-latte/) covers that.

Protein is the number people look for, but it predicts less than you'd think. In a 2025 study of plant drinks sold as barista versions, Tarasov and colleagues found that foaming didn't track protein content; our oat guide has their figures. The pea carton below is a good example: Ripple's plain pea milk is sold on its protein, but its barista blend lists 2 g of protein a cup, with sunflower oil second on its ingredient list.

## How plant milks foam: one lab comparison

The most direct comparison we found is a 2024 study by Lena Hassan and colleagues, who frothed cow's and goat's milk and six plant milks (almond, oat, soy, pea, coconut and rice) with an electric frother, cold and hot, and measured how much foam each made and how long it lasted. From the paper's abstract:

- **Frothed hot,** the three plant milks that foamed best were oat, pea and soy, though the animal milks made more foam than any of them.
- **Frothed cold,** oat, soy and almond beat cow's milk on both foam volume and foam lifetime.
- **Oat** made the longest-lasting foam of the eight, and **rice** the least foam.

The abstract doesn't name the brands, and one carton of a milk type isn't every carton. Treat the results as a guide to the milk types, and the maker's claim as the guide to a particular carton.

## Soy

Soy milk can be high in protein: Pacific's Original Ultra Soy lists 10 g a cup. It is also one of the three plant milks that foamed best hot in Hassan's comparison, and one of three that beat cow's milk cold.

Its catch is curdling. In a 2019 lab study of soymilk in instant coffee, Mairi Brown and colleagues found that curdling depended mainly on how hot the mixture was and how much soymilk was in it, not on a clear acidity threshold; our [oat milk label test](/blog/your-6-dollar-oat-milk-is-wrecking-your-latte/) summarizes what they found and what helps.

Pacific makes no coffee claim for Ultra Soy, and we didn't find a soy milk sold for steaming on Pacific's or Silk's US websites when we checked. If your store has a barista soy, its own steaming claim and an acidity regulator on the label are the things to look for.

## Pea

**Ripple Barista Blend** is a pea-protein milk made for coffee. Ripple says it is "specially designed to foam and froth like dairy," that it foams "either hot or cold," and that you can foam it with a handheld frothing wand or a countertop frother instead of a steam wand. Its label lists 8 g of fat and 2 g of protein a cup, from water, high oleic sunflower oil, cane sugar and pea protein, with dipotassium phosphate, carrageenan and gellan gum.

That's a different drink from Ripple's regular milks, which Ripple sells on their "8g of satisfying protein." Pea was one of the three best hot foamers in Hassan's comparison.

## Almond

**Pacific Barista Series Original Almond** is the barista almond milk here. Pacific says it was "designed by professional baristas" and "steams like dairy." Its label lists 4 g of fat and 1 g of protein a cup, from water, cane sugar and ground almonds, with carrageenan, gellan gum, potassium and sodium citrates and lecithin. It lists no phosphate.

In Hassan's comparison, almond wasn't among the three plant milks that foamed best hot, but it beat cow's milk when frothed cold, so it may suit iced drinks with cold foam.

## Coconut

**Califia Coconut Barista Blend** is made, in Califia's words, "to froth, foam, and steam like dairy milk," with "the added creaminess of coconut." Its label lists 5 g of fat a cup, 4.5 g of it saturated, and 1 g of protein, from a coconut-cream base with cane sugar, coconut water concentrate, tricalcium phosphate, fava bean protein and three gums: xanthan, gellan and guar.

Califia sells it as tasting of coconut, so expect the flavor in the cup. Hassan's abstract doesn't report how coconut milk foamed.

## Macadamia

**milkadamia Barista**, its maker says, "produces delicate, velvety foam with a steam wand" and is "formulated for microfoam and latte art." Its label lists 4.5 g of fat and 1 g of protein a cup, with 7 g of added sugar, and adds calcium and potassium phosphates, sunflower lecithin and gellan gum. Macadamia wasn't one of the milks in Hassan's study.

An earlier version of this page also ranked hemp and potato milks. We couldn't check a US maker's barista label for either, so they're no longer covered here.

## Steaming a plant milk

Steam a barista plant milk the way you'd steam dairy, in two phases: add the air first, while the milk is cold, then heat it. Scott Rao's rule in *The Professional Barista's Handbook* is to keep the steam tip just below the surface and finish stretching before the milk reaches 100°F&nbsp;(38°C), then sink the tip so the milk rolls as it heats. James Hoffmann's *World Atlas of Coffee* gives the same order: air first, then heat.

Neither book covers plant milks, and none of the four barista makers above gives a steaming temperature on its product page. Ripple's advice for a handheld frother is simply to heat the milk "to the desired temperature" and froth. Our [steaming guide](/blog/how-to-steam-milk-for-lattes/) has the full method and the dairy temperatures; start there, and adjust by taste and texture.

Pour straight away. Rao's rule for dairy is to pour within 30 seconds of steaming, because the texture degrades even when you spin the pitcher; in his photo, a café latte has fully separated two minutes after it was made.

## Iced drinks

Foam matters less over ice, so choose by taste. If you want cold foam, Hassan's results favor oat, soy and almond, which all beat cow's milk when frothed cold, and Ripple says its barista blend foams cold too. For the coffee itself, our [cold brew concentrate guide](/blog/cold-brew-concentrate-guide/) has a recipe.
