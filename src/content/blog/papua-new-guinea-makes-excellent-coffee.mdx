Papua New Guinea doesn't get the attention of Ethiopia or Colombia, partly because so much of its coffee is grown in small gardens in remote highlands, where, Hoffmann notes, many growers lack proper post-harvest facilities and coffee is hard to trace. The traceable lots are worth seeking out. Here's what they taste like, where they grow, and how to read a PNG bag.

## What PNG coffee tastes like

Our 2019 video describes Papua New Guinean coffee as combining sweet tangs with a low-toned fruity richness: medium acidity, a heavy body and an earthy flavor, with a bright, clean finish and a zesty, fruity aroma. It names mango and papaya as notes that PNG single-origin coffees commonly show.

Hoffmann's description in *The World Atlas of Coffee* is shorter: great coffees from PNG often have a buttery quality, great sweetness and wonderful complexity. The country's Coffee Industry Corporation (CIC), which profiled coffees province by province, lists sweet, chocolate and fruit notes everywhere, with differences in emphasis:

| Province | What CIC's profiles describe |
|---|---|
| **Western Highlands** | Nutty, toffee, chocolate and caramel aromas; sweet fruit, citrus, tropical fruit; smooth and balanced |
| **Eastern Highlands** | Chocolate and spice aromas; a tea-like body, a lemon note and a sweet, fruity, lingering aftertaste |
| **Simbu (Chimbu)** | Fruit, floral, chocolate and caramel aromas; tropical fruit, melon, honey, pineapple and blackcurrant; juicy and complex |

These are what a PNG coffee *can* taste like, not a promise for any one bag.

### Not Indonesia's twin

PNG shares an island with Indonesia's Papua provinces, and its coffees are often filed alongside Indonesia's. Hoffmann argues against that: many people would associate Papua New Guinea's coffees with Indonesia's, he writes, "but it would be unfair to do so," since PNG rightly stands apart and shares relatively little with neighboring Papua when it comes to coffee. There isn't one Indonesian taste to compare it with anyway: our [Indonesian coffee guide](/blog/what-does-indonesian-coffee-taste-like/) shows how much there depends on the island and on wet-hulling, while Café Imports lists washed processing for PNG.

## How much PNG grows

USDA forecasts 800,000 60&nbsp;kg bags for 2026/27, of which 750,000 are arabica, about 94 percent (calculated). That's about 0.4 percent of USDA's world total (calculated), so the video's description of PNG as "one of the world's largest producers of high-grade coffee" is generous on volume. USDA's estimates since 2021/22 run from 775,000 bags to 980,000 in 2023/24. CIC puts the split at about 95 percent arabica, grown inland at altitude, and 5 percent robusta, grown mostly in the lowlands of the Sepik region.

Most of it comes from small farms. Hoffmann writes that 95 percent of producers are smallholders, often subsistence farmers, and that they grow around 90 percent of the country's coffee. Café Imports counts about 280,000 smallholder farmers with gardens of around 1 to 2 hectares, where coffee grows alongside whatever else a family needs. CIC writes that almost half of rural households grow and harvest coffee.

## Where it grows

Most PNG coffee comes from the central highlands. Hoffmann's atlas gives these regions, all harvested from April to September, with Bourbon, Typica and Arusha as the varieties:

| Region | Altitude | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Western Highlands** | 1,000–1,800 m | Around Mount Hagen; CIC puts the province at about 42–46 percent of PNG's production, the largest share |
| **Eastern Highlands** | 400–1,900 m | Around Goroka and Kainantu; CIC calls it the second-largest producer |
| **Simbu (Chimbu)** | 1,300–1,900 m | Third-largest, mostly from coffee gardens around smallholders' homes |

CIC notes that Jiwaka province was created from part of Western Highlands around 2012, so newer bags may name it. Hoffmann notes that much Western Highlands coffee is milled in Goroka, in the Eastern Highlands, which can make it hard to trace.

Café Imports gives a main harvest from May to September with a small fly crop in January and February, and typical arrivals in October and November. Our [harvest calendar](/blog/coffee-harvest-calendar/) has PNG beside the other origins.

## How coffee came to the highlands

CIC dates coffee in what is now PNG to the late 1800s, in the German colony of New Guinea and in Papua, where Catholic missionaries may have planted arabica on Yule Island around 1885. Most early plantings were experimental. Serious commercial planting came in the 1920s, and accounts of the highland seed differ:

- **Hoffmann** writes that in 1926, 18 estates were established using seeds from Jamaica's Blue Mountain, and that production began in earnest by 1928. Café Imports also dates the arabica to the 1920s, as Typica brought from Jamaica.
- **CIC** traces the highland seed to an experimental station at Wau, in Morobe, planted in 1928, and to the Aiyura research station, set up in 1937, which planted the first highland coffee from Wau seed and distributed seed across the highlands.

Either way, "Blue Mountain" on a PNG bag means Typica: World Coffee Research lists Blue Mountain as another name for Typica, the variety grown in Jamaica as Jamaica Blue Mountain. WCR rates Typica's quality potential at high altitude as Very Good, with low yields and high susceptibility to leaf rust; our [varietal guide](/blog/coffee-varietal-guide/) has the full family tree.

Hoffmann describes the industry's growth in the 1950s, when infrastructure was built to move coffee around the island, and again in the 1970s with government programs encouraging cooperatives. Since the 1980s, he writes, low prices have left many estates in trouble, and production has shifted to smallholders, who are less exposed to market swings.

## Grades and traceability

PNG grades its exports by quality. Hoffmann and CIC give the main grades, in descending order, as AA, A, X, PSC and Y: the first three are estate grades and the last two smallholder grades, with PSC standing for Premium Smallholder Coffee. Café Imports notes that the grades take in bean color, shape, defects, aroma and cup quality, not just screen size.

Traceability is the weak point. Hoffmann writes that there isn't a long history of it, that in the past some farms bought coffee from other producers to pass off as their own, and that selling coffee by region is fairly new. His advice is to look for coffees traceable to a specific estate or a group of producers. Some large estates still operate, so single-estate lots exist.

## Buying and brewing PNG coffee

On a bag, look for:

- **The region:** Western Highlands, Eastern Highlands, Simbu or Jiwaka.
- **The grade:** an estate grade (A or AA) or PSC for smallholder coffee.
- **A named estate, washing station or producer group,** which is the best sign of a traceable lot.
- **The process:** washed is the norm; Café Imports lists no other.

The video suggests a steel-filter dripper or a French press to keep the coffee's rich texture. Hoffmann writes that a French press's metal mesh lets a little of the coffee's oil and tiny particles into the cup, which gives a bigger, richer body. A paper filter gives a cleaner cup; our [French press guide](/blog/how-to-use-a-french-press-the-most-amazing-coffee-youve-ever-had/) has the full method.
