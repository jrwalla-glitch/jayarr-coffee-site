Coffee bags are covered in seals: Fair Trade, Rainforest Alliance, USDA Organic, Bird Friendly. They all suggest you're doing something good by buying this bag over the one next to it. But what does each one guarantee, who checks it, and how much reaches the farmer?

After digging into the standards, the audits and the economics behind each label, I've found the answers are more complicated than the seals suggest. Each one does a specific job, and none of them does every job. Below, each seal is described from its certifier's own current rules, with the date I checked them.

## The seals at a glance

| Seal | What it certifies | Price floor? |
|---|---|---|
| Fairtrade | A minimum price and a premium for small-farmer groups | Yes |
| Fair Trade Certified (Fair Trade USA) | A minimum price and a premium; farms of any size | Yes |
| USDA Organic | No prohibited substances for three years before harvest | No |
| Rainforest Alliance | Its Sustainable Agriculture Standard | No; a negotiated premium |
| Bird Friendly | Organic, no deforestation, and habitat kept | No |
| Direct Trade | Nothing: a roaster's own claim | No |

## Fairtrade (Fairtrade International)

**What it certifies:** a minimum price for the coffee, plus a premium on top that the farmers' organization decides how to spend. Fairtrade International's coffee standard is written for small-scale producer organizations, cooperatives whose members are mostly smallholders who farm mainly with their own and their family's labor. FLOCERT, which describes itself as the global certification body for Fairtrade, does the auditing.

**The prices:** Fairtrade quotes them in US dollars per pound, free on board (FOB, the price at the port of export). The minimum applies only when the market is lower: when market prices are above the minimum, as they have been, the market price prevails, and the premium is paid on top of whichever is higher. The organic differential is added the same way.

| Per pound, FOB, as published (checked October 15, 2026) | Since August 1, 2023 | Contracts signed from December 1, 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Washed arabica, minimum | $1.80 | $2.00 |
| Natural arabica, minimum | $1.75 | $1.95 |
| Washed robusta, minimum | $1.25 | $1.35 |
| Natural robusta, minimum | $1.20 | $1.30 |
| Fairtrade Premium | $0.20 | $0.20 |
| Organic differential | $0.40 | $0.40 |

The new prices and the 2023 washed arabica price are Fairtrade's own figures; the other 2023 values are calculated from the increases Fairtrade gives in its FAQ (20 cents for natural arabica, 10 cents for each robusta). Fairtrade says it reviews these prices every four years, with an inflation update halfway through, so check the date on any figure you read, including these.

**What it gets right:** the price floor is a safety net for the years when the market crashes, which it has done before. Fairtrade itself says that while coffee prices have recently been above the minimum, markets stay volatile and many farmers lack tools to protect themselves when prices fall. The premium also funds farm, cooperative and community investments: Fairtrade reports that its coffee cooperatives earned €57 million in premium in 2024.

**Where it falls short:** in a high market the floor does nothing, and for specialty lots that sell well above commodity prices it rarely comes into play. The cooperative model also means estates can't join, and James Hoffmann notes in *The World Atlas of Coffee* that critics question both whether the money always reaches the farmer and whether the model gives farmers a reason to improve quality.

## Fair Trade USA

Fair Trade USA is a separate organization with its own seal, "Fair Trade Certified", and its own rules. The difference matters on a bag sold in the US.

**Who can be certified:** its Agricultural Production Standard covers "different sizes of farms and processing facilities" under one set of requirements. A certificate can cover a single farm or a group, such as a cooperative or "an exporter buying from independent farms", and its requirements are scaled by the number of workers on site. So, unlike Fairtrade International's coffee standard, it can certify estates that employ hired workers. Fair Trade USA and its partner certification bodies do the audits.

**The prices:** Fair Trade USA's published coffee terms are a minimum of $1.40 a pound for washed arabica and $1.05 for washed robusta, a premium of $0.20 a pound and an organic differential of $0.30 a pound, all checked on its site October 15, 2026. Those figures come from its 2023 announcement. On October 16, 2026, Fair Trade USA announced new minimums for contracts signed on or after February 1, 2027: $2.00 a pound for washed arabica, $1.95 for natural arabica, $1.35 for washed robusta and $1.30 for natural robusta. The current minimums stay in force until then, and the premium and organic differential don't change (checked October 18, 2026).

## USDA Organic

**What it certifies:** that the coffee was grown and handled under the USDA's National Organic Program rule (7 CFR part 205). The core of it for a farm:

- **Three years clean:** no prohibited substances applied to the land for the three years before the harvest, with defined boundaries and buffer zones to keep drift from neighboring farms off the crop (§205.202).
- **What "organic" on the label means:** "100 percent organic" means exactly that; "organic" means at least 95 percent organic ingredients; "made with organic" means at least 70 percent (§205.301). A bag of plain coffee is a single ingredient.
- **Inspections:** a certifying agent inspects every certified operation on site once a year, and makes unannounced inspections of at least 5 percent of the operations it certifies each year (§205.403).

**What it doesn't certify:** price or wages, and it doesn't impose Bird Friendly's specific shade or forest-conservation criteria. The rule does require farms to maintain or improve natural resources, which it defines to include wetlands, woodlands and wildlife, but its crop requirements don't call for shade trees; that's what Bird Friendly adds. It doesn't certify flavor either: an organic coffee can be stale or poorly roasted like any other. For whether organic changes the cup, see our [guide to organic coffee](/blog/the-truth-about-organic-coffee-and-best-coffees/).

**The cost question:** certification costs money and paperwork, and some of that falls on cooperatives. Fairtrade's 2026 price review lists the cost of organic certification among the operating expenses felt at cooperative level, and Fairtrade set its organic differential by comparing what organic and conventional farms spend.

## Rainforest Alliance

**What it certifies:** that farms meet the Rainforest Alliance's Sustainable Agriculture Standard. The organization says its green frog seal means farms and companies "support healthy forests, better farmer livelihoods, human rights, good environmental practices, as well as climate resilience." The Rainforest Alliance merged with UTZ in 2018, and the merged program carries the Rainforest Alliance name.

**Who checks:** independent certification bodies that the Rainforest Alliance authorizes, and a certificate holder with farming in scope is audited every year.

**The money:** "Rainforest Alliance certification does not guarantee a minimum price for certified crops," in its own words. Instead, buyers of certified crops pay certified farms a premium. Since October 2025 that's a single premium paid in cash, by volume, under signed agreements, and for most sectors its amount is negotiated between farmers and companies. Cocoa keeps a set minimum; coffee isn't among the exceptions.

**How much of the bag is certified:** under the current Labeling and Claims Policy (version 1.2, October 14, 2026), a single-ingredient product such as coffee can carry the seal only if it physically contains at least 90 percent certified content by weight, through identity-preserved or segregated supply chains. Coffee isn't on the list of crops allowed to use the mass-balance route. If you've read that the frog needs only 30 percent certified coffee, as an earlier version of this article said, that's not the rule today.

**A newer seal:** the Rainforest Alliance also has a Regenerative Agriculture certification, which it describes as its sustainable standard plus "additional requirements for soil health, biodiversity, and climate resilience." It's a separate seal, so read which one is on the bag.

## Smithsonian Bird Friendly

**What it certifies:** coffee (and now cocoa) grown in a way that keeps habitat for birds. The Smithsonian's current criteria, as it summarizes them:

- **Organic certification**, or a participatory guarantee system.
- **Zero deforestation** in the past 10 years.
- **Biodiversity, by one of two pathways.** An agroforestry system: shade cover of 40 percent for coffee, 10 shade-tree species per hectare and 60 percent native trees. Or forest conservation: a forest-to-crop ratio of 2:3, a stable governance and management plan, and tenure over primary or secondary forest.
- **Traceability:** certified coffee kept separate and labeled through processing and storage, with documentation.

It also recommends practices such as several canopy layers, ground cover, limited pruning of shade trees and buffers along streams. Older descriptions of Bird Friendly, including earlier versions of this article, describe a single shade-only route; the forest-conservation pathway is newer.

**Why habitat is the point:** the case for shade standards rests on field research, not just good intentions. In one study, Stacy Philpott and colleagues at the Smithsonian Migratory Bird Center compared eight coffee cooperatives in Chiapas, Mexico, that were organic, organic and fair trade, or uncertified. They found no differences in vegetation or in ant or bird species richness by certification, and none of the cooperatives would have passed the shade standard, yet the vegetation measures that the shade standard uses correlated significantly with ant and bird diversity. Their conclusion: organic and fair trade farms there may not protect as much biodiversity as shade-certified farms.

**Who checks:** organic certification agencies on the USDA's accreditation list that the Smithsonian has approved for Bird Friendly, whose auditors are trained by the Bird Friendly team.

**The catch:** you'll rarely find it. Because it requires organic certification or a participatory guarantee system, plus its habitat and traceability criteria, the farmer has to clear two sets of requirements. The Smithsonian's pages don't publish a premium or a farm count, so I've removed the figures the older version of this article gave. Bird Friendly is still the seal I'd point to if habitat is what you care about.

## What certification does for farmers' incomes

The honest answer is: less than the seals imply, and it depends on context. A Campbell systematic review led by Carlos Oya (2017), covering 43 quantitative and 136 qualitative studies of farm certification in low- and middle-income countries, found positive effects on the prices farmers received and higher income from the certified crop, but no clear effect on total household income, and lower wages on average in the pooled studies of hired workers. The authors judged the evidence limited and mixed, the review covers many crops, not coffee alone, and found that certification costs and local conditions often decided whether farmers benefited.

## Direct Trade

**What it certifies:** nothing. Direct Trade is a marketing term with no shared definition and no external audit. Hoffmann describes it as roasters saying they bought coffee directly from the producer rather than through an importer or exporter, and he points out two problems: it can unfairly paint importers and exporters, who do useful work, as middlemen taking a cut, and "without third-party certification it can be difficult to ascertain whether or not a roaster is actually buying the way they say they are."

**When it's real:** the strongest version is a long-term relationship in which a roaster buys enough coffee, year after year, to make a difference. Some roasters publish what they paid: Counter Culture, for example, shows the price per pound it paid next to the C market price and the Fair Trade minimum. Hoffmann's rule of thumb is that a coffee kept traceable, with the producer's, farm's or cooperative's name on it, has probably earned a better price, and that a good roaster should be glad to tell you how they source.

**How to tell:** look for specifics you could check. A named farm or producer, a region, a harvest, a price paid, and the same relationship mentioned year after year all count. "Ethically sourced" with no names doesn't. The same seals and the same questions turn up on <a href="https://jayarrchocolate.com/blog/how-to-read-craft-chocolate-labels/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">craft chocolate labels</a>.

## Cup of Excellence

**What it is:** a competition and auction run by the Alliance for Coffee Excellence, not a certification. Farmers submit lots; national juries cup them, and an international jury of importers, buyers and other experienced cuppers scores the finalists. Under the published rules, a coffee must score at least 87 points in the final round to win a Cup of Excellence award, and the top 30 coffees scoring 87 or more go to an online auction.

**What it gets right:** the entered lot is moved to a bonded warehouse under an independent auditing company's control, so the coffee sold is the coffee that was judged. The auction results are public, the rules say "All of the revenues from the auction remain in the country," and farmers receive most of the proceeds.

**Where it falls short:** it covers a small number of lots from countries that hold a competition. It rewards the best coffees; it isn't a safety net for everyone else.

## Other seals and programs you'll see

- **4C:** a third-party certification system for coffee and cocoa. Its Code of Conduct covers environmental principles (no deforestation, water and soil protection), social ones (safe working conditions, human and labor rights) and economic ones (farmer income, yield and quality). Its own overview doesn't describe a minimum price or premium.
- **C.A.F.E. Practices:** Starbucks' own sourcing program, short for Coffee and Farmer Equity Practices, developed with SCS Global Services and Conservation International. Third-party verifiers score suppliers against more than 200 quality, economic, social and environmental indicators, and suppliers scoring 85 percent or more get better pricing and terms from Starbucks. It's a buyer's program, not a seal you'll find on other brands' bags.

## Which seal for which concern

| If you care most about | Look for | Keep in mind |
|---|---|---|
| A price floor for small farmers | Fairtrade or Fair Trade Certified | The floor only matters when the market falls below it |
| Farming without prohibited synthetic inputs | USDA Organic | Nothing about price or wages; no shade criteria |
| Forest habitat and birds | Bird Friendly | Rare; needs organic or a participatory guarantee first |
| Broad farm practices, at scale | Rainforest Alliance | No minimum price; read which frog seal it is |
| Knowing where your money went | Traceability: farm, producer, price paid | Only as good as the roaster's openness |

A seal tells you how a coffee was farmed and traded. It tells you nothing about how fresh it is. That's on the bag too, as the roast date; our guide to [reading a coffee bag label](/blog/how-to-read-coffee-bag-label/) shows where to find it.
