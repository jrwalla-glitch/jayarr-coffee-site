El Salvador is a small producer with an unusual mix of trees. While its neighbors replanted with newer, higher-yielding varieties, El Salvador kept most of its old Bourbon. It also bred Pacamara, a variety with unusually large beans and a distinctive cup.

## Coffee and El Salvador's history

Hoffmann, in *The World Atlas of Coffee*, writes that El Salvador first produced coffee commercially in the 1850s, as it moved away from indigo, its old export crop, after chemical dyes were invented. The landowning families used their influence in government to pass laws that pushed poor and indigenous people off their land so it could become coffee plantations. Compensation was all but nonexistent, he writes; sometimes people were simply offered seasonal work on the new farms.

Coffee paid for highways, ports and railroads, and it also kept a small landed elite in political and economic control. The importer Sucafina writes that by the 1920s coffee made up 90 percent of El Salvador's exports.

The civil war of 1979–1992 hit the industry hard. Hoffmann writes that production fell and foreign buyers looked elsewhere. Café Imports adds that land redistribution in the 1980s disrupted the industry, and that many farms were abandoned and left overgrown until the peace agreement.

## How the war kept the Bourbon

The war had an unexpected side effect. Hoffmann explains that across Central America at the time, producers were replacing their heirloom varieties with new, higher-yielding ones, choosing yield over cup quality. El Salvador never went through that change, so it kept an unusually high share of heirloom Bourbon trees. He puts them at about 68 percent of the country's coffee. World Coffee Research (WCR) says Tekisic, a Salvadoran Bourbon selection, and unselected Bourbon together make up nearly 70 percent of production.

The same trees have a weakness. Bourbon and its relatives are susceptible to coffee leaf rust, and Café Imports notes that El Salvador's Bourbon-based varieties suffered in the rust outbreak of the 2010s. USDA's estimates of El Salvador's crop fell from 1.86 million 60 kg bags in 2010/11 to 550,000 in 2013/14, a drop of about 70 percent (calculated). Since then the crop has stayed between about 510,000 and 700,000 bags. Our [coffee leaf rust history](/blog/coffee-leaf-rust-hemileia-vastatrix-history/) explains the disease.

## How much El Salvador grows

USDA forecasts 542,000 bags for 2026/27, down from 586,000 in 2025/26. Against a forecast world total of 189.7 million bags, that's about 0.3 percent (calculated).

## Growing regions

El Salvador's coffee grows on the slopes of its volcanoes and mountain ranges. Hoffmann writes that its well-drained but mineral-rich volcanic soils give the country the potential for "stunningly sweet" coffees. He also notes that most roasters don't use the region names, and that some would call the whole country one region with pockets of coffee in it.

| Region | Altitude | Harvest | Varieties (Hoffmann) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Apaneca–Ilamatepec | 500–2,300 m (1,600–7,500 ft) | Oct–Mar | 64% Bourbon, 26% Pacas, 10% others |
| Alotepec–Metapán | 1,000–2,000 m (3,300–6,600 ft) | Oct–Mar | 30% Bourbon, 50% Pacas, 15% Pacamara, 5% others |
| El Bálsamo–Quezaltepec | 500–1,950 m (1,600–6,400 ft) | Oct–Mar | Mostly Bourbon |
| Chichontepec | 500–1,000 m (1,600–3,300 ft) | Oct–Feb | 71% Bourbon, 8% Pacas, 21% mixed and others |
| Tecapa–Chinameca | 500–2,150 m (1,600–7,100 ft) | Oct–Mar | 70% Bourbon, 22% Pacas, 8% mixed and others |
| Cacahuatique | 500–1,650 m (1,600–5,400 ft) | Oct–Mar | 65% Bourbon, 20% Pacas, 15% mixed and others |

- **Apaneca–Ilamatepec,** in the west, is the largest producer and the source of many competition-winning coffees, Hoffmann writes, despite the volcanic activity there: the Santa Ana volcano erupted as recently as 2005.
- **Alotepec–Metapán,** on the borders with Guatemala and Honduras, gets over a third more rain than average.
- **Chichontepec** still commonly follows the old practice of planting alternating rows of coffee and orange trees for shade.
- **Tecapa–Chinameca** (spelled "Tepeca" in Hoffmann) is the third-largest producer.

Bags sometimes carry an altitude grade too. Hoffmann lists Strictly High Grown (above 1,200 m, or 3,900 ft), High Grown (above 900 m, or 3,000 ft) and Central Standard (above 600 m, or 2,000 ft), and points out that these grades have nothing to do with quality or traceability. Our [altitude guide](/blog/coffee-altitude-guide/) explains what elevation does and doesn't tell you.

## The varieties

| Variety | What it is | Bean size | Cup quality potential | Leaf rust |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bourbon | Heirloom, from Yemeni stock via Réunion | Average | Very Good | Susceptible |
| Tekisic | Salvadoran selection of Bourbon | Average | Very Good | Susceptible |
| Pacas | Dwarf mutation of Bourbon, found in El Salvador | Average | Good | Susceptible |
| Pacamara | Pacas × Maragogype, bred in El Salvador | Very large | Exceptional | Susceptible |

The ratings are WCR's, for high altitudes; "Exceptional" is the top of its scale.

**Bourbon.** WCR calls Bourbon one of the most culturally and genetically important arabica varieties in the world, with relatively low production and excellent cup quality at the highest altitudes. It reached Brazil around 1860 and spread north. In much of Latin America its own descendants, such as Caturra and Catuaí, have largely replaced it, but it's still grown in El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras and Peru.

**Tekisic.** El Salvador's coffee research institute, the Instituto Salvadoreño de Investigaciones del Café (ISIC), selected Tekisic from Bourbon for nearly 30 years, beginning in 1949, and released it in 1977. WCR writes that it ripens early and yields more than unselected Bourbon between 900 and 1,500 m, but it isn't resistant to leaf rust. The name joins *tekiti*, Nahuatl for "work," with ISIC.

**Pacas.** A natural, dwarf mutation of Bourbon, found in 1949 on a farm owned by the Pacas family in the Santa Ana region. Its small size lets farmers plant more trees and get more fruit per hectare. ISIC began selecting it in 1960. WCR describes it as standard quality in Central America, with very high susceptibility to leaf rust, and says it makes up about a quarter of El Salvador's production. It's also grown in Honduras, where it was introduced in 1974.

**Pacamara.** ISIC crossed Pacas with Maragogype, a Typica mutation from Brazil known for its very large beans, in 1958. A 2024 report in El Salvador's *Diario El Mundo* says the result was released in 1980 and spread widely from 1992. That's 22 years from cross to release (calculated). WCR describes it this way:

- **Quality:** capable of producing exceptional cup quality.
- **Beans:** very large.
- **Yield:** low.
- **Disease:** very high susceptibility to leaf rust.
- **Stability:** not uniform; plants aren't stable from one generation to the next.

Pacamara isn't exclusive to El Salvador. WCR says it is grown primarily there, where it frequently dominates Cup of Excellence competitions, and Hoffmann notes that Pacas and Pacamara are grown in neighboring countries too. Reporting on the national coffee institute's Pacamara Day event in March 2026, *Dinero.com.sv* put Pacamara's Cup of Excellence scores between 90.00 and 93.52 points. Sucafina adds that the variety was overlooked for a while before quality-focused producers brought it back over the last decade. March 23 is now El Salvador's national Pacamara Day.

Café Imports lists Typica among El Salvador's common varieties, and Sucafina adds Caturra and Catuaí. Our guide to [coffee varieties](/blog/coffee-varietal-guide/) explains how they're related.

## Processing

Café Imports lists washed processing as the main method, with some experimentation with natural and honey. Sucafina notes that the dry season coincides with the harvest, which helps with sun-drying. Most coffee dries on clay patios, though raised beds are increasingly common. It adds that El Salvador produced naturals for the German market in the 1970s, and that producers have recently experimented with honey and natural styles and with soaking coffee after washing.

Hoffmann names El Salvador, with Costa Rica, among the Central American countries that use the honey process. The fruit's skin is removed by machine, and a chosen share of its sticky flesh is left on the bean to dry. Our guides to [processing methods](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/) and [honey processing](/blog/honey-process-colors-explained/) go further.

| Process | What it tends to do (Hoffmann) |
|---|---|
| Washed | More acidity, complexity and a clean cup |
| Honey | Close to pulped natural: more sweetness and body |
| Natural | Fruit flavors; sometimes wild or fermented ones |

These are tendencies across many lots, not rules for a particular bag.

## What Salvadoran coffee tastes like

Hoffmann's summary is that El Salvador's Bourbon coffees are famously sweet and well balanced, with a pleasing soft acidity. Pacamara is less predictable: he writes that it can taste like chocolate and fruit, but can also produce unpleasantly herbal, onion-like cups.

These are notes a Salvadoran coffee *can* show; no single bag has them all:

- **Bourbon lots:** caramel, milk chocolate, clean sweetness and a delicate acidity, with a smooth finish
- **Pacamara:** floral aromatics, bright acidity, tropical and stone fruit, and a fuller body
- **Honey and natural lots:** more body and fruit sweetness; naturals can turn toward dried fruit, berry and wine-like notes

## What to look for on a bag

- **The variety.** Bourbon for a sweet, balanced cup; Pacamara if you want something more unusual, accepting that it varies more.
- **A farm or mill name.** Hoffmann writes that it's relatively easy to trace high-quality Salvadoran coffee back to the farm, and that many farms make micro-lots by process and variety.
- **The process.** Washed for clarity; honey or natural for more sweetness, fruit and body.
- **A harvest or roast date.** Hoffmann gives most regions an October–March harvest. Café Imports lists November–April, with typical US arrivals from May to July.

## Brewing Salvadoran coffee

Hoffmann notes that paper filters give the cleanest cup, which suits a sweet, balanced Bourbon. For a medium roast in a V60, our Brew Guide starts at 1:16 with the kettle at 201°F&nbsp;(94°C); for a light roast it goes up to 205°F&nbsp;(96°C). If the cup tastes sour or bitter, change the grind first. Pacamara's big beans don't need a special setting: the grinder's setting decides the size of the grounds, so start where you would for any coffee and adjust by taste. Our [V60 guide](/blog/ultimate-pour-over-coffee-guide-and-best-v60-technique/) and [grind size guide](/blog/coffee-grind-size-guide-what-grind-for-every-brew-method/) go further.
