The advice to drink a glass of water for every coffee rests on something real: caffeine does make you pass more urine. The question is whether that extra urine outweighs the water in the cup. Controlled studies have measured it, and for ordinary amounts in people who drink coffee regularly, it doesn't.

## Why caffeine gets the blame

Caffeine is a diuretic. In a study in mice, its effect on urine depended on caffeine blocking the kidney's adenosine A1 receptors, which reduces how much sodium and water the kidney tubules take back (Rieg et al., 2005).

The human experiments behind the warning mostly used large doses, often in people who had stopped caffeine first. The Institute of Medicine's 2005 report on water needs summarizes two of them:

- After 12 regular caffeine users went five days without any, 642 mg of caffeine given as coffee raised their 24-hour urine output by about 0.75 liter, a 41 percent increase.
- Eight men who had been taking about 240 mg a day skipped caffeine for 24 hours, then took test doses of 45 to 360 mg. Only the 360 mg dose increased their urine over the next three hours.

The report's reading of the evidence is that "higher doses of caffeine (above 180 mg/day) have been shown to increase urinary output, perhaps transiently," and that whether high intakes lead to a total body water deficit "is uncertain."

## What the controlled trials found

These are the studies that measured hydration directly, with their designs and sizes.

- **Coffee against water (Killer, Blannin and Jeukendrup, 2014).** Fifty healthy men who usually drank three to six cups a day took part in a counterbalanced crossover trial. In one arm they drank four 200 ml mugs of black instant coffee a day, dosed at 4 mg of caffeine per kilogram of body weight; in the other, four mugs of water. Diet, other drinks and exercise were controlled, and hydration was measured over three days in each arm. Total body water, measured by deuterium dilution, didn't differ between the two, and neither did a wide range of blood and urine markers; 24-hour urine averaged 2,409 ml on coffee and 2,428 ml on water. Urinary sodium was a little higher on coffee. The authors concluded that, drunk in moderation by caffeine-habituated men, coffee "provides similar hydrating qualities to water." They list the limits themselves: men only, one moderate dose and no decaf arm. The Institute for Scientific Information on Coffee funded the study.
- **Caffeine capsules for 11 days (Armstrong et al., 2005).** Fifty-nine young men took 3 mg of caffeine per kilogram a day for six days, then 0, 3 or 6 mg per kilogram for five more, in capsules and with no other caffeine. Body mass, 24-hour urine volume, urine concentration and blood markers didn't differ by dose on any measured day, and none of the groups showed "evidence of hypohydration."
- **One liter of each drink (Maughan et al., 2016).** In a randomized trial of 13 common drinks, 72 men each drank a liter of water over 30 minutes and, in separate trials, a liter of three of the other drinks. Their urine over the next four hours after coffee wasn't different from after water. Milk and an oral rehydration solution were the drinks retained better than water.
- **A large dose at once (Seal et al., 2017).** Ten adults who drank coffee daily had 200 ml of water, or of coffee carrying 3 or 6 mg of caffeine per kilogram (about 269 and 537 mg), on separate mornings. Over three hours, urine came to 356 ml after water, 316 ml after the lower dose and 613 ml after the higher one. The large dose acted as a diuretic; the moderate one didn't.
- **Pooling the studies (Zhang et al., 2015).** A meta-analysis of 16 studies, with a median dose of 300 mg, found caffeine raised urine output by about 109 ml, or 16 percent, compared with no caffeine. The effect was clear at rest but not significant during exercise, and it was larger in women than in men.

So the honest summary has two parts. Moderate amounts, in people used to caffeine, don't leave them short of water. A large dose at once can add a few hundred milliliters of urine over the next few hours.

## The water in the cup

Brewed coffee is almost all water. The USDA lists it at 99.39 g of water per 100 g, so an 8 fl oz (237 ml) cup carries about 236 g of water (calculated). The meta-analysis's average extra urine, 109 ml, is less than half of that (calculated). That's why everyday coffee adds to your fluid intake rather than taking from it.

The agencies that set fluid guidance say the same, in their own words:

- **The National Academies (Institute of Medicine, 2005):** "unless additional evidence becomes available indicating cumulative total water deficits in individuals with habitual intakes of significant amounts of caffeine, caffeinated beverages appear to contribute to the daily total water intake similar to that contributed by noncaffeinated beverages."
- **The NHS:** "Water, lower-fat milk and lower-sugar drinks, including tea and coffee, all count as part of your daily intake." It suggests that "most people should aim to drink enough during the day so their pee is a clear pale yellow colour," and adds: "drink caffeine in moderation – some people are more sensitive to caffeine than others."

## How much caffeine is "everyday"?

The trial doses are easier to judge next to real cups. Killer's 4 mg per kilogram works out to about 300 mg a day for a 75 kg (165 lb) man (calculated). Home-brewed coffee at the Brew Guide's dose usually carries more than that: our [Caffeine Calculator](/tools/caffeine/), which starts from the Brew Guide's doses, estimates a 200 ml (about 6¾ fl oz) drip mug at about 90–165 mg, so four of them come to about 360–660 mg. The large single doses that did raise urine, 360 to 642 mg, are the caffeine of two or more big mugs drunk together.

For safety rather than hydration, the FDA's population guide is: "For most adults, the FDA has cited 400 milligrams a day — that's about two to three 12-fluid-ounce cups of coffee — as an amount not generally associated with negative effects." The full set of agency figures, in their own wording, is in our [caffeine in coffee guide](/blog/caffeine-in-coffee-guide/).

## Exercise and hot days

In the 2015 meta-analysis, caffeine's effect on urine wasn't significant during exercise. That doesn't make coffee a sports drink. The NHS notes that its 6 to 8 cups or glasses a day is "just a guide," and that you may need more fluid if you're pregnant or breastfeeding, in hot conditions, physically active for long periods, or ill or recovering. That holds whether or not you drink coffee.

## When your situation is different

The trials above were in healthy adults, most of them young men. They don't tell you what happens if you're ill with vomiting or diarrhea, have a kidney or heart condition, take a diuretic medicine, are pregnant, or are older and drink little. If any of those apply, ask your clinician how much fluid and caffeine suit you. The FDA says the same for caffeine: "If you are concerned about a condition or medication, or if you're pregnant, trying to become pregnant, or breastfeeding, we recommend talking to your health care provider about whether you need to limit caffeine consumption."

Children and teens are a separate case. The FDA warns that "too much caffeine in children and teens can cause increased heart rate, heart palpitations, high blood pressure, anxiety, and lead to sleep problems, digestive problems and dehydration."
