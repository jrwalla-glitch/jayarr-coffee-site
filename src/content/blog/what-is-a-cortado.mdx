*Cortado* means "cut" in Spanish: espresso cut with milk. The idea is the same everywhere. The amounts are not, so this page sets out published recipes side by side rather than one rule.

## How much milk? It depends where you order

Each row is one published recipe or menu listing, not a universal definition.

| Whose recipe | Espresso | Milk | Cup |
|---|---|---|---|
| Café Sabora, common Spanish style | A single, about 30&nbsp;ml | About 10&nbsp;ml; a few drops to equal parts | A small cup or glass |
| James Hoffmann | About 30&nbsp;ml | The same amount, steamed | A glass |
| Ineffable Coffee, Spanish specialty | A double, 40&nbsp;g | 5 to 40&nbsp;g | Not stated |
| Starbucks | Three ristretto shots | Steamed whole milk | An 8&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz cup |
| Blue Bottle's Gibraltar | Not stated | "A generous helping," steamed | A 4½&nbsp;oz glass |

So a Spanish cortado comes to about 40&nbsp;ml, roughly 1⅓&nbsp;US&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz, at Sabora's common proportions; about 60&nbsp;ml (2&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz) at Hoffmann's equal parts; and 45 to 80&nbsp;g at Ineffable's, about 1½ to 2¾&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz if you count a gram as a milliliter (all calculated). Sabora itself says there's no single strict formula, and that equal parts is less usual in Spain than in other definitions. Starbucks' version, with three shots, comes in a bigger, 8&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz cup.

## Where the cortado comes from

Hoffmann calls the cortado one of the few coffee drinks without Italian origins: it comes from Spain, "most likely Madrid," where it's commonly served. He notes that Spanish espresso is traditionally brewed a little longer, and often a little weaker, than Italian, and that the cortado is traditionally served in a glass. It has since spread and been reinterpreted in different ways.

Within Spain, Café Sabora describes regional differences: small glasses in Catalonia and the Levante region, espresso cups in Galicia. The milk is usually steamed so it stays creamy rather than just hot, though some people take it cold or warm.

In the US, Blue Bottle Coffee tells how its Gibraltar began. Someone bought a stock of 4.5&nbsp;oz Gibraltar glass tumblers for the company's Hayes Valley kiosk, thinking they'd be big enough for cupping. They weren't, so the baristas used them to check espresso shots, and then began adding steamed milk to the espresso for regulars. "It's really a cortado, but we didn't know that at the time," founder James Freeman writes. Blue Bottle says the Gibraltar has since appeared on the menus of unaffiliated cafés in the US, Europe and Japan. Our [cortado glasses](/blog/best-cortado-glasses/) guide compares the glass and others like it.

## How to make a cortado at home

You need an espresso machine with a steam wand. This recipe uses Ineffable Coffee's specialty proportions, with the milk temperature from Breville's Bambino manual.

- **Espresso:** about 40&nbsp;g, a double. Our Brew Guide's double is 18&nbsp;g of coffee to 36&nbsp;g in the cup, a little shorter; either works.
- **Milk:** cold whole milk, about 100&nbsp;ml in the pitcher. A cortado uses only 5 to 40&nbsp;g of it, but you'll steam more than you pour: Breville's own macchiato recipe steams 100&nbsp;ml to use two teaspoons.
- **Makes:** one cortado of about 45 to 80&nbsp;g, about 1½ to 2¾&nbsp;US&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz (calculated).

1. Pull a double espresso of about 40&nbsp;g into a small glass.
2. Pour about 100&nbsp;ml of cold milk into the pitcher. Stretch it only briefly, then spin it until the jug is hot to touch, about 140–149°F&nbsp;(60–65°C).
3. Tap and swirl the milk until it looks glossy, then pour 5 to 40&nbsp;g of it into the espresso: the less milk, the stronger the drink.
4. Serve it straight away.

**On the temperature:** 140–149°F&nbsp;(60–65°C) is the range Breville's manual gives. James Hoffmann says milk begins to degrade in flavor and texture above 154°F&nbsp;(68°C). Scott Rao's café target in *The Professional Barista's Handbook* is 150–160°F&nbsp;(66–71°C), and the top of that is past Hoffmann's limit; go there only if you want the drink hotter. Our [milk steaming guide](/blog/how-to-steam-milk-for-lattes/) explains the stretch and the spin, and our [espresso dial-in guide](/blog/how-to-dial-in-espresso/) covers the shot.

## Cortado, piccolo, flat white or macchiato?

These are all small espresso-and-milk drinks, and they're easy to confuse. One published recipe for each:

| Drink, recipe | Espresso | Milk | Cup |
|---|---|---|---|
| Macchiato, illy | A single, 20–25&nbsp;ml | Foam from about 28&nbsp;ml | Small |
| Cortado, Café Sabora | A single, about 30&nbsp;ml | About 10&nbsp;ml | Small |
| Piccolo, Saša Šestić | A single, 20–30&nbsp;ml | 40–60&nbsp;ml | 85–114&nbsp;ml glass |
| Flat white, La Cimbali | A double | 125–130&nbsp;ml, frothed | 150–160&nbsp;ml |

Šestić's piccolo is from Perfect Daily Grind, and Cimbali's flat white has about 0.5&nbsp;cm of foam. Perfect Daily Grind notes that the piccolo and the cortado are commonly mistaken for each other. It describes the piccolo as the sweeter, less intense drink and the cortado as bolder, with less milk; Grant Gamble, quoted there, calls the cortado a double and the piccolo a single. For the flat white, Hoffmann describes "a small, strong latte" of 150–175&nbsp;ml, so it's several times the size of a Spanish cortado. Our guides to the [macchiato](/blog/what-is-a-macchiato/) and the [flat white](/blog/what-is-a-flat-white-latte-vs-flat-white-comparison/) go further.

## Ordering one

Cafés' cortados differ, so if the size matters to you, ask how they make theirs. At Starbucks, the Cortado is three ristretto shots with steamed whole milk in a Short, 8&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz cup. If a café lists a Gibraltar, it's the same idea, in that glass. For the rest of the menu, see our [guide to ordering at a coffee shop](/blog/how-to-order-at-coffee-shop/).

## The cortado by other names

Café Sabora's guide lists what to ask for in other places:

- **Cuba and Puerto Rico:** a *cortadito*, sometimes made with condensed milk.
- **Portugal:** a *pingo* in the north, a *garoto* in Lisbon.
- **Venezuela:** a *marrón*, equal parts with no foam; a *marroncito* is closer to the Spanish proportions.
- **Colombia:** a *café pintado* or *perico*.
- **Argentina:** a *cortado*, by that name.
- **France:** a *café noisette*.
- **Austria:** a *kleiner Brauner*.
- **Italy:** a *caffè macchiato*, with less milk.
