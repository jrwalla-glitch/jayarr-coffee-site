Here's a question that stumps a lot of coffee drinkers: which Central American country grows the most coffee? It's not Guatemala, and it's not Costa Rica. It's Honduras, by a wide margin. And yet most people would struggle to name a single Honduran farm, region or variety.

## How much coffee Honduras grows

USDA's July 2026 forecast puts Honduras's 2026/27 crop at 6.03 million 60 kg bags, all of it arabica. That makes it the fourth-largest arabica producer in the world on USDA's table, after Brazil, Colombia and Ethiopia, and about 34 percent of the 17.7 million bags USDA expects from Central America and Mexico together (calculated). It's about 3 percent of world production (calculated from USDA's 189.7 million).

| Marketing year | Production (million 60 kg bags) |
|---|---|
| 2021/22 | 4.80 |
| 2022/23 | 5.70 |
| 2023/24 | 5.05 |
| 2024/25 | 5.20 |
| 2025/26 | 5.53 |
| 2026/27 | 6.03 (forecast) |

USDA expects Honduras to account for nearly all of the region's growth in 2026/27, which its Tegucigalpa office, citing IHCAFE, credits to better plant nutrition, the crop's two-year cycle, more planted area, better pruning and newly planted farms coming into production. It forecasts exports of about 5.5 million bags in 2026/27, and ranks Honduras the eighth-largest coffee exporter in the world and the largest in Central America. In marketing year 2025, the United States took about 34 percent of Honduras's green coffee exports, Germany 23 percent and Belgium 13 percent.

## A short history

Coffee has been in Honduras longer than its reputation suggests. James Hoffmann, in *The World Atlas of Coffee*, cites what's probably the earliest record, from 1804, which discusses the quality of Honduran coffee and so dates the first plants to before 1799. But production only took off after 2001, he writes, and because coffee came late, Honduras lacked the infrastructure it had built for its neighbors in the 1800s, so much of that new coffee went to the commodity market. By 2011 it was producing just under 6 million bags a year, more than Costa Rica and Guatemala combined.

The Instituto Hondureño del Café (IHCAFE) was founded in 1970 and, according to the importer Café Imports, privatized in 2000. Hoffmann notes that it runs a cupping laboratory in each of its six regions to help local producers, and USDA reports it has six research and training centers.

Most Honduran coffee farmers are smallholders. IHCAFE says coffee is a way of life for more than 100,000 families and comes from 210 of the country's 298 municipalities, in 15 of its 18 departments. In 2024/25, it registered 86,895 small, 6,359 medium and 374 large coffee farmers, according to USDA: about 93 percent were small, they grew about 55 percent of the crop, and the average across all sizes was about 3 hectares of harvested coffee (all calculated).

## The six growing regions

IHCAFE divides the country into six coffee regions. Hoffmann's altitudes and harvest months match IHCAFE's earlier region pages almost exactly; IHCAFE's current chart gives wider bands, mostly 1,100 to 1,700 m.

| Region | Departments (IHCAFE) | Altitude (Hoffmann) | Harvest (Hoffmann) | IHCAFE's cup description |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Copán | Copán, Ocotepeque, Santa Bárbara | 1,000–1,500 m | November–March | Sweet aroma, chocolate, creamy and balanced |
| Opalaca | Santa Bárbara, Intibucá, Lempira | 1,100–1,500 m | November–February | Fine, delicate acidity; tropical fruit like grapes and blackberries |
| Montecillos | La Paz, Comayagua, Santa Bárbara, Intibucá | 1,200–1,600 m | December–April | Fruity, sweet; lively, bright acidity; citrus and peach |
| Comayagua | Comayagua, Francisco Morazán | 1,100–1,500 m | December–March | Sweet citrus; vibrant citric acidity, chocolate, creamy body |
| Agalta | Olancho, Yoro, Atlántida, Colón | 1,000–1,400 m | December–March | Caramel fragrance, tropical fruit, a sweet finish |
| El Paraíso | El Paraíso and parts of Choluteca and Francisco Morazán | 1,000–1,400 m | December–March | Sweet fragrance, smooth body, citrus |

Coffee Origins draws Honduras's harvest as November to April, from importers' calendars, with some finishing in March.

Hoffmann adds that many roasters label coffee "Santa Barbara" after the department, which several of the regions cross into, even though IHCAFE doesn't treat it as a region. He singles out excellent Pacas lots from there, with "a distinctive and quite intense fruity quality" when well produced.

### Marcala and Montecillos

Hoffmann writes that Montecillos contains several notable sub-areas, above all Marcala, "now a protected name," and La Paz; Marcala is a municipality in the department of La Paz, and roasters are more likely to use these names than the wider "Montecillos."

Café Marcala's denomination of origin was legally recognized on November 18, 2005, according to the association of local producers that administers it, ADOPCAM. A Honduran law firm's account adds that it was granted to the municipality of Marcala at the association's request, that denominations of origin in Honduras are granted by the Industrial Property Registry, and that Marcala's was the first legally recognized in the country. It's also protected in Europe: the EU's 2024 summary of its association agreement with Central America names "café de Marcala de Honduras" among the Central American geographical indications protected in European markets.

In the cup, Montecillos and Marcala coffees can be sweet, with bright citric acidity, stone fruit such as peach, caramel and chocolate, and a clean finish. Café Imports buys from Cooperativa RAOS, a 250-member cooperative in Marcala, La Paz, and reports some of its most stable results there.

### Comayagua

Hoffmann describes the Comayagua region, in west-central Honduras, as dense with tropical rainforest; its city was once the country's capital. IHCAFE's earlier region page described Comayagua's coffee as sweet and citrusy, with vibrant citric acidity, chocolate and a creamy body, and Comayagua coffees can also lean nutty-sweet, with brown sugar. (IHCAFE's current chart repeats Montecillos' departments and cup description under Comayagua, so the table uses the earlier page.)

Leaf rust has been a particular problem there lately. In March 2026, USDA reported an incidence of 14.08 percent in the department of Comayagua, the highest of the departments it listed, against a national average of 8.44 percent.

### Copán, Opalaca, Agalta and El Paraíso

- **Copán** borders Guatemala. Hoffmann uses it to make a point about labels: "Geopolitical borders can be somewhat arbitrary," and coffees from either side can be closer than buyers expect. Its coffees can be fruit-forward and chocolatey, with a fuller body.
- **Opalaca**, named after the Opalaca mountains, covers the southern coffee areas of Santa Bárbara as well as Intibucá and Lempira. Its coffees can be sweet and fruity, with tropical and honey-like notes.
- **Agalta** stretches across the north; Hoffmann notes that much of it is protected forest. Its coffees can show chocolate and tropical fruit.
- **El Paraíso**, in the east near Nicaragua, is one of the oldest growing regions and the largest, Hoffmann writes, and has suffered badly from leaf rust.

In a 2003 study of 52 plots across six Honduran producing regions, Decazy and colleagues found the best-quality coffees came from Olancho (part of IHCAFE's Agalta region) and El Paraíso, and that high altitude and annual rainfall under 1,500 mm favored quality. Our [coffee altitude guide](/blog/coffee-altitude-guide/) puts that study alongside others.

## Altitude grades on the bag

Honduras grades coffee by altitude. Hoffmann gives Strictly High Grown (SHG) as above 1,200 m (3,900 ft) and High Grown (HG) as above 1,000 m (3,300 ft); Café Imports also lists Central Standard (CS). He adds that while there's some correlation between altitude and quality, the grades are most often used to market less traceable lots. A named region, farm or cooperative tells you more. Our [altitude guide](/blog/coffee-altitude-guide/) compares these cutoffs with Guatemala's and El Salvador's.

## Varieties

Hoffmann lists Bourbon, Caturra, Typica and Catuai as the varieties many Honduran farmers grow, and IHCAFE has bred its own. Here's how World Coffee Research (WCR) rates them:

| Variety | What it is | Yield | Quality potential (WCR) | Leaf rust (WCR) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bourbon | Old line from Yemen via Réunion; tall | Medium | Very Good | Susceptible |
| Typica | Old line from Yemen; tall | Low | Very Good | Susceptible |
| Caturra | Bourbon mutation from Brazil; compact | Medium | Good | Susceptible |
| Catuai | Mundo Novo × Caturra; compact | Medium | Good | Susceptible |
| Pacas | Bourbon mutation from El Salvador; compact | Medium | Good | Susceptible |
| Lempira | IHCAFE's Timor Hybrid × Caturra (Catimor) | High | Low | Intermediate resistance |
| IHCAFE 90 | IHCAFE's Timor Hybrid × Caturra (Catimor) | High | Very Low | Susceptible |
| Parainema | IHCAFE's selection of T5296 (Sarchimor), released 2004 | Very High | Good | Highly resistant |

A few notes from WCR's entries:

- **Lempira and IHCAFE 90** were bred for rust resistance, but WCR says both have now been confirmed susceptible to leaf rust in Honduras. Lempira's "intermediate" rating comes from WCR's international trials, and local performance depends on which races of rust are present.
- **Parainema** is rated well adapted to medium altitudes and resistant to rust and some nematodes. USDA reports that its introduction is driving the growth in Honduras's planted area.
- **Pacas** was introduced to Honduras by IHCAFE in 1974; **Catuai** arrived in 1979 and was tested by IHCAFE. Bourbon itself is still grown in Honduras, WCR notes, though much of Latin America has replaced it with its descendants.

Our [coffee varietal guide](/blog/coffee-varietal-guide/) explains the families these belong to.

## Leaf rust

Hoffmann writes that Honduras declared a state of national emergency after leaf rust badly damaged the 2012/13 harvest, and that its effects usually last a few years. Our research note on [coffee and climate change](/deep-brew/research/coffee-climate-change-data/) covers what that epidemic did across Central America and why weather played a part. The disease hasn't gone away: USDA reported in March 2026 that national average incidence had risen from 7.57 to 8.44 percent, a "yellow alert," and that IHCAFE's April 2025 survey found very high incidence, above 15 percent, on 21.63 percent of sampled farms. Our history of [coffee leaf rust](/blog/coffee-leaf-rust-hemileia-vastatrix-history/) tells the longer story.

## Processing, drying and freshness

Washed is the standard process: it's the only one in Café Imports' Honduras profile. Drying is the hard part. Hoffmann explains that the high rainfall makes it difficult to dry the coffee after processing, so some producers combine sun and mechanical drying, and Café Imports adds that fully mechanical drying can leave a lot's moisture unstable, which "can result in quality concerns over time." Hoffmann notes that this has given Honduras a reputation for great coffees that can fade quite quickly, and that much of the coffee waits for shipment in very hot warehouses near Puerto Cortés, which can degrade it further; the very best tend to hold up better.

Café Imports also credits the Cup of Excellence with encouraging larger and wealthier producers to plant new varieties and experiment with processing. Our [processing methods guide](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/) explains how washed, honey and natural coffees differ.

## Quality and the Cup of Excellence

Honduras has held a Cup of Excellence competition since 2004: the Alliance for Coffee Excellence lists results from that year and marked the program's 20th anniversary in 2024, when the Honduran winner sold for 136.50 US dollars a pound. Café Imports notes that IHCAFE is closely involved in organizing and promoting the competition.

The specialty side has grown into a big share of the crop. IHCAFE counts about half of each recent harvest as "differentiated" coffee, which covers certified and specialty lots: between 52 and 58 percent from 2019/20 to 2024/25, according to USDA. USDA adds that specialty coffee in Honduras is typically grown above 3,000 ft (about 900 m).

## What Honduran coffee tastes like

Hoffmann writes that Honduran coffees range widely, but the best often have "a complex fruity quality, and a lively, juicy acidity." Café Imports describes much of the country's coffee as "mild": nutty, sweet and heavy-bodied, but without the "oomph" of acidity of a fantastic Guatemalan or the dynamic profile of a top Costa Rican. The best Honduran coffees can offer clean sweetness, bright citric acidity, stone fruit, caramel and chocolate, sitting somewhere between Guatemala's complexity and Colombia's balance, and higher-grown lots can surprise you with floral and tropical fruit notes.

Coffee Origins' small sample of Honduran lots that roasters described from January to March 2026 shows that range: mango and tropical fruit, jasmine, caramel, raisin, chocolate and orange each came up twice across six lots.

## What to look for on a bag

- **A region, and ideally a place within it.** One of IHCAFE's six regions, or a sub-area such as Marcala or La Paz. "Santa Barbara" is a department, not an IHCAFE region, but it's a name worth knowing.
- **The Marcala denomination of origin**, if the coffee claims Marcala.
- **A producer, cooperative or mill.** Hoffmann says Honduran coffee can be traced to the estate or to a cooperative or producer group, so a bag that stops at the country is leaving information out.
- **The variety.** A Pacas, Bourbon or Parainema rates higher for quality potential in WCR's catalog than Lempira or IHCAFE 90. It's a clue, not a verdict.
- **A recent harvest and roast date.** Because Honduran coffee can fade, freshness matters more than usual. Our guide to [coffee freshness](/blog/coffee-freshness-roast-to-stale/) explains the roast-date side.
- **An altitude grade on its own tells you little.** SHG says only that the coffee was grown above 1,200 m.
