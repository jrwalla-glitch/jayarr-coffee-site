"Does coffee break a fast?" has more than one right answer, because people mean different things by fasting. A time-restricted eating plan, a fast before a blood test and a water-only fast have different rules. Start with the rules, then look at what's in the cup.

## What counts as breaking a fast depends on the fast

**Time-restricted eating, as studied.** Research trials spell out what's allowed outside the eating window, and the two we checked both allow black coffee:

- In a crossover trial in adults with type 2 diabetes, Andriessen and colleagues (2022) set a 10-hour eating window. Outside it, "volunteers were only allowed to drink water, plain tea and black coffee," with zero-energy soft drinks allowed in moderation in the evening.
- In a 12-month trial in adults with obesity, Lin and colleagues (2024) set a 16-hour fast during which participants "were allowed to drink water and energy-free beverages like black coffee, tea, and diet sodas (limit 2 per day)."

In those protocols, black coffee was part of the fast, and anything with calories wasn't.

**A fast before a blood test or a procedure.** This is stricter. MedlinePlus, from the US National Library of Medicine, answers "Can I drink anything besides water during a fast?" with "No. Juice, coffee, soda, and other beverages can get in your bloodstream and affect your results." Follow the instructions your clinic gave you.

**A water-only fast.** Coffee is out by definition.

So the useful questions are which rules you're following and what goes into the cup.

## Calories in black coffee

These are calculated from the USDA's FoodData Central values per 100 g and its own portion weights:

| Coffee | Serving | Calories (calculated) |
|---|---|---|
| Brewed coffee | 8 fl oz (237 g) | about 2 |
| Brewed decaf | 8 fl oz (237 g) | about 0 |
| Espresso | 1 fl oz (29.6 g) | about 3 |
| Double espresso | 2 fl oz (59.2 g) | about 5 |

The USDA doesn't list French press or cold brew separately, so we don't give figures for them. Espresso's fat, by the USDA's figures, comes to about 0.1 g in a double shot (calculated). The better reason to care about coffee oils is cholesterol, not calories: paper filters hold back most of them, including cafestol and kahweol, the compounds behind unfiltered coffee's effect on LDL cholesterol (Urgert and Katan, 1997).

## What additions add

Also calculated from the USDA's values and portion weights:

| Addition | Amount | Calories | Carbs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole milk | 1 tbsp (15 g) | about 9 | 0.7 g |
| Whole milk | 1 fl oz (30.5 g) | about 19 | 1.5 g |
| Half-and-half | 1 tbsp (15 g) | about 20 | 0.6 g |
| Heavy cream | 1 tbsp (15 g) | about 51 | 0.4 g |
| Sugar | 1 tsp (4.2 g) | about 16 | 4.2 g |
| Butter | 1 tbsp (14.2 g) | about 102 | 0 g |
| Whole milk in a latte | 120 ml (about 124 g) | about 75 | 5.9 g |

The latte line uses illy's recipe, 120 ml of whole milk to a single shot. A coffee blended with a tablespoon of butter carries about 100 calories before any oil goes in, so under any rule that counts calories it isn't a fasting drink. Under the trials' rules above, even a splash of milk falls outside the fast.

Sweeteners are a judgment call that the rules you follow should settle: the two trials allowed zero-energy soft drinks or diet sodas in limited amounts.

## Caffeine during a fast

Caffeine has a short-term effect on blood sugar control. A 2016 meta-analysis of seven randomized trials (Shi and colleagues) found that a dose of caffeine reduced insulin sensitivity in healthy people, and the authors conclude that "in the short term, caffeine might shift glycemic homeostasis toward hyperglycemia." Those trials looked at single doses of caffeine, not at fasting plans.

Over years, the picture looks different, though it comes from observational studies rather than trials. In a 2014 meta-analysis of 28 prospective studies with more than a million participants, each extra cup a day was associated with a 9% lower risk of type 2 diabetes for caffeinated coffee and 6% for decaf (Ding and colleagues). A 2018 meta-analysis of 30 studies found 6% per cup, with similar figures for caffeinated and decaf (Carlström and Larsson). These are associations; they don't show that coffee prevents diabetes, and they didn't test coffee during a fast. If you have diabetes or prediabetes, ask your clinician how coffee and fasting fit together for you.

How much caffeine is in your cups is in our [caffeine in coffee guide](/blog/caffeine-in-coffee-guide/), along with the FDA's population figure: "For most adults, the FDA has cited 400 milligrams a day … as an amount not generally associated with negative effects." Fasting doesn't change how much is in the cup. If your fast ends late in the day, see [coffee and sleep](/blog/coffee-and-sleep-when-to-stop/) for how long caffeine lingers.

## What about autophagy?

Autophagy is the cell's way of breaking down and recycling its own parts, and it's often given as a reason to fast. The coffee evidence is in mice. Pietrocola and colleagues (2014) found that both regular and decaffeinated coffee increased markers of autophagy in the liver, muscle and heart of mice one to four hours after drinking, along with lower mTORC1 activity. The authors speculate that coffee's polyphenols are responsible. We found no human trial that measured whether black coffee changes autophagy during a fast, so the mouse result is a lead, not an answer for people.

## Before you start

If you take medicine for diabetes, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or have a history of disordered eating, ask your clinician before starting a fasting routine. That's a question about the fast itself, not just the coffee in it.
