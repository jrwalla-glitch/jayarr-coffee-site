## Why the grinder comes first

Writing about espresso, Scott Rao calls a fresh, high-quality grind "the most important prerequisite for great espresso", and he advises putting the grinder ahead of the machine in your budget.

A burr grinder crushes beans between two burrs set a chosen distance apart, so you can return to the same setting tomorrow. A blade grinder chops for as long as it runs and has no setting to return to.

No grinder makes particles of one size. Rao treats the spread of sizes as the main sign of grind quality: a narrower spread puts more of the coffee near the size you chose, while in his account the finest particles over-extract and the largest under-extract. Our [grind size guide](/blog/coffee-grind-size-guide-what-grind-for-every-brew-method/) covers which size each brewer needs.

## What the specs tell you, and what they don't

### Burr size

Rao's rule is simple: bigger burrs are better, because larger sets make fewer fines and a narrower spread, and heat the coffee less. Jonathan Gagné's 2023 analysis of 24 espresso grinders, measured by the Kaffeemacher team and the ZHAW, found less to go on: within that sample, larger burrs did not systematically grind more evenly, and burr geometry and coating looked like bigger factors. Treat burr size as one clue, not a score.

### Conical or flat

In the same analysis, conical burrs were on average less even than flat ones, but the overlap was large: Gagné notes you can easily find a specific flat burr that is less even than a specific conical one. Our [conical vs flat burrs](/blog/conical-vs-flat-burr-grinders/) post goes further.

### Steps or stepless

A stepped dial lets you return to an exact number. A stepless one lets you stop anywhere between. Some grinders split the difference:

- **Encore ESP.** Baratza says each setting from 1 to 20 changes the grind by a small amount, for espresso, while settings 21 to 40 make larger jumps across the filter range.
- **Fellow Opus.** Fellow's chart puts espresso between 1 and 2. On the outer ring's quarter steps, that's five positions (calculated), and Fellow suggests the inner ring for finer espresso adjustments.
- **Sette 270.** Baratza pairs macro steps with a stepless micro ring, for what it calls "270 pinpoint grind sizes".

### Single-dosing and retention

A single-dose grinder is built to grind exactly the beans you weigh into it, so you can change coffees every cup. Makers describe retention in words more often than numbers:

- Niche advertises "zero retention" for the Niche Zero.
- Fellow says the Ode Gen 2 has "low grind retention", without a figure.
- Turin says the DF64's bellows help, but "low retention does not mean every dose will have identical input and output weight."

Independent numbers are rarer. In a 2021 espresso comparison, Gagné measured his Niche Zero's retention at an average of 0.18 ± 0.04 g.

Static makes retention worse. Turin recommends misting the beans with one or two drops of water before grinding, the Ross Droplet Technique. Méndez Harper and colleagues (2024) found that added water suppressed the charging of ground coffee without causing clumps, and that with fixed espresso settings, less charge went with higher extraction. Our [RDT guide](/blog/rdt-ross-droplet-technique-grinding/) covers the method.

## Finding your grind setting

A grind setting only means something on one grinder. Baratza's chart for the Encore ESP suggests these starting points, to adjust coarser or finer for your recipe and taste:

- espresso 15 (for a medium roast and an 18 g dose);
- AeroPress 22;
- Hario V60 25;
- automatic brewer 28;
- Chemex 30;
- French press 32.

Other makers publish their own: Fellow suggests starting the Ode Gen 2 at 5 for its 22 g pour-over, and Niche's guide puts a V60 between 35 and 45 on the Niche Zero.

## Grinding findings that changed how I brew

Two published findings changed how I brew:

- **Cold beans grind to a narrower spread.** Uman and colleagues (2016) ground the same coffee at four temperatures, from room temperature down to liquid nitrogen. Colder beans gave a narrower spread and a smaller average size, and the biggest change came between room temperature and freezer beans at −2°F&nbsp;(−19°C). If you freeze beans, our [single-dose freezing guide](/blog/single-dose-coffee-freezing-guide/) covers how.
- **New burrs change as they wear in.** Niche says the Niche Zero's burrs are at peak consistency after about 10 kg of coffee. Turin says new burrs need about 10 lb (4.5 kg, calculated) to stabilize, and recommends simply using the grinder rather than wasting coffee to get there. In Gagné's 2019 measurements, his new burrs ground coarser, then more evenly, over 24 lb of coffee. Our [burr seasoning](/blog/burr-seasoning-grind-quality/) post has more.

## Hand grinder or electric?

Capacity is the practical difference. 1Zpresso lists 30–35 g for its JX-Pro hand grinder and Timemore 20 g for the standard C3, enough for a cup or two. Baratza lists the Encore ESP's hopper at 300 g, though single-dose electrics hold less: about 50 g in the DF64, by Turin's figure. If you grind for several people every morning, an electric saves the cranking; for one or two cups, see our [manual grinder comparison](/blog/top-10-best-manual-coffee-grinders/). Building an espresso setup? Our [espresso machine reviews](/blog/espresso-battle-royale-reviews-of-gaggia-breville-bambino-and-phillips-3200/) cover the other half.
