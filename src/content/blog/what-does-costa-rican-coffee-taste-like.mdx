## What Costa Rican coffee tastes like

Hoffmann describes Costa Rican coffees as typically very clean and sweet, though often very light-bodied, and notes that the country's micro mills now produce a wider range of flavors and styles. These are notes a Costa Rican coffee *can* show; no single bag has them all:

- **Chocolate,** from milk chocolate to darker cocoa depending on the roast
- **Citrus and stone fruit:** lemon, orange, peach, apricot, especially in lighter roasts
- **Floral sweetness:** jasmine, honeysuckle
- **Nuts and caramel**
- **Brown spice:** cinnamon, nutmeg or vanilla in some lots
- **Gentle, apple-like acidity** rather than a sharp, lemony one

At its best the acidity makes the cup feel lively without being sharp. If you're exploring [what single-origin coffee means](/blog/what-is-single-origin-coffee/), Costa Rica is a good place to taste the idea, because so many lots are traceable to a farm or mill.

## Arabica, and the robusta question

You'll still read that growing robusta is illegal in Costa Rica. That was true for 30 years, but it isn't now. According to ICAFE, the national coffee institute, robusta was banned from 1988, when a decree prohibited planting it except for research, and the existing trees were removed in 1990. The aim was to protect the quality image of Costa Rica's exports, and to protect farms from the coffee berry borer, which hadn't yet reached the country.

A 2018 decree, No. 41110-MAG, authorized robusta again. Planting is allowed only under ICAFE's policy: in zones it approves, with varieties it has evaluated, and with robusta kept separate from arabica so the two aren't mixed. ICAFE's board approved the current version of that policy in October 2024.

The authorization doesn't tell you how much robusta is actually planted. USDA's production figures still count Costa Rica's whole crop as arabica.

## Costa Rica's growing regions

Hoffmann and importers list the same eight regions. Hoffmann notes that there's wide variety in flavor within each one, so treat a region name as a starting point rather than a flavor promise.

| Region | Altitude | Harvest | Notes (Hoffmann) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tarrazú | 1,200–1,900 m | Nov–Mar | The country's highest farms; a dry season during harvest |
| Central Valley | 900–1,600 m | Nov–Mar | Around San José, Heredia and Alajuela; the oldest coffee region |
| West Valley | 700–1,600 m | Oct–Feb | Highest around Naranjo; Sarchí gives Villa Sarchi its name |
| Tres Ríos | 1,200–1,650 m | Nov–Mar | Small, east of San José; shrinking as land goes to housing |
| Orosi | 1,000–1,400 m | Aug–Feb | A long valley: Orosi, Cachí and Paraíso |
| Brunca | 600–1,700 m | Aug–Feb | Coto Brus and Pérez Zeledón; much Caturra and Catuaí |
| Turrialba | 500–1,400 m | Jul–Mar | An early harvest; less defined wet and dry seasons |
| Guanacaste | 600–1,300 m | Jul–Feb | Large region, little of it in coffee, mostly lower-grown |

Tarrazú is the famous name. Hoffmann adds a caution: the name became valuable enough that coffee from outside the region was once sold as Tarrazú. A bag that names the farm or mill as well as the region is easier to trust.

Harvest months shift with altitude and with the year's weather, so read the table as typical. ## The micro mill revolution

For a long time, Hoffmann writes, Costa Rican coffee earned good prices for being clean and pleasant rather than distinctive. Around 2000, most exports still carried big mills' brands, which hid where each coffee had been grown.

That changed in the mid to late 2000s, when many farmers bought small-scale processing equipment of their own. These micro mills let a farm or a small group process and sell its own lots, and the range of Costa Rican styles widened sharply. With about 90 percent of Costa Rican coffee producers owning small or medium farms, Hoffmann notes, it's now possible to find coffees traceable to a single farm or cooperative.

## Processing: washed, honey and natural

Processing changes the cup too. Costa Rica has a long washing tradition: Hoffmann dates the wet process there to 1830, with 200 wet mills by 1905. In his general account, washed coffees tend toward more acidity, more complexity and a cleaner cup.

**Honey processing** is closely tied to Costa Rica. Hoffmann lists it among the processes used there and in El Salvador, and the importer Café Imports says it originated in Costa Rica. The fruit's skin is removed by machine, but some of the sticky flesh (the mucilage, *miel* in Spanish) is left on the bean as it dries. Hoffmann describes it as close to the pulped natural process, which tends to add sweetness and body. He also notes that the depulpers can be set to leave a chosen share of the flesh, giving names like "100 percent honey" or "20 percent honey," and that more flesh means more risk of defects in drying.

Many Costa Rican honey lots are named by color instead: yellow, red or black. Those names aren't a standard measure. Café Imports reports that some mills set the color by how much mucilage they remove, while others leave all of it on and change the drying instead. Our [honey process guide](/blog/honey-process-colors-explained/) goes further.

**Natural** lots, dried in the whole cherry, are less common. Hoffmann notes that the natural process often adds fruit flavors such as blueberry, strawberry or tropical fruit, and sometimes wilder, fermented ones.

| Process | What it tends to do (Hoffmann) |
|---|---|
| Washed | More acidity, complexity and a clean cup |
| Honey | Close to pulped natural: more sweetness and body |
| Natural | Fruit flavors; sometimes wild or fermented ones |

These are tendencies across many lots, not rules for a particular bag. For how the methods work, see our [processing guide](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/).

## Varieties you'll see on the bag

Café Imports lists Caturra, Catuaí, Bourbon, Villa Sarchi, Villa Lobos, SL28 and Gesha as common Costa Rican varieties. World Coffee Research's catalog describes the main ones:

- **Caturra:** a dwarf mutation of Bourbon, found in Brazil. WCR rates its quality potential "Good."
- **Catuaí:** a cross of Mundo Novo and Caturra bred in Brazil, also rated "Good."
- **Villa Sarchi:** a dwarf Bourbon mutation discovered in Costa Rica's Alajuela province in the 1950s or 1960s. WCR rates it "Good," and it isn't widely grown outside Costa Rica. Hoffmann calls it capable of excellent cup quality.
- **Costa Rica 95:** a high-yielding Catimor, Timor Hybrid 832/1 crossed with Caturra, selected by ICAFE. WCR rates its quality potential "Low," and reports that scientific evaluation has recently confirmed it is susceptible to coffee leaf rust in Costa Rica.

WCR rates Caturra, Catuaí and Villa Sarchi as susceptible to leaf rust too; our [coffee leaf rust history](/blog/coffee-leaf-rust-hemileia-vastatrix-history/) covers why that matters.

## What to look for on a bag

- **A farm or mill name.** It's the clearest sign of a traceable micro-lot, and it lets you look for the same farm's coffee next year.
- **The region,** as a starting point for flavor rather than a promise.
- **The process.** Washed for clarity; honey or natural for more sweetness, fruit and body.
- **The variety,** if the roaster lists it.
- **A harvest or roast date.** Hoffmann's regional tables put most Costa Rican harvests between July and March, and the importer Café Imports lists typical US arrivals from May to July.

Roast level changes the cup too; our guide to [how coffee is roasted](/blog/how-is-coffee-roasted/) explains what changes from light to dark.

## Brewing Costa Rican coffee

Hoffmann notes that paper filters give the cleanest cup, which suits a coffee prized for being clean and sweet. If the cup tastes sour or bitter, change the grind first.
