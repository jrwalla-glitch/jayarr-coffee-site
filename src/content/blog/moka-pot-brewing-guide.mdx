A moka pot brews with steam. Water heated in the closed base builds pressure that pushes it up a tube, through the coffee in the basket and out into the top chamber. James Hoffmann credits its invention, in 1933, to Alfonso Bialetti, who held the patent, and Bialetti still makes the Moka Express.

It isn't an espresso machine. Italy's espresso institute certifies espresso brewed with water at about 9 bar of pressure; a moka pot has only the steam in its base, and Navarini and colleagues found that extraction starts at fairly low temperatures, before the water reaches the boil. Hoffmann writes that a moka pot tends to make very strong, very bitter coffee; his method below is meant to keep the bitterness down.

## The pot decides the amounts

A moka pot is built to brew one batch. In Hoffmann's words, you fill the coffee holder and then fill the base with water to just below the valve, "so there is little room for manoeuvre." Bialetti's manual says the same: fill the base up to the lower edge of the safety valve.

- **The basket sets the coffee.** Fill it level. Bialetti says to fill the funnel generously, without pressing.
- **The valve sets the water.** Fill to just below it. Hoffmann warns not to cover it, because it's a safety valve that stops too much pressure building up. Bialetti adds that water above the valve makes the coffee too watery.
- **The size sets the batch.** To make less coffee, use a smaller pot rather than a part-filled basket.

Bialetti's "cups" are small: the company says a moka cup is about a 2 oz espresso serving, and its US listing gives a 3-cup pot as 4.4 oz and a 6-cup pot as 9.1 oz.

Our Brew Guide works the same way. You weigh what your basket holds level, and it estimates the water at about ten times that, 1:10, and the coffee in the cup with its usual 2 g of water kept back per gram of coffee. For a basket that holds 15 g, that's about 150 g of water and about 120 g, or 4 US fl oz, of coffee (calculated). The water figure is an estimate; your valve decides. The Brew Guide has no strength setting for a moka pot: for a milder cup, add hot water to the coffee.

## Step by step

This follows Hoffmann's method from *The World Atlas of Coffee*, which the Brew Guide uses, with Bialetti's safety instructions.

- **Coffee:** enough to fill the basket level, ground a little coarser than for espresso
- **Water:** just-boiled, to just below the safety valve
- **Time:** about 3 minutes to set up; the Brew Guide expects about 4 to 5 minutes on the heat, though your stove and pot will change that; up to about 8 minutes in all
- **Makes:** whatever your pot makes; about 120 g (4 US fl oz) from a basket that holds 15 g (calculated, as above)

1. Grind the coffee a little coarser than for espresso and fill the basket level with the rim. Do not press it down.
2. Boil the kettle and pour the hot water into the base, to just below the safety valve and never over it.
3. Drop in the basket, wipe any grounds off the rim, and screw the top on firmly, holding the hot base with a towel.
4. Put the pot on low to medium heat, with any flame no wider than the base.
5. Take the pot off the heat as soon as it starts to gurgle or sputter.
6. Run the base under cold water to stop the brew, stir the coffee in the top, and serve straight away.

**The grind.** Bialetti says to use coffee ground for moka and never a fine grind. Hoffmann grinds "quite fine," like salt, but a little coarser than most people choose, to keep bitterness down. The Brew Guide's band is about 300–450&nbsp;µm, like fine sand: coarser than espresso, finer than a V60. Our [grind size guide](/blog/coffee-grind-size-guide-what-grind-for-every-brew-method/) shows the texture.

**The gurgle.** Hoffmann's cue to stop is the gurgling sound: by then most of the water has been pushed up and steam is starting to come through the coffee, which makes it more bitter. Cooling the base under the tap condenses the steam so the pressure drops and the brew stops.

**Safety.** Bialetti's manual says never to heat the pot without water in it, to point the safety valve away from you, to handle it by the handle and knob because the surfaces get hot, and to let it cool before opening it.

## Bialetti's method and Hoffmann's

The two agree on most things and differ mainly on the water and the lid. If your own pot's manual says something different, follow it.

| Step | Bialetti | Hoffmann (and the Brew Guide) |
|---|---|---|
| Water in the base | Room temperature (the manual says cold), up to the valve | Just-boiled, to just below the valve |
| Coffee | Ground for moka, not too fine; fill generously, don't press | Quite fine, like salt; fill level, don't compress |
| Heat | Low; on gas, no wider than the base; on electric, never the highest setting | Low to medium |
| Lid | Closed while it brews | Open, to watch the coffee come through |
| When to stop | When it starts to gurgle | At the gurgle, then cool the base under the tap |

**Why hot water?** Hoffmann's reason: the pot spends less time on the heat and the coffee in the basket doesn't get as hot, which helps keep bitterness down. It also means a hot base to hold while you screw it together, so use a towel.

## Which coffee to use

Hoffmann finds it hard to make light-roasted, dense or very acidic, juicy coffees taste good in a moka pot, because of its strong ratio and fast brew. He suggests a light espresso roast or a coffee grown at lower altitudes, and steers away from dark roasts because the method already tends toward bitterness. Bialetti asks for coffee ground for moka, not coffee ground for filter or espresso machines.

Moka coffee is strong, and Bialetti recommends drinking it as soon as it's poured. For a longer drink, add hot water, as the Brew Guide suggests, or milk.

## Fixing the cup

Change one thing at a time. The first fixes below are the ones our Fix My Coffee tool gives for a moka pot; the venting and leaking rows come from Bialetti's manual.

| What happens | Try first | Then |
|---|---|---|
| Bitter, harsh | Take the pot off the heat the moment it starts to sputter, and cool the base | Grind a touch coarser |
| Sour, thin | Grind one step finer, still coarser than espresso, basket level and unpressed | Use a slightly lower flame so it brews more slowly |
| Weak | Fill the basket level to the top, without pressing | Grind one step finer |
| Muddy, gritty | Grind one step coarser | Check that the filter plate and gasket are clean and seated |
| Steam comes out of the valve | Check the coffee is ground for moka, isn't pressed, and the water isn't above the valve | If it keeps happening, stop using the pot and contact the dealer (Bialetti) |
| Leaks at the joint | Wipe coffee off the rim and screw the top on firmly | Check the gasket for wear and replace it |

## Choosing a moka pot

**Size.** Buy the size you'll brew, since a pot makes one batch. Bialetti's cups are about 2 oz each.

**Aluminum or stainless steel.** As published by the maker, the classic aluminum Moka Express works on gas and electric hobs, and on induction only with Bialetti's induction plate. The stainless-steel Venus is made of 18/10 stainless steel and works on gas, electric and induction, except the 2-cup size. Hoffmann prefers stainless steel, and notes that a scare story about aluminum some years ago was false. Neither Bialetti pot goes in the dishwasher.

## Cleaning and care

All of this is from Bialetti's manual unless noted.

- **First use:** wash it with water and run it once with water only, then brew at least three pots of coffee and throw them away.
- **After each use:** let it cool, then wash it by hand in warm water, without detergent or abrasive sponges. It can't go in the dishwasher.
- **Storage:** dry every part and store it unscrewed. Hoffmann adds that leaving it locked together ages the rubber seal faster.
- **Gasket:** replace it when it's worn, and at least once a year anyway.
- **Safety valve:** during washing, move the small piston that sticks out of the valve, so limescale doesn't clog it.
- **Descaling:** now and then, fill the base to the valve with water and two teaspoons of citric acid or vinegar, run it once without coffee, throw the liquid away, rinse, and brew a pot.
