Coffee came to Yemen from Ethiopia, and from Yemen it went around the world. James Hoffmann, in *The World Atlas of Coffee*, calls Yemen's coffee distinctive, perhaps challenging and certainly unusual, and points out that its trade has never been turned into a commodity. That history is also why "mocha" is, in his words, probably the most confusing word in coffee.

## How coffee came to Yemen

Nobody knows exactly when coffee crossed the Red Sea. Hoffmann writes that it came from Ethiopia, through trade or with pilgrims traveling from Ethiopia to Mecca, and that it was well established in Yemen in the 15th and 16th centuries.

The earliest written records of coffee as a drink come from Yemen's Sufis, Muslim mystics who held all-night devotions. The historian Jonathan Morris writes that during the 15th century they took up an infusion of dried coffee husks, *qishr*, on the advice of a mufti, Muhammad al-Dhabani, who died in 1470. From there the drink spread to Mecca, Medina and Cairo. In 1511 Mecca's governor had coffee declared un-Islamic, but the authorities in Cairo overruled him, and Istanbul had its first coffeehouse in the 1550s.

Jeff Koehler, in *Where the Wild Coffee Grows*, adds a caution: people in the Ethiopian forests where coffee grows wild were very likely brewing it first, but they left no written record.

## Mocha: the port and the name

Morris dates the terraced coffee plots of Yemen's mountain villages to the 1540s, and writes that for the next 150 years they were the world's only source of cultivated coffee. The beans were carried down to Bayt al-Faqih on the coast, then shipped from Mocha and Hudaydah. Hudaydah supplied the Ottoman Empire, but Mocha supplied the rest of the Muslim world and, later, the European traders who came to buy. So coffee bought there took the port's name.

Yemen guarded the trade. A story repeated in Mark Pendergrast's *Uncommon Grounds* says no beans could leave unless they had first been steeped in boiling water or partly roasted, so they couldn't sprout. Koehler thinks that was probably just rumor: boiling every bean would have been impractical and would likely have spoiled the coffee. Later edicts simply banned taking out plants.

The monopoly still broke:

- **India.** In the best-known story, a Sufi pilgrim, Baba Budan, carried seven seeds from Mocha to the hills of Mysore. Koehler dates it to 1670 and calls it alleged; Pendergrast says only "sometime during the 1600s."
- **Java.** In 1696, a Dutch East India Company ship took coffee from India's Malabar Coast, descended from Baba Budan's seeds according to Koehler, to Java. That first planting was lost, and a second one around 1700 took hold.
- **Europe and the Americas.** According to World Coffee Research (WCR), a plant from Java reached Amsterdam's botanical garden in 1706 and one was shared with France in 1714. Plants from Paris reached Martinique in 1723, and their descendants, the Typica variety, spread through the Caribbean and Latin America.
- **Réunion.** In the early 1700s, WCR writes, French missionaries took coffee from Yemen to Bourbon Island, now Réunion. That gave the world the Bourbon variety, which reached Brazil around 1860.

Java soon overtook Mocha. Morris notes that 90 percent of the coffee sold in Amsterdam in 1721 came from Mocha, and by 1726 Java supplied 90 percent. The pairing of the two gave us what he calls the world's first coffee blend, Mocha Java.

Because Typica and Bourbon both came from Yemeni stock, a 2021 genetic study (Montagnon and colleagues) describes it as established that almost all cultivated arabica varieties originated in Yemen. Ethiopia's own landraces are a separate story.

## "Mocha" today: three different things

- **A Yemeni coffee.** Bags still use "Mocha" or "Mokha" for Yemeni coffee, after the old port.
- **A blend.** Hoffmann notes that the name Mocha-Java was never protected, so it became a style that roasters use for a blend they like, whatever its beans. The old spelling "moka" also stuck to some potent natural coffees from elsewhere, such as Ethiopia's Moka Harrar.
- **A drink.** The café mocha is espresso mixed with hot chocolate, a use of the word that, Hoffmann says, only confuses things further. Our [mocha recipe](/blog/making-a-mocha-with-and-without-espresso-machine/) is about that drink.

## Growing coffee in Yemen today

Hoffmann writes that only 3 percent of Yemen's land is suitable for farming, because water is so scarce. Coffee grows on terraces at high altitude and needs irrigation, much of it from deep groundwater that won't be replaced. Soils are rarely fertilized. The importer Café Imports adds that much of the water available for farming goes to qat, a stimulant crop that has displaced coffee and many food crops.

Farms are tiny. Hoffmann cites Yemen's 2000 census: about 99,000 households grew coffee, and he estimates the average household produced just 113 kg (249 lb) of raw coffee that year.

How much Yemen grows now isn't well measured:

- **USDA** stopped publishing Yemen in its coffee data after the 2014/15 season, when it estimated 130,000 60 kg bags. That's under 0.1 percent of its world total for that season (calculated from USDA's data).
- **Hoffmann** gives 125,000 bags for 2016.

Hoffmann also writes that since the civil war began in 2015, the amount produced has fallen only a little, but exports have dropped to a little over half their prewar level, and about half of exports go to Saudi Arabia.

## Where it grows

Hoffmann describes Yemen's coffee regions by governorate. Only 12 of the 21 governorates grow coffee, and he notes that Western spellings of the names vary a lot.

| Governorate | Altitude | Harvest | Local variety names (Hoffmann) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sana'a | 1,500–2,200 m (4,900–7,200 ft) | Oct–Dec | Mattari, Ismaili, Harazi, Dawairi, Dawarani, Sanani, Haimi |
| Raymah | About 1,850 m (6,050 ft) | Oct–Dec | Raymi, Dwairi, Bura'ae, Kubari, Tufahi, Udaini |
| Mahweet | 1,500–2,100 m (4,900–6,900 ft) | Oct–Dec | Mahwaiti, Tufahi, Udaini, Kholani |
| Sa'dah | About 1,800 m (5,900 ft) | Oct–Dec | Dawairi, Tufahi, Udaini, Kholani |
| Hajjah | 1,600–1,800 m (5,200–5,900 ft) | Oct–Dec | Shani, Safi, Masrahi, Shami, Bazi, Mathani, Jua'ari |

Sana'a is the largest producer. Many names do double duty: Hoffmann notes that "Mattari" can mean both the area around Bani Matar and a variety, probably named after it. Café Imports lists Bani Matar, Bani Hammad, Bura'a, Haraaz and Haimateen as growing regions, and gives the harvest as October to March.

## The varieties: local names, unusual genetics

Hoffmann puts the huge number of heirloom varieties in Yemen down to its isolation, its harsh conditions and its remoteness, with most of them peculiar to their own growing regions. Genetic work is starting to sort them out, with two findings so far:

- **The names don't track the genes.** Montagnon and colleagues (2022) recorded which variety names farmers used on 148 farms and genotyped 88 of their trees. Names like Udaini, Tufahi and Dawairi follow a clear geographic pattern, but they showed no significant link to the trees' genetics. On a bag, a variety name tells you about local custom more than about the plant.
- **Some Yemeni coffee is unlike anything else.** Their 2021 study found some Yemeni trees grouped with the familiar Typica–Bourbon varieties, and others with SL34. One group, though, was found only in Yemen and matched no known cultivated variety or Ethiopian sample. The authors call it the "New-Yemen" cluster.

One of the authors of both studies, Faris Sheibani, founded the Yemeni coffee company Qima Coffee. Our guide to [coffee varieties](/blog/coffee-varieties-explained/) explains Typica, Bourbon and the rest.

## How Yemeni coffee is processed

Hoffmann describes the traditional method, and Café Imports how its own lots differ:

- **Picking.** Coffee is picked by hand, with pickers visiting each tree several times a season, but selective picking isn't common, so underripe and overripe cherries are often harvested.
- **Drying.** The whole cherries are dried in the sun, often on the roofs of the farmers' houses. Roofs rarely have enough room, so the cherries are often piled too deep to dry evenly. That causes defects such as uneven drying, fermentation and mold.
- **Sorting.** Café Imports says the traditional dried naturals it buys are sorted extensively. For its own lots, it pays a premium for ripe-picked cherry dried on raised beds, which it says gives a cleaner, fruit-forward cup.

Raised drying tables let air flow around the cherries, which, Hoffmann explains, gives more even drying than a patio. Our guide to [processing methods](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/) compares natural, washed and honey processing.

*Qishr* (also spelled qesher or q'shr) is the dried husk of the cherry, and Hoffmann notes that Yemenis often brew it like tea. It's a cousin of the cascara now made in Central America.

## What Yemeni coffee tastes like

Hoffmann's summary is "wild, complex and pungent." Some people find the slightly fermented fruit off-putting, he writes, while others prize it highly. Café Imports says Yemeni coffees grown in the dry, full-sun climate tend toward deep chocolate tones, and that natural processing adds a winey character.

These are notes a Yemeni coffee *can* show; no single bag has them all:

- **Dried fruit:** raisin, date, fig, dried apricot
- **Wine-like:** a deep, vinous fruitiness
- **Chocolate:** dark chocolate and cocoa
- **Spice:** cinnamon, cardamom, nutmeg or allspice in some lots
- **Soft acidity:** more like a dried fig than a lemon
- **Heavy body:** full and almost syrupy, with a long finish

Some of the wildness comes from the processing itself. Hoffmann writes that the natural process often adds fruit flavors, but sometimes notes described as barnyard, ferment or worse, and that the high demand for Yemeni coffee rests partly on flavors that come from defects.

## Buying Yemeni coffee

Hoffmann warns that buying Yemeni coffee blind stacks the odds against you, and you may get something that tastes dirty or rotten. His reasons:

- **Traceability usually stops at the region.** Coffees rarely trace back to a single farm, and lots are often blended before export, then sold under the most valuable name.
- **Some coffee waits a long time to ship.** Some exporters sell their oldest stocks first, so coffee can sit for years at the point of export.
- **Some "Yemeni" coffee isn't.** Since the war began, he notes more Ethiopian coffees being mislabeled as Yemeni to command its premium.

What helps:

- **Buy from a roaster or importer you already trust,** who names the region (Bani Matar or Haraaz, for example) and, ideally, the importer or exporter.
- **Look for a harvest or roast date.** Hoffmann puts the harvest at October to December; Café Imports gives October to March, with arrivals that vary.
- **Know the specialty exporters.** Qima Coffee, founded by Faris Sheibani in 2016 during the conflict, said in 2021 that it worked with more than 3,000 farmers in 55 communities. With the Alliance for Coffee Excellence it has run Best of Yemen auctions; the 2021 edition was the first open to any Yemeni farmer, not only those in Qima's network. Port of Mokha was founded by Mokhtar Alkhanshali, whose story Dave Eggers tells in *The Monk of Mokha* (2018).
- **Don't read "Mocha Java" as Yemeni.** A blend with that name may contain no Yemeni coffee at all.

## Brewing Yemeni coffee

Any brewer works; the choice is about texture. Hoffmann explains that a French press's metal mesh lets some of the coffee's oil and fine particles into the cup, which gives a bigger, richer body, while a paper filter gives a cleaner cup. If you love Yemeni coffee's heavy body, the French press keeps it. If you'd rather taste the fruit more clearly, use a paper filter, as in our [V60 guide](/blog/ultimate-pour-over-coffee-guide-and-best-v60-technique/).
