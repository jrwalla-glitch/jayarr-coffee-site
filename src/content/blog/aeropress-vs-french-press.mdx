Both brewers steep coffee in hot water and then separate it from the grounds, so neither needs a pouring technique. They differ in how much they make, how long they take, what the filter lets through and how you clean up. Those differences usually decide which one fits your mornings.

## Which one suits you

| If you mostly… | Choose | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Make one mug at a time | AeroPress | It brews one serving per press, up to 10 oz in the Original, and AeroPress puts brewing and cleanup at under 2 minutes. |
| Make coffee for three or more | French press | One batch fills several mugs; Fellow's Clara holds 24 oz. The AeroPress Original XL makes 20 oz. |
| Travel or camp | AeroPress | AeroPress calls it shatterproof; the parts you'd pack weigh 6.5 oz (about 184 g). |
| Want the heaviest body | French press | The metal mesh lets coffee oils and fine particles into the cup. |
| Want a clean, clear cup | AeroPress, with its paper filter | Paper holds back the oils and fines. |

## How each one brews

**A French press** is a full immersion brewer. The coffee steeps in all of the water, then a metal mesh plunger holds the grounds back while you pour. The mesh stops the grounds but not the oils, and some fine particles get through too; as Jessica Easto reminds readers of *Craft Coffee*, there is still sediment in a French press brew, most of it sunk to the bottom.

**An AeroPress** also steeps the coffee, but only briefly, in a plastic chamber, then you press it through a paper filter with the plunger. AeroPress describes its method as fast immersion, micro-filtration and air pressure, and says the press uses "air pressure and micro-filtration to brew faster." It calls its strongest brews "espresso style" coffee, not espresso. AeroPress also sells a stainless steel filter; like a French press's mesh, metal doesn't hold back the oils the way paper does.

## One 8 fl oz mug, brewed both ways

Here is the same mug, 8 fl oz (237 ml), made each way with our Brew Guide's numbers for a medium roast. The two recipes come from different published methods, so the doses, grinds and times differ.

### AeroPress

The Brew Guide's AeroPress is adapted from the Classic AeroPress Method in Jessica Easto's *Craft Coffee*, at the Brew Guide's own 1:14 ratio. Its 185°F&nbsp;(85°C) is both Easto's temperature and AeroPress's.

- **Coffee:** about 20 g, ground medium-fine, like fine sand (about 350–500&nbsp;µm)
- **Water:** 277 g at 185°F&nbsp;(85°C): 230 g in the chamber, then 47 g of hot water added to the mug after pressing, because the chamber holds only about 230 g with the coffee in it
- **Time:** about 2 minutes, pressed by about 1:20
- **Makes:** about 237 g, 8 US fl oz (237 ml). That's calculated: 277 g of water, less about 40 g held back by the grounds at roughly 2 g per gram of coffee.

1. Rinse a paper filter in the cap, lock it on, stand the AeroPress on a sturdy mug, add the coffee and tare the scale.
2. Start the timer and pour 230 g of water.
3. Stir for ten seconds.
4. At 0:30, press down slowly and steadily, and stop when you hear a hiss.
5. Top up the mug with 47 g of hot water.

### French press

The Brew Guide's French press is Jessica Easto's eight-minute method, at the Brew Guide's 1:15 ratio. Easto's own recipe is 28.5 g of coffee to 400 g of water, about 1:14, which is the Brew Guide's "stronger" setting.

- **Coffee:** about 18 g, ground extra coarse, like coarse sea salt (about 900–1,200&nbsp;µm)
- **Water:** 273 g, just off the boil, 205°F&nbsp;(96°C)
- **Time:** 8 minutes of steeping, then pour straight away
- **Makes:** about 237 g, 8 US fl oz (237 ml). That's calculated: 273 g of water, less about 36 g held back by the grounds at roughly 2 g per gram of coffee.

1. Warm the press, add the coffee and tare the scale.
2. Start the timer and pour all 273 g of water, so all the grounds are wet.
3. At 0:30 to 0:45, stir gently to sink the crust.
4. Put the lid on with the plunger up, and leave it.
5. At 8:00, press gently and pour all of the coffee out straight away.

Easto's reason for the long steep: following barista Nick Cho, she found that extremely coarse grounds and longer steeps, up to eight minutes, give a more even, delicate cup. She also gives a five-minute version with a coarse grind at 1:16 for busier mornings.

## What the makers' own instructions say

The makers keep it simpler, and the French press makers steep for half as long as the Brew Guide does.

**AeroPress,** for the Original with freshly ground coffee: one heaping scoop or 16–18 g of medium-fine coffee, water at 185°F (85°C), or boiling water if your kettle can't be set to a temperature. Fill to the 4 on the chamber, stir gently for a few seconds (3 on its website, about 5 in its 2024 printed instructions), then push the plunger in about ½ inch to stop drips. Wait 30 seconds, or 60 for store-bought medium-ground coffee, and press with gentle, constant pressure until the hissing begins and ends. AeroPress strongly discourages the inverted, upside-down method, because the brewer is less stable that way and can tip hot coffee onto you.

**French press makers** steep for about 4 minutes. Fellow's recipe for its Clara uses a 1:14 ratio, water at 195–205°F (91–96°C) and a 30-second stir; the filter goes in just above the water, pressed down about 1 cm to keep the grounds under, for 4 minutes before plunging. Espro's guide for its 18 oz press uses 1:15 for medium roasts, the same 195–205°F, a steep of up to 4 minutes, then a slow press to a count of 20.

So the makers say 4 minutes and Easto says 8, with a coarser grind. Easto writes that in her and co-author Andreas Willhoff's home experiments, four minutes or less made a balanced cup too hard to get. Both are starting points: pick one, then adjust the grind or the time by taste. Our [French press guide](/blog/how-to-use-a-french-press-the-most-amazing-coffee-youve-ever-had/) also covers a slower method that lets the fines settle before you pour.

## In the cup: body, sediment and the filter

The filter makes most of the difference. Coffee beans carry oils, including cafestol and kahweol, that hot water pulls into the brew. Paper filters hold them back, which is why paper-filtered coffee doesn't raise LDL cholesterol while boiled and plunger (French press) coffee do, according to a review by Rob Urgert and Martijn Katan. Those same oils, with the fine particles the mesh lets through, give a French press cup its heavier body and its sediment; a paper-filtered AeroPress cup is lighter and clearer.

If you drink several cups of unfiltered coffee a day and are watching your cholesterol, ask your clinician whether paper-filtered coffee is worth choosing.

Two habits help a French press. Pour the coffee off the grounds as soon as it's done, because Easto notes the grounds at the bottom keep extracting even after you press. And when you pour for several people, pour each cup a little at a time, in turns, so the last cup doesn't get all the sediment.

## Living with them

- **Cleanup.** AeroPress: eject the puck into the bin and rinse; AeroPress says a rinse is enough day to day because the plunger wipes the chamber, and the Original is top-rack dishwasher safe. French press: tip out the wet grounds and rinse the carafe and plunger; Fellow suggests warm, soapy water for the Clara, and says its mesh filter is the only dishwasher-safe part.
- **Travel.** AeroPress calls the Original compact, lightweight and shatterproof. It weighs 7.75 oz (about 220 g) with its scoop and stirrer, and 6.5 oz (about 184 g) for just the chamber, plunger and cap. A glass French press can break; insulated steel presses like the Clara don't, but they're heavier: Fellow lists the Clara at 917 g.
- **Care.** AeroPress makes the Original from BPA-free polypropylene. To make the plunger's silicone seal last, it says to eject the grounds right after brewing and store the plunger pushed all the way through the chamber; replacement seals are sold separately.
- **Capacity.** AeroPress says the Original makes one 10 oz serving, or up to three shots of espresso-style coffee, per press. For more, you press again, or use the Original XL and its 20 oz carafe.
