## What the Prismo is

The Prismo is made by Fellow, which says it isn't affiliated with or endorsed by AeroPress. It twists onto the bottom of an AeroPress chamber in place of the filter cap, and has two parts:

1. **A pressure-actuated valve.** It stays sealed while the coffee steeps, so nothing drips through, and opens when you press. Fellow says it allows a build-up of pressure for espresso-style coffee and gives a no-drip seal for recipes that would otherwise mean inverting the AeroPress. Fellow suggests it for full-immersion brews, cold brew, hot and iced tea and Americanos too.
2. **A reusable 150-micron etched steel filter,** set in a rubber gasket, so you don't need paper. Fellow's instructions say the filter can't be removed from its gasket; you place it with the Fellow logo facing you.

In our video we found it nicely made: cast plastic, a rubber seal, and a filter that's a little bendy.

**As published by Fellow,** checked October 18, 2026:

- **Price and stock:** listed at $24.95, in stock. In 2020 our video put it at around £20 ($25). Replacement valves (two for $2) and filters ($5) are sold separately; Fellow says to replace the valve if the Prismo leaks or needs only light pressure to push coffee through.
- **Fit:** not compatible with the AeroPress Go, the AeroPress XL or any AeroPress made from 2005 to 2009; compatible with the AeroPress Clear.
- **Materials:** polypropylene body, silicone valve and a stainless steel filter; 2.75 inches across and 1 inch tall; BPA-free.
- **Care:** rinse the body and filter after every use, and soak them in warm, soapy water for 15 minutes if oils build up. The product page says hand wash only. (Fellow's 2021 safety and use guide called it top-rack dishwasher safe; the current page is the one to follow.)
- **Safety:** don't brew over thin glass. Fellow says the Prismo "creates a great deal of pressure," and AeroPress says the same of its own brewer: never press into a thin-walled glass mug or carafe.

## Fellow's espresso-style recipe

This is the espresso-style recipe in Fellow's safety and use guide. The 2021 version online gives the same steps our 2020 video followed for the Prismo cup:

1. Use the freshest coffee you can; Fellow suggests an espresso blend or a dark roast.
2. Heat the water to 212°F&nbsp;(100°C).
3. Twist the Prismo onto the AeroPress chamber until all its tabs lock.
4. Add 20 g of coffee, ground very fine, then 50 ml of water. Stir hard for 20 seconds.
5. Let it steep for 1 minute in all, counting the stir.
6. Press firmly and keep up a steady pressure until the plunger reaches the coffee.

That's about 1:2.5 coffee to water (calculated: 50 ÷ 20). Fellow's guide adds that if a clean Prismo creates too much pressure, you should grind coarser.

## Espresso-style, not espresso

Fellow calls the result espresso-style, and AeroPress says the same of its own Flow Control cap: an AeroPress "does not make real espresso," it writes, but a concentrated, espresso-style coffee with a crema-like foam. Neither company's pages that we checked give a pressure figure for these caps. An espresso machine pushes the water through with a pump; our [guide to what espresso is](/blog/what-espresso-actually-is/) explains what that changes.

## The blind taste test

We used Taylors of Harrogate's Lazy Sunday, a gentle, easygoing ground coffee we know well, and made three small, strong cups with the same amount of coffee and water: one with the Prismo and Fellow's method, one with a standard upright AeroPress, and one with an inverted AeroPress. A partner brewed all three and noted which cup was which, so the tasting was blind. The three cups looked almost identical.

- **Upright AeroPress (tasted first):** a rich aroma of dark fruits, cranberry and red currant. Smooth and not too bitter, a little tart, nicely balanced with dark chocolate and cranberry. Pretty good, but mellow for an espresso-style shot.
- **Inverted AeroPress (tasted second):** a slightly smokier aroma. Very fruity and much stronger, fuller-bodied and more bitter at first sip. When we ranked the three, it was the one with the best balance of chocolate and fruit, a really nice texture, not too smoky and not too sharp.
- **Prismo (tasted third):** a lovely dark chocolate aroma, but the cup was a little vinegary and sharp, with a rough, grainy texture that may have come through the filter. Strong but not delivering much flavor: bitter, vinegary, and it had lost the fruit and the sweetness.

| Rank (2020) | Method | What we tasted |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | Inverted AeroPress | Best balance of chocolate and fruit, nice texture |
| 2nd | Upright AeroPress | Smooth and balanced, but mellow |
| 3rd | Fellow Prismo | Bitter, vinegary and a little grainy |

Why the gap? After the reveal we could only guess. The inverted cup had about a minute of steeping after the stir, which an upright AeroPress, dripping from the start, doesn't get. And 20 g of coffee to 50 ml of water, pushed under pressure, is an intense ratio; we thought Fellow's measurements might need tweaking. These are hypotheses from one tasting, not measurements.

## The inverted method, and what replaced it

The inverted AeroPress won our tasting, and in 2020 it was the method we were used to. But flipping a chamber full of hot coffee can be messy, and it can go wrong. AeroPress now strongly discourages it, because the brewer is less stable upside down and can tip and expose you to hot liquid. Our [AeroPress guide](/blog/why-and-how-you-should-use-an-aeropress/) covers its maker's upright method.

In our video, that was the best thing about the Prismo: its no-drip seal holds the coffee in place while it steeps, then lets go when you press. In our video it even felt like the AeroPress's inventor never meant people to turn it upside down.

AeroPress now sells its own answer. Its Flow Control Filter Cap uses the same idea, a pressure-actuated valve that stops drip-through, and AeroPress says these caps were designed to replace the inverted method. As published by each maker, checked October 18, 2026:

| | Fellow Prismo | AeroPress Flow Control Filter Cap |
|---|---|---|
| Listed price | $24.95 | $24.95 (standard size) |
| Filters | Its own 150-micron steel filter only | AeroPress paper or AeroPress metal filters |
| Fits | Not the Go, the XL or 2005–2009 models | Standard-size AeroPress models; a separate cap for the XL |

AeroPress's comparison also argues that the Prismo's steel filter is harder to clean, because espresso-machine cleaners damage the rubber gasket fixed to it. That's a competitor's claim; Fellow's own advice is a rinse after every use and a soak in soapy water when oils build up. If you'd like the no-drip seal with a paper filter, the Flow Control cap is the one that takes paper.

## Cleanup

We made quite a few Prismo cups that week, and we found it a nightmare to clean. The puck didn't come away from the filter well, which takes away one of the AeroPress's best features, how easy it is to tidy up. Maybe we needed more practice, but for us it was a pain.

## Our verdict (2020)

The Prismo didn't do well for us. It came last, behind both plain AeroPress methods, and we wanted to tweak Fellow's measurements before we could truly vouch for it. We still liked it as a fun accessory: the no-drip seal does away with the riskiest part of the inverted method, and it comes with a metal filter, which can be pricey on its own. Our 2020 video's description put it this way: the jury is still out.

Today, the same no-drip steep is also available from AeroPress's own Flow Control cap, which takes paper filters. If you'd still like the Prismo, Fellow sells it directly, and Amazon lists the <a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Fellow+Prismo+Attachment+for+AeroPress&tag=jayarrcoffee-20" rel="sponsored noopener">Fellow Prismo Attachment for AeroPress</a>, sold by Fellow, at $24.95 when checked October 18, 2026.
