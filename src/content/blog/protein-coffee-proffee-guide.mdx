Proffee puts two things many people already have in the morning into one glass. This page takes the two halves one at a time: what's in a serving, what the studies say each part does, and how to mix the two without lumps.

## What goes into a glass

**The coffee.** Any coffee works: brewed, espresso or cold brew. The caffeine depends on the coffee, not the protein. Our [Caffeine Calculator](/tools/caffeine/) estimates 105–195 mg for 8 fl oz (237 ml) of drip coffee and 120–150 mg for a double espresso; for 8 fl oz of cold brew diluted one to one, its model gives about 100–220 mg (calculated). Our [caffeine in coffee guide](/blog/caffeine-in-coffee-guide/) explains where those ranges come from.

**The protein.** Either a powder you mix in or a shake that's already liquid. The amounts below are as published by each maker on its US site, checked October 18, 2026.

| Product | Serving | Protein |
|---|---|---|
| Optimum Nutrition Gold Standard 100% Whey | 1 scoop | 24 g |
| Optimum Nutrition Gold Standard 100% Isolate | 1 scoop | 25 g |
| Vital Proteins Collagen Peptides, unflavored | 4 tbsp (20 g) | 18 g |
| fairlife Nutrition Plan, vanilla shake | 1 bottle, 340 ml | 30 g |

The fairlife bottle is about 11.5 fl oz (calculated). Optimum Nutrition describes both of its powders as mixing "with only a glass and spoon," and says whey protein isolate is the main protein source in its 100% Whey. Vital Proteins says its collagen "dissolves in hot and cold liquids," and its label adds that "Collagen peptides are an incomplete protein because they do not provide all the essential amino acids your body needs." The label lists its 18 g of protein at 0% of the Daily Value. If a plant protein is your choice, check its own label; for plant milks in coffee, see our [plant milks guide](/blog/best-plant-milks-for-coffee/).

## What the protein does

**How much you need.** The National Academies' Recommended Dietary Allowance for adults is 0.8 g of protein per kilogram of body weight a day, which is 56 g for a 70 kg reference man and 46 g for a 57 kg reference woman. For people who exercise, the International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN) puts 1.4–2.0 g per kilogram a day as enough for most to build and keep muscle and, for building muscle, suggests servings of 20–40 g spread every 3 to 4 hours. A 30 g shake is roughly half to two-thirds of the reference RDAs (calculated).

**Fullness.** A 2016 meta-analysis by Dhillon and colleagues pooled five preload studies in healthy people that met its strict criteria and found that higher-protein preloads raised fullness ratings more than lower-protein ones; a wider count of 28 studies pointed the same way. The authors warn against stretching that to body weight or eating behavior outside those conditions.

**The energy it takes to digest.** In a 2004 review, Klaas Westerterp gives diet-induced thermogenesis values of 20 to 30% for protein, 5 to 10% for carbohydrate and 0 to 3% for fat. The 18 to 30 g of protein in the products above is 72 to 120 kcal at the standard 4 kcal a gram, so that 20 to 30% comes to roughly 15 to 36 kcal (calculated). That's the size of the effect. It isn't evidence that proffee helps with weight loss, and we found no study that tested proffee for it.

**Does hot coffee ruin the protein?** Heat unfolds proteins, which is why whey clumps, but that doesn't necessarily make protein harder to digest. In one small study of five people with ileostomies, Evenepoel and colleagues measured cooked egg protein as 90.9% digestible against 51.3% for raw. That was egg, not whey, so read it as a pointer rather than proof for your shake.

## What the coffee does

The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) says caffeine "in moderate doses increases alertness and reduces sleepiness." What that means for work and focus is in our [coffee and productivity guide](/blog/coffee-and-productivity/).

**Before exercise.** The ISSN's 2021 position stand on caffeine says it has improved exercise performance "in many but not all studies," consistently at 3 to 6 mg per kilogram of body weight, and that the most common timing is 60 minutes before exercise. EFSA's safety view is narrower. It says single doses up to 200 mg, about 3 mg per kilogram, "do not raise safety concerns for the general healthy adult population," and that "the same amount of caffeine does not raise safety concerns when consumed less than two hours prior to intense physical exercise under normal environmental conditions." It adds that no studies are available in pregnant women or in middle-aged and older adults doing intense exercise. Those are population statements, not a dose for you; if you're pregnant, take medicines or have a heart condition, ask your clinician.

**Protein before or after.** The ISSN's protein position stand says benefits come from protein taken before or after a workout, and that the best time is "likely a matter of individual tolerance." If a whole shake sits badly before training, have the coffee first and the protein afterward.

**An afternoon proffee is still caffeine.** It carries the coffee's caffeine like any other cup. Our [coffee and sleep guide](/blog/coffee-and-sleep-when-to-stop/) sets out what the sleep studies found and how much is left at bedtime.

## How to mix it without clumps

Whey proteins are unstable to heat: in their review, Wijayanti, Bansal and Deeth describe heating as causing them to denature and aggregate. In a glass of hot coffee, that shows up as lumps. There are several ways around it.

**Blend it hot.** Optimum Nutrition's own Gold Standard 100% Whey Protein Coffee recipe blends 1 cup of hot coffee with 1 scoop of its Mocha Cappuccino whey and 10 g of coconut oil "until frothy." The maker lists it at 25 g of protein and 210 calories.

**Go iced.** Optimum Nutrition's Protein Iced Coffee pours 2 oz of espresso into a heatproof glass, stirs in about half a bottle of its vanilla Gold Standard ready-to-drink shake and ½ teaspoon of vanilla extract, then adds ice. The maker lists it at 12 g of protein. Two ounces of espresso is about two shots by volume, so the calculator's 120–150 mg applies.

**Use a protein made to dissolve hot.** Vital Proteins says its collagen peptides dissolve in hot and cold liquids, so they can go straight into a hot cup. Remember the label's own note: collagen isn't a complete protein.

**No blender? Our advice.** Stir the powder into a few spoonfuls of cold milk or water until smooth, then stir that into coffee that has cooled for a few minutes. Or make the whole drink cold: shake the powder with cold brew and ice in a sealed bottle.
