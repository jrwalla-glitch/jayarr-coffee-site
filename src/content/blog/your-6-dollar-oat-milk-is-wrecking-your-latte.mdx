If your oat milk latte comes out flat, thin or separated, the carton is the first thing to check, and the steaming the second. This guide covers both. For how oat milk compares with dairy on nutrition and foam, see our [oat milk vs dairy guide](/blog/oat-milk-vs-dairy-in-coffee/).

## Not every oat milk is made for steaming

The word "oat milk" on the front tells you little. One brand can sell several oat milks with different recipes, and only some of them are meant for a steam wand. Califia Farms is a clear example: of its three oat milks below, two say they're for lattes and one doesn't.

**Califia Organic Oatmilk** has three ingredients: water, organic oats and sea salt. Califia lists it as "No Oils. No Gums." and suggests mixing it into coffee, but makes no claim that it steams or froths.

**Califia Oat Barista Blend** adds sunflower oil and minerals (dipotassium phosphate, calcium carbonate and tricalcium phosphate) to the oat base, which takes the fat from 1.5 g to 7 g a cup. Califia says it "froths, foams, and steams perfectly for all your espresso creations."

**Califia Organic Oat Barista Blend** sits between the two: organic oat base, organic sunflower oil and sea salt, 5 g of fat a cup, and no acidity regulator. Califia makes the same steaming claim for it.

**Oatly Barista Edition**, which our oat milk vs dairy guide compares with whole milk, has 7 g of fat a cup from rapeseed oil, plus dipotassium phosphate. Oatly says it added the phosphate "as an acidity regulator" and that it "keeps our Barista Edition Oatmilk from separating when it hits your favorite hot drinks."

## What to read on the carton

Three things, in order:

1. **Does the maker say it's for steaming?** Look for "barista" and a claim that it steams or froths. That's the maker's own promise for a latte, and the best single guide.
2. **Is there added oil?** The barista cartons above add sunflower or rapeseed oil, bringing their fat to 5 to 7 g a cup; the plain three-ingredient carton has 1.5 g.
3. **Is there an acidity regulator?** Dipotassium phosphate is the one Oatly and Califia use. Its job, in Oatly's words, is to stop the milk separating in hot drinks.

A regulator isn't required to make a carton steamable: Califia's organic barista blend leaves it out and still claims to steam. But a plain carton with no oil, no regulator and no steaming claim is made for pouring, not for the wand.

Recipes differ between brands, and fat and protein figures alone don't predict foam. Two recent lab studies, which our [oat milk vs dairy guide](/blog/oat-milk-vs-dairy-in-coffee/) summarizes, found that foaming didn't track protein, and that how a drink is formulated, and how it's foamed, mattered more.

If you're choosing a carton for lattes, the two with an acidity regulator above are <a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Califia+Farms+Oat+Barista+Blend&tag=jayarrcoffee-20" rel="sponsored noopener" target="_blank">Califia Farms Oat Barista Blend</a> and <a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Oatly+Barista+Edition+Oatmilk&tag=jayarrcoffee-20" rel="sponsored noopener" target="_blank">Oatly Barista Edition</a>.

## How to steam oat milk for a latte

On their product pages, neither Califia nor Oatly gives a steaming method beyond heating and frothing, so start with the technique Scott Rao teaches for dairy in *The Professional Barista's Handbook*:

1. **Use a small pitcher.** Pick the smallest one that holds the milk you need, about a third to half full before steaming, and pour only as much milk as the drink takes.
2. **Purge the wand** into a damp cloth or the drip tray to clear condensed water.
3. **Set the tip just below the surface,** near the center, with the wand about 10 to 30 degrees from vertical, and open the steam fully.
4. **Add the air early.** Stretch the milk with a quiet, steady sucking sound and no visible bubbles, and finish before the milk reaches 100°F&nbsp;(38°C). Rao finds that above that, it's harder to make good foam.
5. **Then sink the tip and roll.** Raise the pitcher so the tip sits deeper and the milk keeps rolling, and turn the steam off as it approaches your target temperature.
6. **Wipe and purge the wand** straight away.

For a latte, Rao wants moderately aerated milk: once it has separated in the cup, the foam should be about ¼ in deep. The surface should look glossy, with no visible bubbles.

**The finishing temperature.** Rao heats dairy to 150 to 160°F&nbsp;(66 to 71°C). We found no finishing temperature from the oat milk makers. The lab studies in our oat milk vs dairy guide frothed oat milk (Hassan) and steamed barista plant drinks (Tarasov) at 149°F&nbsp;(65°C), just below Rao's range, so start at the low end, about 150°F&nbsp;(66°C), with a thermometer, and adjust by taste and texture.

## Pour straight away

Steamed milk doesn't wait. Rao's rule is to serve drinks before they separate: in his photo, a café latte has fully separated two minutes after it was made. If the milk has to wait a moment, spin the pitcher in tight circles, just fast enough to keep the surface glossy without making new bubbles. Rao uses spinning both to check the texture and to delay separation.

## When it goes wrong

| What you see | What to check | What to try |
|---|---|---|
| Big bubbles, stiff foam | Air added late or too much | Finish stretching before 100°F (38°C), then roll |
| Thin, with little foam | A carton not made for steaming | One its maker says steams |
| Separates soon after pouring | Milk left to stand | Pour straight away; spin the pitcher while you wait |
| Separates as it meets the coffee | No acidity regulator | A carton with dipotassium phosphate |

These are starting points drawn from the makers' claims and Rao's method, not a diagnosis; change one thing at a time.

## What about soy and other plant milks?

Soy milk has a different problem: it can curdle in coffee. In a 2019 lab study of an unsweetened soymilk in instant coffee, Mairi Brown and colleagues at the University of Strathclyde found that curdling depended mainly on temperature and on how much soymilk was in the cup: hotter mixtures separated, and cooling the mixture or adding more soymilk brought it back to a single phase. They found no clear pH threshold. They also note that soymilks that resist curdling generally contain acidity regulators and stabilizers, such as monopotassium phosphate and gellan gum.

In practice, that points to a barista soy milk with a regulator, not overheating it, and using enough of it relative to the coffee. Our [plant milk guide](/blog/best-plant-milks-for-coffee/) covers soy, pea, almond, coconut and others in coffee.
