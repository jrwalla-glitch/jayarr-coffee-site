Pour-over and French press are the two manual brewers most people choose between. Both are cheap, both can make excellent coffee, and they make noticeably different cups. The differences come from two things: how the water meets the coffee, and what the filter lets through.

## Which one suits you

| If you mostly… | Choose | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Brew one or two mugs and like to tinker | Pour-over | You control the pour, and Hario rates its V60 02 dripper for 1 to 4 cups. |
| Brew for three or more | French press | One batch fills several mugs; our Brew Guide's French press goes up to about a liter of water. |
| Want the heaviest body | French press | The metal mesh lets oils and fine particles into the cup. |
| Want the clearest cup | Pour-over | Paper holds the oils and fines back. |
| Want the least time at the counter | French press | Pour, stir once, then wait; a pour-over needs you at the kettle for about 2½ minutes. |
| Are watching your LDL cholesterol | Pour-over | Paper holds back cafestol; ask your clinician whether it matters for you. |

## Percolation vs immersion

A **pour-over is percolation:** water runs through a bed of coffee and out through a filter. A **French press is immersion:** the coffee steeps in all of the water, then a mesh holds the grounds back while you pour.

Jonathan Gagné explains in *The Physics of Filter Coffee* why that changes the cup. In percolation, fresh water keeps flowing past the grounds and carries what it dissolves away with it, which extracts efficiently. In immersion, the water around the grounds gets steadily more concentrated, so extraction slows down as the brew goes on. That makes immersion brews less sensitive to brew time and easier to repeat, he writes, but less efficient at extracting, and they have more body because fine particles stay suspended in the cup. Only percolation can usually make a very clean cup, because the bed of coffee filters itself as the water passes through. Immersion also tastes different for a subtler reason: the compounds that diffuse fastest end up over-represented in the cup.

How far can a French press extract? A 2021 study by Liang, Chan and Ristenpart at the University of California, Davis, brewed full-immersion coffee until it stopped changing. At equilibrium, extraction came out at about 21 percent across a wide range of brew ratios, so in their tests the ratio set the strength while barely moving the extraction. Their brews were open beakers left for 20 minutes or more, not a four- or eight-minute French press, and they note that a single full-immersion brew can't reach higher extraction yields, while flow methods such as drip offer more flexibility.

With a pour-over you can change the brew as it happens: how fast you pour, where, and in how many pours. Hario says pouring a V60 quickly gives a more delicate body and pouring slowly a heavier flavor. With a French press, you set the grind, ratio and time at the start and let it run.

## The filter: paper vs metal

Scott Rao, in *The Professional Barista's Handbook*, sums up the trade-off. The more a filter lets through, the more body the coffee has, but those insoluble bits can dull the acidity and muddle the flavor. Metal filters give a great deal of body and poor clarity; paper gives the least body and the most clarity. Of the French press in particular, he writes that its coarse mesh lets in a lot of particles and oil, which gives "a tremendous amount of body but poor flavor clarity." His tip for getting both: brew in the press, then pour it through a paper filter.

Jessica Easto, in *Craft Coffee*, puts it the same way: metal lets oils and fine sediment through, so the coffee feels heavy on the tongue, while paper traps them for what professionals call a cleaner cup. Which you prefer, she adds, is personal.

### The cholesterol question

The oils a metal mesh lets through carry cafestol and kahweol, compounds that raise LDL cholesterol. In their review, Rob Urgert and Martijn Katan found that paper filters hold them back, which is why paper-filtered coffee doesn't raise LDL cholesterol while boiled and plunger (French press) coffee do.

A 2025 Swedish study by Erik Orrje and colleagues measured how much ends up in the cup. Paper-filtered drip coffee brewed at home from five brands had a median of about 12 mg of cafestol per liter; their one French press brew measured about 87 mg per liter, about 7½ times as much (calculated). The authors note that earlier studies found French press coffee closer to boiled coffee, which had 939 mg per liter in their own sample.

If you drink several cups of unfiltered coffee a day and are watching your cholesterol, ask your clinician whether paper-filtered coffee is worth choosing.

## One 12 fl oz mug, brewed both ways

Here is the same 12 fl oz (355 ml) mug made each way with our Brew Guide's numbers for a medium roast. The two recipes come from different published methods, so the doses, grinds and times differ.

### V60 pour-over

The Brew Guide's V60 is our 1:16 adaptation of the continuous-pour method in Jessica Easto's *Craft Coffee*.

- **Coffee:** about 25 g, ground medium-fine, like table salt (about 450–650&nbsp;µm)
- **Water:** 406 g at 201°F&nbsp;(94°C). The Brew Guide starts light roasts at 205°F&nbsp;(96°C) and dark ones at 196°F&nbsp;(91°C).
- **Time:** pouring from 0:00 to 2:30, then draining; done around 3:00 to 4:00
- **Makes:** about 355 g, 12 US fl oz (355 ml). That's calculated: 406 g of water, less about 51 g held back by the grounds at roughly 2 g per gram of coffee.

1. Rinse a paper filter in the V60 and tip the rinse out. Add the coffee, shake it level and tare the scale.
2. Start the timer and pour about 65 g of water to wet every ground. Wait until 0:45.
3. Pour slowly in small circles at the center, without stopping, to 406 g by 2:30. Make one lap near the edge as you pass the one-third and two-thirds marks.
4. Let it drain. Much faster than 3:00, grind finer next time; much slower than 4:00, coarser.

Our [V60 guide](/blog/ultimate-pour-over-coffee-guide-and-best-v60-technique/) teaches a different recipe, Jonathan Gagné's two-pour method at 22 g to 352 g, which drains in about 2½ to 3½ minutes. Both use 1:16; pick one and repeat it. Flat-bottom drippers such as the Kalita Wave behave a little differently, as our [flat-bottom vs cone guide](/blog/flat-bottom-vs-cone-pour-over/) explains.

### French press

The Brew Guide's French press is Jessica Easto's eight-minute method, at the Brew Guide's 1:15 ratio.

- **Coffee:** about 27 g, ground extra coarse, like coarse sea salt (about 900–1,200&nbsp;µm)
- **Water:** 409 g at 205°F&nbsp;(96°C). The Brew Guide starts light roasts at 208°F&nbsp;(98°C) and dark ones at 199°F&nbsp;(93°C).
- **Time:** 8 minutes of steeping, then pour straight away
- **Makes:** about 355 g, 12 US fl oz (355 ml). That's calculated: 409 g of water, less about 55 g held back by the grounds at roughly 2 g per gram of coffee.

1. Warm the press, add the coffee and tare the scale.
2. Start the timer and pour all 409 g of water, so all the grounds are wet.
3. At 0:30 to 0:45, stir gently to sink the crust.
4. Put the lid on with the plunger up, and leave it.
5. At 8:00, press gently and pour all of the coffee out straight away.

That's one French press method of several. James Hoffmann's no-plunge method grinds medium, steeps 30 g in 500 g of water for 4 minutes, skims the crust and waits at least 5 minutes more before pouring gently with the plunger resting on top; his book uses more coffee, 75 g per liter. Fellow and Espro, which make presses, steep for about 4 minutes and then press. Our [French press guide](/blog/how-to-use-a-french-press-the-most-amazing-coffee-youve-ever-had/) sets all of them side by side, and our [AeroPress vs French press](/blog/aeropress-vs-french-press/) comparison covers the makers' methods.

## Grind

The two brewers want very different grinds. The Brew Guide's V60 is medium-fine, like table salt; its French press is extra coarse, like coarse sea salt, for Easto's long steep. Hoffmann's no-plunge method is the exception: he grinds a French press medium, and only goes coarser if the grinder makes a lot of fines.

In a pour-over, the grind also sets how fast the water drains, so the brew time tells you something. In a French press the time is yours to choose, and a grind that's too fine shows up as silt and a muddy cup. Either way, a burr grinder's more even grounds help; our guides to [electric grinders](/blog/top-5-best-electric-coffee-grinders/) and [manual grinders](/blog/top-10-best-manual-coffee-grinders/) compare them.

## Time, cleanup and caffeine

- **Time at the counter.** The V60 recipe above keeps you pouring for about 2½ minutes, and a gooseneck kettle makes the pour easier to control; our [kettle guide](/blog/best-gooseneck-kettles-pour-over/) compares them. The French press needs about a minute of attention and 8 minutes of waiting, or 9½ to 12½ for Hoffmann's method. Any kettle works for it.
- **Cleanup.** A paper filter lifts out with the grounds inside. A French press leaves wet grounds in the carafe and the mesh to rinse.
- **Batch size.** Hario rates the V60 02 for 1 to 4 cups and sells a larger 03 size for 1 to 6. A French press scales with its carafe: Fellow's Clara holds 24 oz, for example, and our Brew Guide's French press goes up to about a liter of water.
- **Caffeine.** It depends mostly on how much coffee you use. Our [Caffeine Calculator](/tools/caffeine/) puts a 12 fl oz mug made with the Brew Guide's doses at 171–313 mg from a V60 and 160–292 mg from a French press. The ranges overlap almost entirely. Our [caffeine guide](/blog/caffeine-in-coffee-guide/) explains them.

## If you can only own one

My recommendation: **start with a French press** if you're new to brewing. It's cheaper to start with, it needs no special kettle, and it makes a good cup without much practice. If you brew it daily for a few months and find yourself wanting more clarity and more control, you're ready for a pour-over.

The exception: if you already know you love light, bright, fruit-forward coffees, the kind that taste clearer through paper, go straight to a pour-over. And if you want the French press's body with less sediment, try Rao's tip: pour the finished press through a paper filter.

Whichever you choose, the same bag of beans will brew in both. Our guide to [coffee beans for a French press](/blog/best-coffee-beans-for-french-press/) covers which ones suit a press.
