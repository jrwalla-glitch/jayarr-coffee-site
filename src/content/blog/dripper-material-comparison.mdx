The coffee and water in a pour-over cone, the slurry, is always cooler than the water in your kettle. Some of the heat goes into the dripper itself, and how much depends on what the dripper is made of. This page sets out what Jonathan Gagné, an astrophysicist who studies brewing, measured in *The Physics of Filter Coffee*, under the conditions he used, and what you can do about it with the dripper you already own. For how the dripper's shape changes the brew, see our [flat-bottom vs cone guide](/blog/flat-bottom-vs-cone-pour-over/).

## What the material does

Gagné explains it with two properties. **Heat capacity** is how much heat a material must absorb to warm by one degree; heavier drippers have more of it, simply because there's more material to heat. **Thermal conductivity** is how fast heat moves through the material, into it from the water and out of it to the room.

When hot water lands in a cooler dripper, heat flows from the water into the walls until the two reach the same temperature. A heavy dripper has to soak up a lot more heat before that happens, so the water settles cooler. A conductive dripper then keeps passing heat on to the surrounding air for the rest of the brew. Put together:

- **Ceramic and glass** are heavy and conductive, so Gagné ranks them worst for taking heat out of the slurry.
- **Thin metal** is conductive but very light, so it takes little heat at the start of a brew, then keeps losing it.
- **Plastic** is light and insulating, and usually gives the highest slurry temperatures.
- **Vacuum-insulated walls** can insulate even better, which he calls a rare case.

He offers a quick check: a dripper that gets very hot to the touch during a brew is taking a lot of heat out of the slurry. He also found that a lid barely helps, because it has to come off every time you pour.

## What Gagné measured

He ran three tests, each under its own conditions.

**1. Empty drippers after preheating.** He preheated a glass V60, a ceramic V60, a plastic V60 and a Fellow Stagg X with water from a 99°C&nbsp;(210°F) kettle, poured at 8&nbsp;g a second for 15 seconds, then logged how each cooled. The heavy glass and ceramic held their warmth longest; the light plastic V60 and Stagg X cooled fastest. His point is how short-lived preheating can be, depending on the dripper's heat capacity.

**2. Water left in each dripper.** He put 100&nbsp;g of water from a 99°C&nbsp;(210°F) kettle in each of the four, once after preheating them and once at room temperature, 72.5°F&nbsp;(22.5°C), and logged the water's temperature for five minutes. The better-insulated, lighter drippers kept it hotter and much steadier, with the preheated Stagg X warmest. Even at room temperature, the plastic V60 and the Stagg X took less heat from the water than the preheated ceramic and glass ones.

**3. Real pour-overs.** He brewed with a 45-second bloom and two more pours from the kettle, and logged the slurry:

| Dripper and kettle | Slurry, average | Below the kettle |
|---|---|---|
| Plastic V60, kettle at 200°F&nbsp;(93°C) | 182°F&nbsp;(84°C) | about 16°F&nbsp;(9°C) |
| Fellow Stagg XF, kettle at 200°F&nbsp;(93°C) | 186°F&nbsp;(86°C) | about 13°F&nbsp;(7°C) |
| Plastic V60, kettle at 210°F&nbsp;(99°C) | 191°F&nbsp;(88°C) | about 20°F&nbsp;(11°C) |

The last column is calculated from his °C figures. At the same 200°F kettle, the vacuum-insulated Stagg XF averaged about 4°F&nbsp;(2°C) warmer than the plastic V60. Raising the V60's kettle to 210°F moved its average about 7°F&nbsp;(4°C). These are the individual temperature traces shown in his figure, not a replicated ranking of dripper materials. Gagné also notes that the Stagg XF brewed from the cooler kettle gave a steadier temperature through the brew than the V60 from the hotter one, even though its average was slightly lower.

## Material by material

| Material | Example he discusses | What he found |
|---|---|---|
| Plastic | Hario V60 | Light and insulating: hotter, steadier slurry than a ceramic or glass V60 |
| Vacuum-insulated steel | Fellow Stagg X and XF | The most thermally stable pour-over drippers he has encountered |
| Thin stainless steel | Kalita Wave 155 | Takes little heat at first, then loses it fast; the pleated filter's trapped air helps |
| Thin ceramic | Origami | Cooler than plastic as the brew goes on, but not as cool as a heavy ceramic V60 |
| Ceramic | Hario V60 | Lower, less stable slurry temperatures than the plastic V60 |
| Glass | Hario V60, Chemex | Not a good insulator: lower, less stable slurry temperatures |

A few details behind the table:

- **Vacuum-insulated steel.** Gagné writes that the Stagg drippers let him brew at a higher slurry temperature for a slightly higher average extraction, provided the coffee is roasted light enough to avoid bitterness, and that they keep the temperature steadier even for darker roasts.
- **Thin stainless steel.** Steel has a small heat capacity, so the Kalita Wave 155 cools the slurry only slightly at first, but it conducts heat away quickly. The Kalita's pleated filters trap layers of air between the folds, which slows that loss.
- **Thin ceramic.** The Origami is light, so it doesn't drop the temperature as much as a ceramic V60, but ceramic still conducts, so the slurry cools faster than in plastic. With a cone filter instead of a pleated one, there's less contact between filter and wall, so less heat is lost, though more water can bypass the coffee.

The same physics explains a familiar contrast: an AeroPress is very insulating and, Gagné writes, brews almost exactly at the kettle's temperature, while water poured into a V60 loses about 9°F&nbsp;(5°C) the moment it lands on the slurry.

## How much it matters in the cup

Gagné is careful not to call ceramic or glass bad. His two cautions are that the kettle's temperature doesn't tell you the temperature you're brewing at, and that the least-insulating materials swing more widely between pours. He prefers the taste of brews with a stable, hot temperature for all but the darker roasts.

Temperature changes extraction: in his V60 guide, Gagné finds that a slurry much below 196°F&nbsp;(91°C) can leave the cup under-extracted and sour. But a dripper's material is only one of the things that set the slurry temperature. In his pour-overs, a hotter kettle moved the V60 further than switching to the insulated Stagg did, and how you pour matters too: our [brewing temperature guide](/blog/whats-the-ideal-coffee-brewing-temperature/) covers why many small pours, with the bed draining between them, leave the slurry cooler.

## If you brew with ceramic or glass

- **Preheat with the rinse water and brew straight away.** It helps most at the start and fades within minutes, as Gagné's first test shows.
- **Use hotter water.** Gagné brews his V60 close to boiling, 210°F&nbsp;(99°C), except for darker roasts, which he treats separately. He avoids a full 212°F&nbsp;(100°C) because it can make the stream from a narrow gooseneck unstable.
- **Keep the bed covered between pours,** as Gagné does, so it doesn't cool while it drains.
- **Warm anything else the water touches.** Gagné notes that an extra tool such as a Melodrip also cools the slurry unless you keep it in a glass of hot water between pours.

Our Brew Guide gives kettle temperatures by roast, from 205°F&nbsp;(96°C) for light roasts to 196°F&nbsp;(91°C) for dark ones. With a ceramic or glass cone, the slurry will run cooler than with plastic at the same setting, so if a cup tastes sour, a hotter kettle is a reasonable experiment once the grind is right.
