More than 120 species of coffee have been identified, James Hoffmann writes, but only two are grown in any quantity: *Coffea arabica* and *Coffea canephora*. The second is the one we call robusta, a name Hoffmann describes as something of a brand, chosen to highlight its strengths. Arabica's reputation for quality is earned on average, but the two species overlap more than the "100% Arabica" label suggests, and they share a family tree.

## Two species, one family tree

Arabica is a hybrid. A 2024 genome study led by Jarkko Salojärvi describes it as an allotetraploid: a cross between *Coffea eugenioides* and *Coffea canephora*, robusta, that kept both parents' chromosomes, so it has 44 to their 22. The study dates that founding cross to 350,000–610,000 years ago, narrowing earlier estimates that ranged from 10,000 to a million years. Several population bottlenecks before people began growing it left the species with little genetic variation.

Hoffmann writes that the discovery that robusta is one of arabica's parents changed how the industry saw it, after years of treating it as an ugly sister. He places the original cross most likely somewhere in southern Sudan, after which the new species flourished in Ethiopia. World Coffee Research (WCR) describes arabica as native to Ethiopia, where the species' main genetic diversity is found.

Robusta's wild range runs from Guinea to Uganda to Angola, in the understory of humid evergreen forests, WCR writes. Its first documented cultivation began around 1870 in Congo, and the botanist Louis Pierre named the species in 1895. Seeds sent on to Java around the turn of the 20th century were taken up quickly there, because the plants were productive and seemed resistant to coffee leaf rust.

## How they grow

Robusta can grow where arabica can't. WCR writes that it tolerates hotter, more humid conditions, which are typically found at lower elevations, roughly 200 to 800 meters. It usually yields more, with more caffeine and less sugar in the beans; Jessica Easto writes that a robusta plant produces about twice as many pounds of beans as an arabica plant. Hoffmann adds that, because of the way it's grown, robusta is substantially cheaper to produce.

The two species also reproduce differently. Arabica trees can pollinate their own flowers, but a robusta clone can't, so robusta needs pollen from a genetically different plant. As a result, WCR writes, robusta plantations are never genetically uniform.

Arabica's genetic base, by contrast, is narrow. WCR's catalog reports that in Brazil, which grows about 40% of the world's coffee, 97.55% of coffee cultivars are derived from Typica and Bourbon, and it links Latin America's reliance on those two lines to a genetic bottleneck. Hoffmann describes the risk: a disease that can attack one plant can probably attack them all.

Robusta has helped with that. In the 1920s, an arabica and a robusta plant crossed naturally in East Timor, WCR writes, producing the Timor Hybrid, which resists coffee leaf rust. Breeders crossed Timor Hybrid lines with Caturra and Villa Sarchi to create the Catimor and Sarchimor families, which have traditionally had lower cup quality but have been vital where rust is a major threat. Robusta isn't immune itself: WCR notes that many robusta varieties are at least somewhat susceptible to key diseases and pests. Our history of [coffee leaf rust](/blog/coffee-leaf-rust-hemileia-vastatrix-history/) and our guide to [F1 hybrids](/blog/coffee-f1-hybrids-future-of-coffee/) go further.

## What's in the bean

Adriana Farah's review of coffee's chemistry compiles the composition of both species, green and after a medium roast. The figures are grams per 100 g of bean, and Farah notes that they vary with cultivar, farming, climate, soil and the method of analysis.

- **Caffeine.** 0.9–1.3% of green arabica and 1.5–2.5% of green robusta, and 1.1–1.3% and 2.4–2.5% after a medium roast. Hoffmann and Farah both put robusta at about twice arabica. Our Caffeine Calculator assumes 0.9–1.3% for roasted arabica and 1.7–2.5% for roasted robusta; Farah's medium-roast figures sit near the top of those ranges.
- **Chlorogenic acids.** 4.1–7.9% of green arabica and 6.1–11.3% of green robusta, falling to 1.9–2.5% and 3.3–3.8% after a medium roast. Farah and Carmen Donangelo's 2006 review gives wider ranges for green coffee, 4–8.4% and 7–14.4%. Roasting turns these acids into many of coffee's bitter compounds, as our [chlorogenic acids note](/deep-brew/research/chlorogenic-acid-cascades/) explains.
- **Oil.** Arabica holds about 14 g of lipids per 100 g of dry matter, Farah writes, about twice as much as robusta.
- **Sugar.** Sucrose is up to 9% of arabica's dry weight and about half that in robusta. Sugars feed the browning reactions in the roaster, and Farah counts arabica's extra sucrose among the reasons for its better aroma and flavor. Little of it survives as sugar, though: by the end of first crack, Hoffmann writes, few or no simple sugars are left. Our [sweetness note](/deep-brew/research/sweetness-paradox-roasting/) explains where roasted coffee's sweetness comes from instead.

In a cup, robusta's extra caffeine roughly doubles the dose. Our Caffeine Calculator estimates an 8 fl oz (237 ml) drip coffee of arabica at about 107–195 mg. It has no robusta setting, but with its own robusta figures the same cup works out at about 201–375 mg (calculated).

## How they taste

Hoffmann is blunt about robusta. He finds it woody, with a burnt-rubber quality, very little acidity and a heavy body. He grants that there are grades of quality within robusta and that it has long been a staple of Italian espresso, but most of the world's robusta, he writes, goes into instant coffee. WCR describes robusta as often lower in acidity and higher in bitterness, and more full-bodied, which it puts down to its pyrazine content. Farah adds that robusta often has a more aggressive flavor and, when lightly roasted, a flat, popcorn-like aroma.

Higher-quality robusta still goes into espresso blends, Easto writes, particularly Italian-style ones, where it's thought to add a bold flavor, extra caffeine and more crema.

Caffeine isn't the main source of robusta's bitterness. Thomas Hofmann estimated that caffeine accounts for about 15% of coffee's perceived bitterness, and Farah's review puts it at no more than 10%. Robusta's larger store of chlorogenic acids gives the roaster more raw material for bitter compounds: in Farah's 2005 roasting study, robusta's main bitter lactone peaked higher than arabica's, at 254 against 230 mg per 100 g. How much of robusta's bitterness that explains, the sources here don't say.

**Fine robusta.** Some robusta is grown for quality. Farah writes that robusta that's carefully harvested and processed may make a better cup than fermented, oxidized or otherwise low-quality arabica, and WCR notes that properly handled robusta can serve specialty markets. WCR's robusta catalog lists Brazil's BRS 2314, bred by EMBRAPA, as classified a "fine robusta": it scored 80 points or more in all the cuppings reported, reaching 87.2 in one sample, with an average of 80 on the Specialty Coffee Association's scale, and notes of chocolate, caramel and fruit. Hoffmann isn't persuaded by the wider argument. He accepts that a well-produced robusta may taste better than a poor arabica, but says that doesn't show robusta tastes good. ## How much of each is grown

Robusta's share of the world's coffee has grown from 25% to 40% since the early 1990s, WCR writes, and Hoffmann notes that it moves with prices and demand. USDA's latest figures put it higher:

| Crop year | Arabica | Robusta | Robusta share |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2024/25 | 102.3 | 74.5 | 42% |
| 2025/26 | 94.4 | 84.4 | 47% |
| 2026/27 (forecast) | 105.9 | 83.8 | 44% |

Figures are million 60 kg bags, from USDA's July 2026 *Coffee: World Markets and Trade*; the shares are calculated from them.

For 2026/27, USDA forecasts a record 189.7 million bags in all. Vietnam (31.4 million bags) and Brazil (24.4 million) grow most of the robusta, with Indonesia (10.0 million) third. Brazil's arabica rebound, up 9.5 million bags, accounts for most of that year's 11.4 million-bag rise in world arabica (calculated).

## Climate: robusta isn't a safe backup

Robusta has long been treated as the heat-tolerant species. The evidence now says otherwise. Kath and colleagues analyzed 10 years of yields from 798 farms across Southeast Asia and found robusta's optimum mean annual temperature was below 69°F&nbsp;(20.5°C), lower than the 72–86°F&nbsp;(22–30°C) previously assumed from its native range. During the growing season, each 1.8°F&nbsp;(1°C) rise above an average minimum of 61°F&nbsp;(16.2°C) and maximum of 75°F&nbsp;(24.1°C) went with a yield drop of about 14%. WCR now writes that robusta's ability to thrive in hotter climates "may have been previously overstated."

Both species are exposed. Bunn and colleagues' 2015 models project that climate change will reduce the global area suitable for coffee by about 50% across emission scenarios. In their projections, higher temperatures may reduce arabica yields, while robusta could suffer from more variable temperatures within the growing season. Our research note on [coffee under climate pressure](/deep-brew/research/coffee-climate-change-data/) goes region by region.

## What "100% Arabica" tells you

It tells you the species, and that's all. Arabica runs from commodity coffee to the most prized lots in the world, and robusta from instant-coffee filler to the fine robustas above. What predicts the cup is the variety (see our [varietal guide](/blog/coffee-varietal-guide/)), how the coffee was grown and [processed](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/), the [roast](/blog/coffee-roast-levels-explained/), and how recently it was roasted (see [coffee freshness](/blog/coffee-freshness-roast-to-stale/)). If caffeine is what you're after, the species matters most: a robusta or a blend with robusta in it will usually carry more.
