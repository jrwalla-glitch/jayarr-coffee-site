The names are Italian: *ristretto* means "restricted," *lungo* "long," and *normale* is the standard shot in between. Scott Rao points out that outside Italy they have come to mean very different things to different baristas, so they're best read as loose labels for a ratio, the weight of the drink compared with the weight of the coffee.

Our 2020 video covers the same ground. This page was revised in 2026, so where the two differ, follow the numbers in the text.

## The three shots by weight

One published set of ratios comes from Andy Schecter, an amateur coffee scientist whose chart Rao reproduces in *The Professional Barista's Handbook*. Rao says the chart reflects common practice in Italy. Schecter's typical ristretto weighs the same as the dry coffee used to make it, a typical normale twice as much and a typical lungo three times as much.

| Shot | Typical drink weight; from an 18&nbsp;g dose (calculated) | Schecter's range (calculated from his chart) |
|---|---|---|
| Ristretto | 1 × the dose; about 18&nbsp;g | about 0.7 to 1.7 × the dose |
| Normale | 2 × the dose; about 36&nbsp;g | about 1.7 to 2.5 × the dose |
| Lungo | 3 × the dose; about 54&nbsp;g | about 2.5 to 3.7 × the dose |

Schecter writes his ranges the other way up, as the dose divided by the drink: 60–140% for a ristretto, 40–60% for a normale and 27–40% for a lungo. The third column turns those into multiples of the dose.

Rao's later book, *Espresso Extraction* (2013), draws the lines a little differently. His normale weighs 1.5 to 2 times the dose; anything shorter he calls a ristretto, anything longer a lungo. Our Brew Guide starts at 18&nbsp;g to 36&nbsp;g (1:2) and offers a Shorter setting at 1:1.5 (27&nbsp;g) and a Longer one at 1:2.5 (45&nbsp;g). On Rao's 2013 scale the Shorter shot is a short normale; on Schecter's chart it's a ristretto. Neither is wrong, which is why it pays to give the weights rather than the names.

Some recipes give volumes instead, which crema makes harder to compare (see below). Breville's ristretto is 15–20&nbsp;ml, and CoffeeGeek's Mark Prince describes the traditional Italian lungo as 45–50&nbsp;ml per single, against about 30&nbsp;ml for a standard single.

## Why weigh the shot instead of measuring it

Crema makes volume unreliable. In *Espresso Extraction* Rao gives an example: a 30&nbsp;g shot from beans roasted the day before fills almost 60&nbsp;ml, while the same weight from beans roasted 15 days earlier fills only a little over 30&nbsp;ml. A barista who stops shots by eye will pull fresh coffee shorter than intended. A scale under the cup tells you which of the three shots you actually made.

## What a shorter or longer shot does

Two things change as more water runs through the same coffee, and they move in opposite directions. Rao explains that extraction, the share of the coffee that dissolves, goes up, while strength, how concentrated the drink is, goes down, because the extra water also dilutes it.

So a ristretto is the strongest of the three and usually the least extracted. Rao finds it may be impossible to reach his 19–20% target with a very restricted ristretto, and suggests aiming for about 15–16% instead. A lungo, by contrast, reaches 19–20% easily. For strength, Rao reports that most professionals seem to prefer espresso at 10–13% dissolved solids (TDS). Our [Extraction Map](/deep-brew/extraction/) uses 18–22% for extraction and treats 8–12% TDS as a common rule of thumb, a little lower than Rao's range.

The Brew Guide shows an illustrative strength for each ratio. It assumes 18–22% extraction for any espresso, so it shows about 9–11% TDS at 1:2, 12–15% at 1:1.5 and 7–9% at 1:2.5 (calculated: TDS = extraction × dose ÷ drink weight). Real shots don't keep the same extraction as the ratio changes, so a refractometer gives the true number; our [strength vs extraction guide](/blog/coffee-strength-vs-extraction/) explains the difference.

What you'll taste is a hypothesis, not a rule. Rao writes that shots with more coffee for their size come out brighter and more acidic, often sour or sharp, while longer shots tend to be mellower, with more bittersweet and caramel tones. James Hoffmann is blunter about the lungo: most coffee brewed that way tastes very bitter and ashy, he finds, though lighter roasts can make a complex, balanced long shot. He also suggests a longer shot when an espresso blend is too acidic.

Rao adjusts temperature too: the lower end of his 195–203°F&nbsp;(91–95°C) range for longer shots and the higher end for shorter ones. The Brew Guide sets one temperature by roast for every ratio.

## Do you change the grind?

Here the sources differ:

- **Hoffmann** grinds finer for a ristretto, so the brew still lasts long enough to extract the coffee's desirable aromatics, and coarser for a lungo, so the extra water flows faster and the shot doesn't over-extract.
- **Breville's ristretto** cuts the shot short, at about 15 to 20 seconds.
- **Mark Prince's lungo** keeps the dose, goes a touch coarser and aims for a flow of about 3&nbsp;g of espresso a second.
- **Our Brew Guide** keeps the same 25- to 30-second target at every ratio, so reaching 27&nbsp;g or 45&nbsp;g in that time means grinding finer or coarser.

If a shot at a new ratio tastes sour or bitter, change the grind next, one step at a time.

## Lungo vs americano

They're different drinks, made differently.

- **A lungo** runs extra water *through* the coffee. Hoffmann describes it as two or three times the water of an espresso through the same weight of coffee; Prince's traditional Italian lungo is 45–50&nbsp;ml per single.
- **An americano** is brewed espresso diluted with separately added hot water, which never passes through the grounds. Lavazza's is a single espresso with about 120–180&nbsp;ml of hot water. Hoffmann puts the hot water in the cup first and brews a double espresso on top.

Because the water in an americano never touches the grounds, it only dilutes the shot: the extraction stays where the espresso left it. In a lungo the extra water keeps dissolving coffee, so extraction rises along with the dilution, as Rao describes. Hoffmann also scoops the crema off an americano, because the tiny pieces of ground coffee trapped in it add bitterness.

## Taste the ratio for yourself

This short exercise shows what the ratio alone does. It isn't how you'd dial in a ristretto to serve, which, by Hoffmann's method, needs a finer grind.

1. Dial in a shot of 18&nbsp;g to 36&nbsp;g with a coffee you know.
2. Without changing the dose or the grind, pull three shots: stop one at 27&nbsp;g, one at 36&nbsp;g and one at 45&nbsp;g, the Brew Guide's three settings. The short shot will also finish sooner.
3. Taste them side by side while they're warm, and note which you'd want to drink.

You may find what Rao describes, the short shot brighter and sharper and the longer ones mellower and more bittersweet, or your coffee may surprise you. When you've found the ratio you like, change the grind until it runs in your usual time, and our [dial-in guide](/blog/how-to-dial-in-espresso/) takes it from there.
