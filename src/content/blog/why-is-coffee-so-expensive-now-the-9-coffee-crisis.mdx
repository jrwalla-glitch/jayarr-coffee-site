Every figure on this page has a date, because coffee prices move every day. We checked them all on October 15, 2026.

## What a pound of coffee costs now

The US Bureau of Labor Statistics tracks the average price of a pound of ground coffee in US cities. Its averages, checked October 15, 2026: $6.47 in September 2024, $9.14 in September 2025 (41 percent more, calculated) and $9.33 in September 2026. It reached $9.72 in April 2026 and has eased only slightly since.

Green coffee, the unroasted beans roasters buy, tells a different story. The ICO Composite Indicator Price, a benchmark that blends arabica and robusta prices, averaged 354.32 US cents a pound in February 2025. The International Coffee Organization says that was the highest monthly average on record, above the 305.13 cents of March 1977 (both in nominal terms). By September 2026 it had fallen to 262.18 cents, 26 percent below that peak and 19 percent below September 2025 (calculated).

So the coffee in the bag has gotten cheaper over the past year, but the bag on the shelf hasn't, at least not yet.

## The supply shock: Brazil's bad years

Brazil grows nearly 40 percent of the world's coffee, according to USDA, and its arabica crop sets the tone for world prices. USDA's July 2026 report describes five years of underperformance there:

- In 2021/22, drought and high temperatures cut the off-year crop by 13.3 million 60 kg bags.
- The next year's rebound was small, held back by frost, heat and low rainfall.
- After a better 2023/24, drought and heat lowered yields two years in a row. Brazil's arabica output fell from 44.9 million bags in 2023/24 to 38.0 million in 2025/26, a drop of 6.9 million bags or about 15 percent (calculated).

Other origins grew more, but not enough to fill the gap. Global ending stocks fell by 15.5 million bags between 2020/21 and 2024/25, USDA reports.

Vietnam, the largest robusta producer, had its own dip. USDA's figures show its production falling from 31.6 million bags in 2021/22 to 27.6 million in 2023/24, and its bean exports from 26.0 million bags in 2022/23 to 22.0 million in 2023/24 (about 15 percent, calculated).

Supply can't bounce back quickly. Coffee trees take four or five years to mature, Mark Pendergrast notes in *Uncommon Grounds*, so a lost crop or a dead tree shapes prices for years.

## Tariffs: what changed, and when

US tariffs hit coffee hard in 2025, then were lifted. Each step below is from the Federal Register or the Supreme Court:

- **April 5, 2025:** a 10 percent "reciprocal" tariff took effect on imports from all trading partners, coffee included.
- **August 6, 2025:** an extra 40 percent tariff on goods from Brazil took effect. Coffee wasn't on the exemption list, so Brazilian coffee faced 50 percent in all (calculated).
- **November 13, 2025:** two executive orders took coffee off both lists. One exempted it from the reciprocal tariffs, and the other from Brazil's extra 40 percent.
- **February 20, 2026:** the Supreme Court ruled in *Learning Resources v. Trump* that the emergency-powers law used for those tariffs doesn't authorize tariffs. The same day, an executive order ended them, and a proclamation imposed a temporary 10 percent surcharge from February 24 to July 24, 2026. Coffee was on that surcharge's exemption list.
- **July 2026:** the Trade Representative imposed new Section 301 tariffs: 25 percent on goods from Brazil from July 22, and 10 or 12.5 percent on goods from 60 economies from July 24. Both notices exempt coffee, and unflavored instant coffee was added to both exemption lists.

So, as of the July 2026 notices, coffee itself is exempt from the new US tariffs. USDA's July 2026 report also cut its estimate of Brazil's 2025/26 bean exports by 3.0 million bags, citing lower shipments to the EU and the United States, and of US bean imports by 700,000 bags, citing lower consumption. It doesn't say how much of either was due to the tariffs.

Tariffs bite on coffee because the US grows so little of its own. It does grow some commercially: Hawaii's 2025/26 crop came to 3.9 million pounds of green coffee, according to USDA's National Agricultural Statistics Service, or about 30,000 60 kg bags (calculated). USDA estimates US coffee imports in 2025/26 at 25.5 million bags, so Hawaii's crop is about 0.1 percent of that (calculated).

Tariff rules have changed several times in a year, so check the current rules before relying on these.

## Demand keeps growing in Asia

Demand has grown fastest outside the traditional markets. USDA's figures put China's coffee consumption at 4.9 million bags in 2021/22 and forecast 6.75 million for 2026/27, about 37 percent more (calculated). India's rises from about 0.9 million to 1.6 million bags over the same years. The ICO estimates that consumption across Asia and Oceania grew from 42.6 million bags in 2020/21 to 48.5 million in 2025/26, about 14 percent (calculated).

High prices do bite, though. The ICO estimates that world consumption fell 0.8 percent in 2025/26, including a drop in the United States, and its figures for North America show 28.5 million bags in 2025/26, down from 30.2 million the year before.

## This has happened before

The closest parallel is Brazil's "Black Frost." Pendergrast describes it: on July 17 and 18, 1975, a frost killed an estimated 1.5 billion coffee trees, well over half of Brazil's. New plants needed four years to bear, so the market stayed tight. Within two weeks, the retail price of ground coffee had risen 20 cents a pound, and by March 1976 green coffee had doubled in less than a year, to a dollar a pound.

Roasters responded then much as some do now. In the late 1970s, Pendergrast writes, Procter & Gamble sold Folgers Flaked Coffee in full-size cans holding only 13 ounces, claiming it made as many cups as a pound, and Hills Brothers packed 13 ounces of a puffed "High Yield" roast into pound-size cans. Shrinking the package is an old move: check the weight, not just the can.

Pendergrast also notes that those high prices helped specialty coffee. As commodity coffee rose, the percentage gap between ordinary and good coffee narrowed, and more people found that a little more money bought much better coffee.

## The outlook, as of October 2026

The supply picture has turned:

- **A record world crop.** USDA's July 2026 report forecasts world production of 189.7 million bags for 2026/27, a record, with Brazil at a record 71.9 million. On September 24, 2026, Brazil's own crop agency, Conab, raised its 2026 estimate to a record 67.6 million bags, the ICO reports. (The two count different years and use different methods.)
- **A surplus.** The ICO estimates a 3.0 million-bag surplus for 2025/26, after four years of deficits, and USDA expects world stocks to rise for a second year, though still below the long-term average.
- **Forecast prices.** In April 2026, the World Bank forecast arabica prices about 14 percent lower in 2026 than in 2025, and about 3 percent lower again in 2027.
- **Risks.** The ICO points to very low certified arabica stocks on the New York exchange, and to a NOAA outlook, published September 10, 2026, giving a greater than 90 percent chance of a very strong El Niño.

None of this guarantees cheaper coffee on the shelf, or says when. As of the September 2026 figures, falling green prices hadn't yet shown up in the BLS average. USDA's next coffee update is due December 16, 2026.

## What you can actually do

- **Know your cost per cup.** At the BLS average of $9.33 a pound for September 2026 (checked October 15, 2026), a 12 oz pour-over at the Brew Guide's 1:16 ratio, about 25 g of coffee, costs about 52 cents in coffee, calculated. At September 2024's average it was about 36 cents.
- **Weigh your coffee.** A scale keeps you from using more than the recipe needs, and lets you repeat a cup you like.
- **Check the weight on the bag.** As in 1977, a smaller package at the same price is a price rise.
- **Stock up only if you'll freeze it.** Roasted coffee is usually at its best about 7 to 21 days after roasting, a typical window rather than a promise for your bag. Our Freshness tool treats freezer time as paused for up to about a year, and our [guide to freezing coffee beans](/blog/how-to-freeze-coffee-beans/) covers how.
- **Try something better.** If the price of ordinary coffee has risen to meet specialty, as Pendergrast saw in the 1970s, our guide to [single-origin coffee](/blog/what-is-single-origin-coffee/) is a place to start.
