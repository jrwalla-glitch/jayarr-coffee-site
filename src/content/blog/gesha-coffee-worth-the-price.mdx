Gesha is the variety behind the Best of Panama's record auction prices. This guide covers where the variety came from, what its premium actually buys, and what to check before you pay it. The auction story in full is in our [Panama guide](/blog/panama-coffee-guide-geisha-boquete/).

## Gesha or Geisha?

Both spellings are in use, and neither is wrong. World Coffee Research (WCR), whose catalog lists the variety as "Geisha (Panama)," explains that there's no set way to transliterate the name from the languages of Ethiopia into English. The germplasm records first wrote it "Geisha," and researchers and gene banks kept that spelling for decades. But the coffee was collected near a mountain whose name is usually rendered "Gesha," so many in the industry prefer that spelling. Hoffmann's *World Atlas of Coffee* notes the debate too, and says "Geisha" is the more common. Either way, the name comes from the place, not from the Japanese word.

The name also covers more than one plant. WCR writes that several genetically distinct coffees, many from the same part of Ethiopia, have been called Geisha. Its genetic analyses show that the Panamanian Geisha descended from one accession, T2722, is distinct and uniform; other coffees sold under the name may not share those genetics.

## From an Ethiopian forest to Panama

WCR's catalog gives the outline:

- The variety was collected from Ethiopia's coffee forests in the 1930s and sent to the Lyamungu research station in Tanzania.
- In 1953 it went to the CATIE research center in Costa Rica, where it was logged as accession T2722.
- CATIE distributed it through Panama in the 1960s for its tolerance of coffee leaf rust, but its brittle branches made it unpopular with farmers, so it wasn't widely planted.

Jeff Koehler fills in the early years in *Where the Wild Coffee Grows*, drawing on colonial records:

- **1931.** A British botanist in Kenya noted seeds imported from the Geisha forest "in about 1931," planted at the Kitale coffee nursery.
- **1936.** Seed went on to two research stations, Kawanda in Uganda and Lyamungu in Tanzania. Early that year the British consul Captain Richard Whalley also collected seed in the Geisha forest himself, for the Kenyan director of agriculture. Hacienda La Esmeralda's own history credits Whalley's 1936 collection.
- **1953 and 1963.** Seed from Lyamungu reached Costa Rica in 1953, and a Ministry of Agriculture employee, Francisco Serracin, brought Geisha from Costa Rica to his farm in Boquete in 1963.

For decades the cup won few admirers. Koehler quotes a 1969 inventory of East African coffee selections that called Geisha low-yielding, often with long, thin beans and "generally a liquor of poor quality," and noted that it had been used in breeding because it resisted leaf rust.

## The 2004 Best of Panama

That changed at the Best of Panama, the national competition and auction run by the Specialty Coffee Association of Panama (SCAP). For the 2004 competition, Hacienda La Esmeralda's own history says, the Peterson family separated coffee from different parts of their farm into individual lots for the first time; one lot from high on its Jaramillo plot was all Geisha, and it won. Hoffmann writes that the coffee proved so unusual and distinct that it drew a record bid of 21 dollars a pound, beaten again in 2006 and 2007 at 130 dollars a pound, nearly a hundred times the price of commodity coffee. WCR's catalog gives the breakthrough year as 2005; Hoffmann and the farm both say 2004.

Hoffmann adds that the success encouraged many producers in Central and South America to plant the variety, and WCR notes that it's also grown widely in Malawi.

## What the price buys

### Scarcity on the farm

WCR's ratings show why there isn't much Gesha. It gives less coffee than common Central American varieties and makes farmers wait longer for it:

- **Yield:** "Low," against "Medium" for Caturra and "Very High" for the F1 hybrid Centroamericano.
- **First harvest:** in year 4, against year 2 for Centroamericano.
- **The tree:** tall, with the brittle branches that put farmers off in the 1960s.
- **Leaf rust:** intermediate resistance.

Growing it in Panama adds cost. Hoffmann writes that land prices and Panama's more protective labor laws make the country an expensive place to grow coffee; our [Panama guide](/blog/panama-coffee-guide-geisha-boquete/) explains.

### Competition scores

At the Best of Panama the scores are high and close together. SCAP's results:

- **2025:** a washed Geisha from Hacienda La Esmeralda scored 98.00 points and a natural Geisha from the same farm 97, scores SCAP called world records.
- **2026:** the washed Geisha category was won with 96.25 points by Lamastus Family Estate's Elida Estate, a quarter of a point ahead of the runner-up; Finca Lérida's natural Geisha won its category with 95.63. Every Geisha on the final table scored above 93. The top non-Geisha, in the varieties category, scored 93.63.

### Auction records

The lots are tiny, 15 to 25 kg each in recent years, and the bidders come from around the world. Here are the top lots of the last four auctions, as SCAP reported them:

| Year | Top lot | US dollars per kg |
|---|---|---|
| 2023 | Carmen Estates, washed Geisha; 25 kg | 10,005 |
| 2024 | Lamastus Family Estate, Elida Geisha Natural Torre; 20 kg | 10,013 |
| 2025 | Hacienda La Esmeralda, washed Geisha (98 points); 20 kg | 30,204 |
| 2026 | Lamastus Family Estate, Elida Geisha Torre (96.25 points); 15 kg | 18,004 |

Per pound, that's about 4,540, 4,540, 13,700 and 8,170 dollars (calculated).

The 2026 auction sold all 50 lots for 3,041,940 dollars. SCAP reports that every one of the 40 Geisha lots topped 1,500 dollars a kilogram, while the 10 lots of other varieties sold for 897 to 4,216 dollars, and Esmeralda's Rachel Peterson put the average at 4,055.92 dollars a kilogram. The top price fell from 2025 to 2026, but these are the very top lots of one competition, not what a retail bag costs.

Hoffmann credits Esmeralda's prices mainly to the coffee's quality, while granting that high demand and great marketing played a part.

### What it doesn't buy

- **A guaranteed cup.** The roaster Rob Hoos, in his book *Cultivar*, writes that a coffee listed as Gesha "does not automatically guarantee" the same intensity of flavor or quality; some taste subdued.
- **Certain genetics.** WCR warns that the name is often given to coffees that don't share Panamanian Geisha's distinct genetics.
- **An auction lot.** A bag on a café shelf is almost never one of the lots in the table above.

## What Gesha tastes like

WCR describes Panamanian Geisha's aromas as delicate, floral, jasmine-like and peach-like. Hoffmann says the variety is considered to produce exceptionally aromatic, floral cups, and describes Esmeralda's record-breaking coffee as extremely floral and citrusy, with a light, tea-like body. Hoos's expected profile is crisp florals, especially jasmine, candied citrus and sweetness.

These are what a Gesha *can* taste like, not a promise for every bag. Process changes it too: our Panama guide explains why a washed Geisha tends toward the classic floral cup and a natural toward more fruit.

## Is it worth it to you?

That's a judgment only you can make, and it depends on what you like and what you'd otherwise buy. A few questions make it easier:

- **Which farm and lot?** A named estate and lot tells you far more than "Gesha" alone. Hoffmann writes that Panamanian coffees are often traceable to a single estate.
- **Which Gesha?** A roaster who can say where the trees came from, or that the lot is Panamanian Geisha, has answered WCR's warning about the name.
- **Which process?** Washed for the classic floral cup, natural for more fruit, as our [processing guide](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/) explains.
- **How fresh?** Delicate aromatics are the point, so check the roast date; our [Freshness tool](/tools/freshness/) shows where a bag sits, from very fresh to stale.
- **Can you compare?** Tasting a Gesha beside another coffee from the same farm can show you what the variety adds for you.

For how Gesha relates to other varieties, see our [coffee varietal guide](/blog/coffee-varietal-guide/).

## Brewing it

A light, floral coffee suits a paper-filter pour-over; Hoffmann notes that paper filters give the cleanest cup. For a light roast in a V60, our Brew Guide starts at 1:16 with the kettle at 205°F&nbsp;(96°C). If the cup tastes sour, grind a step finer; if it's bitter or drying, a step coarser. Our [V60 guide](/blog/ultimate-pour-over-coffee-guide-and-best-v60-technique/) covers the technique, and our [water guide](/blog/why-water-matters-more-than-your-coffee-beans/) covers the water.
