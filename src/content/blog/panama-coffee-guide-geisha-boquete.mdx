The first time I tasted a Panamanian Geisha, I understood why people pay so much for it. It wasn't just good: it was a different category of experience altogether. Jasmine, bergamot, ripe mango, and a sweetness so transparent it felt like drinking light.

Panama grows a tiny share of the world's coffee, yet one variety and one auction have made it the reference point for what top coffee can cost. This guide covers how Geisha got there, what the auction pays, where Panama's coffee grows and how it tastes. For whether a Geisha is worth buying, see our [Gesha guide](/blog/gesha-coffee-worth-the-price/).

## How much coffee Panama grows

Not much. USDA forecasts 100,000 60 kg bags for 2026/27, up from 80,000 in 2025/26. Against its forecast world total of 189.7 million bags, that's about 0.05 percent (calculated).

Hoffmann writes in *The World Atlas of Coffee* that Panama's output was once about a tenth of neighboring Costa Rica's, and that its coffee didn't have a strong reputation. Two things make it expensive to grow, he and the importer Café Imports agree:

- **Land.** Demand for homes from North Americans and others has turned many former coffee farms into real estate.
- **Labor.** Panama's labor laws are more protective, so pickers tend to earn more, a cost passed on to buyers.

Café Imports adds that much of the coffee comes from smallholders of the Ngäbe and Buglé Indigenous peoples, alongside private estates often owned by European or North American immigrants or their descendants.

## Where coffee grows

The three regions buyers know, Boquete, Volcán–Candela and Renacimiento, all lie in Chiriquí province in the western highlands, around the Barú volcano and along the Costa Rican border. Hoffmann notes that the regions were defined more by how coffee was sold than by geography, and that they're small and close together.

| Region | Altitude; harvest | Varieties |
|---|---|---|
| Boquete | 400–1,900 m (1,300–6,200 ft); Dec–Mar | Typica, Caturra, Catuaí, Bourbon, Geisha, San Ramón |
| Volcán–Candela | 1,200–1,600 m (3,900–5,200 ft); Dec–Mar | The same |
| Renacimiento | 1,100–1,500 m (3,600–4,900 ft); Dec–Mar | The same |

The figures are Hoffmann's. Café Imports gives a 1,000–1,650 m range across the regions, a November–March harvest and typical US arrivals from April to July.

- **Boquete** is the best known. Its mountains create many microclimates, and Hoffmann writes that the cool weather and frequent mists slow the cherries' ripening, which some argue mimics the effect of higher altitude.
- **Volcán–Candela,** named for the volcano and the town of Piedra Candela, borders Costa Rica and grows much of Panama's food as well as coffee. Café Imports notes its volcanic soil.
- **Renacimiento** is a small district on the Costa Rican border, so it isn't a primary source of specialty coffee, Hoffmann writes.

## How Geisha reached Panama

The variety began in Ethiopia. World Coffee Research (WCR) traces it this way:

- It was collected from Ethiopia's coffee forests in the 1930s and sent to the Lyamungu research station in Tanzania.
- In 1953 it went to the CATIE research center in Costa Rica, logged as accession T2722.
- CATIE distributed it through Panama in the 1960s for its tolerance of coffee leaf rust, but its brittle branches made it unpopular with farmers, so it wasn't widely planted.

Hacienda La Esmeralda's own history adds the Panamanian side:

- **The collection.** It dates the gathering to 1936, by a British consul, Captain Richard Whalley, near Geisha Mountain.
- **Why the family got it.** It says the family acquired the seeds from CATIE for their resistance to *ojo de gallo*, a leaf disease.
- **The Jaramillo farm.** In 1997 the Petersons bought a high-altitude plot on the slopes of the Barú volcano, Jaramillo. It had just been hit by leaf rust, and Daniel Peterson noticed the Geisha trees had suffered less. So the family planted Geisha on more of the farm, including above 1,650 m, higher than it had been grown before.

WCR's genetic analyses show that Panamanian Geisha descended from T2722 is distinct and uniform. The name is now applied to other coffees that don't share those genetics. WCR rates its cup quality potential at high altitude "Exceptional," the top of its scale, with a low yield and intermediate resistance to leaf rust. The spelling varies, Geisha or Gesha; our [coffee varietal guide](/blog/coffee-varietal-guide/) explains why and shows how it relates to other varieties.

## Hacienda La Esmeralda and the 2004 Best of Panama

Hacienda La Esmeralda dates the estate to 1940. The Swedish-American banker Rudolph A. Peterson bought it in 1967, when it was mostly cattle pasture, and the family turned to coffee in the mid-1980s.

The turning point came at the Best of Panama, the national competition and online auction run by the Specialty Coffee Association of Panama (SCAP). For the 2004 competition, the farm writes, the Petersons separated coffee from different parts of the farm into individual lots for the first time. One lot from high on Jaramillo was 100 percent Geisha, and it won.

Hoffmann tracks the prices that followed:

- **2004:** the winning lot sold for 21 US dollars a pound, a record (about 46 dollars a kilogram, calculated).
- **2006 and 2007:** the record was beaten again, reaching 130 dollars a pound, nearly a hundred times the price of commodity-grade coffee.
- **2010:** it had crept up to 170 dollars a pound.
- **2013:** a small lot of Esmeralda's natural-processed coffee sold for 350.25 dollars a pound.

He lists Esmeralda's wins as 2004 through 2007, 2009, 2010, and a category in 2013. WCR's catalog gives the breakthrough year as 2005; Hoffmann and the farm both say 2004.

Hoffmann credits the prices mainly to the coffee's quality, though he grants that high demand and great marketing played a role. He describes that record-breaking coffee as extremely floral and citrusy, but light and tea-like in body.

## What the auction pays now

The auction lots are tiny, 15 kg each in 2026, and the bidders come from around the world. So these prices tell you what the very top lots fetch, not what a bag of Panamanian coffee costs.

| Year | Top lot | Price per kg; per lb (calculated) |
|---|---|---|
| 2025 | Hacienda La Esmeralda, washed Geisha | 30,204 US dollars; about 13,700 dollars |
| 2026 | Lamastus Family Estates, Elida Geisha Torre | 18,004 US dollars; about 8,170 dollars |

**2025.** *La Prensa* (Panama City) reported on August 7, 2025:

- the washed Geisha set a new record and was bought by Julith Coffee of Dubai;
- Esmeralda's natural Geisha sold for 23,608 dollars a kilogram, and its Laurina, a different variety, for 8,040 dollars;
- 30 of the 50 lots topped 1,000 dollars a kilogram;
- the average was 2,861.20 dollars, against 1,383.72 the year before.

**2026.** *La Estrella de Panamá* reported on September 24, 2026:

- 50 lots of 15 kg, 40 of them Geisha and 10 other varieties, drew 83 bidders from 21 countries;
- the auction totaled 3,041,940 dollars, an average of about 4,056 dollars a kilogram (calculated, and the figure Esmeralda's Rachel Peterson cited);
- the top lot went to JD.com of China. Lérida Estate's Waterfall Geisha Natural came second at 13,505 dollars a kilogram, and Esmeralda's washed and natural Geishas sold for 10,014 and 10,024 dollars;
- every Geisha lot topped 1,500 dollars a kilogram, and the other varieties sold for 897 to 4,216 dollars.

The 2026 top price was well below 2025's.

## Beyond Geisha

Panama grows more than Geisha. Hoffmann's tables list Typica, Caturra, Catuaí, Bourbon and San Ramón alongside it, and Café Imports adds Mundo Novo. Hoffmann writes that Esmeralda's success led many farms in Panama and across Central America to plant Geisha, and that Geisha lots do usually sell for more than other varieties.

The rest of Panama's coffee is a different style. Café Imports describes Panamanian coffees as mild, with an approachable sweet nuttiness, a foil to the livelier acidity of other Central American coffees. That's a fair expectation for a Caturra or Catuaí from Boquete; Geisha's florals are particular to the variety.

## Processing

Café Imports lists washed and natural processing for Panama, and the Best of Panama sells washed and natural Geishas as separate categories. Hoffmann describes the general tendencies:

- **Washed:** more acidity and complexity, and a cleaner cup.
- **Natural:** fruit flavors, sometimes wild or fermented ones.

That's why a washed Geisha is the classic floral, tea-like cup and a natural Geisha tends to be fruitier. These are tendencies across many lots, not rules for one bag. Our [processing guide](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/) explains each method.

## What Panamanian coffee tastes like

Hoffmann's summary for the country: the better coffees tend to be citrusy and floral, light-bodied, delicate and complex. WCR describes Panamanian Geisha's aromas as delicate, floral, jasmine-like and peach-like.

These are notes a Panamanian coffee *can* show; no single bag has them all:

- **Washed Geisha:** jasmine and other florals, bergamot and citrus, stone fruit, a light, tea-like body.
- **Natural Geisha:** the same florals with more fruit, sometimes wilder.
- **Caturra, Catuaí and other varieties:** mild and sweet, often nutty.

## What to look for when buying

- **The farm and the lot.** Hoffmann writes that you should expect high traceability from Panama: coffees are often traceable to a single estate, and estates often sell separate lots by process or variety.
- **Which Geisha.** WCR warns that the name is often applied to coffees that don't share Panamanian Geisha's genetics, so a roaster who names the farm, and where its trees came from, tells you more.
- **The process.** Washed for the classic floral cup; natural for more fruit.
- **The roast date.** Our [Freshness tool](/tools/freshness/) shows where a bag is, from resting to stale.

## How to brew it

A delicate, light-bodied coffee suits a paper-filter pour-over: Hoffmann notes that paper filters give the cleanest cup. For a light roast in a V60, our Brew Guide starts at 1:16 with the kettle at 205°F&nbsp;(96°C). If the cup tastes sour, grind a step finer; if it's bitter or drying, a step coarser.
