There are two things people mean by Cuban coffee. One is a way of making and drinking coffee, sweet, strong and small, at home in Cuba and in Cuban American communities such as Miami's; you can make it with any dark, finely ground coffee and a moka pot. The other is coffee grown in Cuba's mountains, which is rare outside the island. James Hoffmann notes in *The World Atlas of Coffee* that "Cuban coffee" advertised in the United States often means the drink, made with coffees, often from Brazil, chosen to taste the part. This page covers both, starting with the cup.

## How to make a cafecito

This follows Café Bustelo's published cafecito recipe for four cups, with Bialetti's instructions for filling a moka pot and James Hoffmann's cue for taking it off the heat. Bustelo's times are 8 minutes to prepare and 7 to cook, about 15 minutes in all, of which 1 to 2 minutes is beating the sugar.

**You need:** a moka pot that takes about 1¼ cups of water (Bustelo's recipe), a small metal pitcher or 2-cup measuring cup, a small spoon and four espresso cups.

**Ingredients**

- About 296 g (1¼ US cups) water
- ⅓ cup espresso-style ground coffee, such as Café Bustelo Espresso, or enough to fill the moka pot's basket level
- 50–67 g (¼ to ⅓ cup) sugar

The grams are calculated: 236.6 ml to a US cup of water, and USDA's 200 g to a cup of granulated sugar.

**Steps**

1. Fill the moka pot's base with water to just below the safety valve, fill the basket level with coffee without pressing it down, and screw the pot together.
2. Put the sugar in a small metal pitcher or a 2-cup measuring cup.
3. Set the pot over medium heat. As soon as coffee starts to come up, pour about ½ tablespoon of it over the sugar, then put the pot back on the heat.
4. Beat the sugar and coffee with a small spoon for 1 to 2 minutes, until it turns into a creamy, pale beige paste and most of the sugar has dissolved.
5. When the pot starts to sputter, take it off the heat. Pour the rest of the coffee gently over the paste and stir gently; a thin layer of foam should float on top.
6. Pour into espresso cups and serve right away.

**How much it makes.** About 8 US fl oz (240 ml) in all from a pot that takes about 300 g of water, by our Brew Guide's estimate, so about 2 fl oz (60 ml) a cup (calculated). That's a sweet cup: Bustelo's ¼ to ⅓ cup of sugar works out to about 12.5–17 g per serving, roughly 3 to 4 teaspoons (calculated from USDA's 4.2 g per teaspoon). Bustelo gives a range, so start at the lower end if you like it less sweet.

**Why the order matters.** The first coffee goes into the sugar while there's very little of it, so you get a thick paste you can beat rather than sweet coffee you stir; Bustelo's step 3 says to pour it "as soon as espresso begins to brew." Barista Magazine's guide to the drink mentions demerara sugar, and Hoffmann describes a café cubano made a different way again, with the sugar added to the ground coffee before brewing.

## Which coffee to use

Barista Magazine describes a café cubano as made from dark, Italian-style roasts, and names Pilón, Bustelo and La Llave as popular store-bought brands. Two of them:

- **Café Bustelo.** As published by the maker, its Espresso Ground Coffee is a dark, finely ground "espresso style" coffee that works in a moka pot, a drip machine or an espresso machine. The company says Gregorio Bustelo founded it after arriving in New York's East Harlem from Spain over a century ago; the J.M. Smucker Company owns it now.
- **Café Pilón.** The brand's site, run by Rowland Coffee Roasters, says its origins can be traced back to Cuba and pitches it for espresso drinks.

Neither is grown in Cuba. If you'd like to try the classic, the <a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Cafe+Bustelo+Espresso+Ground+Coffee+Brick+10+oz&tag=jayarrcoffee-20" rel="sponsored noopener">Café Bustelo Espresso ground coffee, 10 oz brick</a> and the <a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Cafe+Pilon+Espresso+Ground+Coffee+10+oz&tag=jayarrcoffee-20" rel="sponsored noopener">Café Pilón Espresso ground coffee, 10 oz brick</a> are both on Amazon. Hoffmann, writing about moka pots in general, steers away from dark roasts because the method already tends toward bitterness; in a cafecito, the sugar does a lot to balance it. If yours tastes harsh, our [moka pot guide](/blog/moka-pot-brewing-guide/) gives the first fixes: take the pot off the heat the moment it sputters and cool the base, then grind a touch coarser.

## The moka pot

You don't need an espresso machine. Barista Magazine notes that a café cubano at home starts with a moka pot, while Miami's walk-up windows typically make their coladas on espresso machines. It isn't espresso, strictly: our [moka pot guide](/blog/moka-pot-brewing-guide/) explains that espresso machines brew at about 9 bar, while a moka pot pushes the water up with the steam in its base.

Bialetti's manual gives the essentials:

- **Fill the base to the lower edge of the safety valve,** never above it.
- **Fill the basket generously but don't press the coffee down.** Use coffee ground for moka, not a fine espresso grind.
- **Point the safety valve away from you, hold the pot by its handle and knob, and let it cool before opening it.**

Hoffmann adds the cue to stop: take the pot off the heat when it starts to gurgle and sputter, because by then steam is coming through the coffee and making it more bitter.

## The Cuban coffee drinks

| Drink | What it is | How it's served |
|---|---|---|
| Cafecito (café cubano) | Strong coffee sweetened with the whipped sugar foam | One demitasse cup |
| Colada | The same coffee, made to share | A foam cup of about 12 oz with about six small cups |
| Cortadito | Coffee and milk in about equal measure, sweetened with the sugar foam | A small cup |
| Café con leche | Coffee with steamed milk, comparable to a latte | One cup, or coffee and milk in two |

Barista Magazine's guide (2022) describes all four, and Hoffmann lists the cortadito, café con leche and café cubano as the Cuban preparations that have spread around the world. The colada is the one built for sharing: one order, poured out for the people around you.

Barista Magazine credits Felipe Valls Sr., who designed and installed the first *ventanitas*, walk-up service windows, at his Miami restaurants after moving there in the 1960s. Ventanitas still sell café cubano and pastries, some of them around the clock.

## Coffee grown in Cuba

Hoffmann tells the island's coffee history briefly. Coffee came to Cuba from Hispaniola in 1748, but there was little industry until French settlers fled the Haitian revolution in 1791. By 1827 Cuba had about two thousand coffee farms, and coffee earned more than sugar. After the revolution nationalized the farms, production dropped; it reached a later peak in the 1970s, at around 30,000 tonnes, about 500,000 60 kg bags (calculated). The Soviet Union's collapse left Cuba isolated, and the US embargo closed a major potential market; Japan has been the main buyer of Cuban coffee, with Europe a strong market too.

USDA's figures, by marketing year, show how far it has fallen:

| Marketing year | Cuba's production (60 kg bags) |
|---|---|
| 1961/62 | 750,000 (the series' high since 1960) |
| 1990/91 | 480,000 |
| 2000/01 | 313,000 |
| 2007/08 | 70,000 (the low) |
| 2016/17 | 150,000 |
| 2026/27 | 100,000 (forecast) |

USDA has kept its Cuban estimate at 100,000 bags every year since 2020/21, and expects about 10,000 of them to be exported in 2026/27. That's in line with Hoffmann's 6,000–7,000 tonnes a year, about 100,000–117,000 bags (calculated). (An earlier version of this page, and our 2020 video, said Cuba produced 7,000 bags in 2007; USDA's figure is 70,000.)

Hoffmann adds that the best coffees, about a fifth of the crop, are exported, and that Cuba's own production doesn't cover its demand, so high prices brought back the habit of mixing roasted peas into coffee. A 2011 BBC report carried by *Marketplace* traced that pea blend in the ration-book coffee back to the 1980s.

**Where it grows and what it tastes like.** Hoffmann puts most of Cuba's coffee in the Sierra Maestra, along the southern coast, at 1,000–1,200 m, with smaller amounts in the Sierra del Escambray (350–900 m) and the Sierra del Rosario (300–550 m); he gives a July–December harvest, mostly Typica with some Bourbon, Caturra, Catuai and Catimor, and a typical island profile, low in acidity with a heavier body. Most export coffee is washed, and few lots can be traced to a single farm.

## Can you buy Cuban-grown coffee in the US?

Sometimes. The US Treasury's Cuban Assets Control Regulations bar anyone subject to US jurisdiction from dealing in goods of Cuban origin unless authorized (31 CFR 515.204). One authorization, section 515.582, covers goods produced by independent Cuban entrepreneurs that the State Department lists as eligible.

- **Coffee was added in 2016.** On April 22, 2016, the State Department updated its list to allow coffee and some textiles produced by independent Cuban entrepreneurs, and dropped the requirement that they be imported directly from Cuba; the notice was published in the Federal Register on May 18, 2016.
- **It still applies.** When we checked the State Department's list on October 18, 2026, coffee (tariff heading 0901) was still carved out of the excluded vegetable products, so it remains eligible. The importer must hold documentary evidence that the producer is independent, such as the producer's Cuban self-employment license, or proof that a private entity isn't owned or controlled by the Cuban government.
- **The 2026 tightening didn't change it.** OFAC's September 30, 2026 amendments to the regulations restricted financial transactions and travel; they didn't amend section 515.582, whose last amendment dates from May 2024.
- **Travelers.** People traveling to Cuba under an authorized category may bring back Cuban merchandise as accompanied baggage for personal use; the exclusion is for alcohol and tobacco, not coffee (31 CFR 515.560(c)(3)).

This is a summary of the published rules, not legal advice; anyone importing should check with OFAC. Hoffmann's 2018 line that "true Cuban coffee is illegal in the United States" is too broad: it leaves out the entrepreneur exception, in force since 2016.

For other coffee cultures built around a cup, Mexico's [café de olla](/blog/whats-the-flavor-of-mexican-coffee/) is a good parallel, and our guide to [how coffee is roasted](/blog/how-is-coffee-roasted/) explains what a dark roast does to a bean.
