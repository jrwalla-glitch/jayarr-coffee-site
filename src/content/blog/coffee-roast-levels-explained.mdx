Light, medium and dark are useful words, but they're shorthand. Jessica Easto puts it plainly in *Craft Coffee*: the terms don't correspond to any "magical roasting time." Roasters treat each lot of beans differently, testing roast profiles until they get the result they want.

This page covers what the levels mean and how to read them on a bag. For how roasting works, from drying to second crack, see [how is coffee roasted?](/blog/how-is-coffee-roasted/), and to roast your own, [how to roast coffee at home](/blog/how-to-roast-coffee-at-home/).

## There's no standard for light, medium or dark

Nobody owns the words, and the people who measure roasts most closely use them differently:

- **Researchers.** Christina Rune and colleagues roasted five specialty coffees to three levels for a 2023 study and called them lighter, medium and darker. They note that their "medium" roast is closer to what some earlier studies call light, and that their "darker" roasts fall in the range other studies call medium.
- **Roasters.** Rob Hoos, a production roaster, tells readers to trust the number a roast-color meter gives rather than the roast level it names, which he has never found helpful. Of his own weight-loss scale he writes that people all have different perspectives on light, medium and dark.
- **The Specialty Coffee Association.** For cupping, the SCA says the recommended roast "has mostly been described as 'medium'," and lists the color readings different authors have given for medium. It adds that the scales used by roast meters are arbitrary, and that even meters on the same scale don't always agree.

So compare roast levels within one roaster's range. Across roasters, a bag marked "medium" tells you only roughly what to expect.

## How roasters measure a roast

The SCA lists four ways to control roast level: color, measured against the Agtron/SCA Roast Color Classification discs or on the CIELAB color scale; infrared meters, such as Agtron's; the dry matter the beans lose; and how much they swell.

**Color.** On the Agtron scale, a higher number means a lighter roast, as the readings below show. In Hoos's figures, ground coffee reads well above whole beans from the same roast, so a published number should say which it is. Here are the references we could trace to their sources:

| Source | What they call it | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| SCA (2024) | Cupping roast, usually described as medium | Agtron "Gourmet" 63.0 target; authors' medium references from 54 to 65 on the Agtron/SCA discs |
| SCA (2024) | Dark roast, its taste reference for bitterness | About Agtron #35 |
| Rune and colleagues (2023) | Lighter, medium, darker | Agtron, ground coffee: 102–115, 71–81 and 50–62 |
| Hoos (2025) | Light, for his Typica roasts | Agtron about 70 whole bean and 110 ground, at 11–12% weight loss |

**Weight loss.** Roasting drives off water and other compounds, so the beans weigh less. Hoos uses weight loss as one guide to roast degree: under about 11% he suspects the coffee is under-roasted, 11–13% is almost always light, 14–16% is medium to him, and 17–18% is dark, with roast notes taking over. Above that, he finds the roast extremely dark. For comparison, James Hoffmann writes that slow roasts of 14 to 20 minutes lose about 16 to 18%, and in Adriana Farah and colleagues' 2005 roasting study, about 14% weight loss was a light-medium roast. Weight loss also depends on how wet the green coffee was, as Hoos notes, so it's a guide rather than a grade.

## What changes from light to dark

Hoffmann describes the trade-off with three curves. Acidity falls the longer a coffee is roasted, while bitterness rises slowly with roast time and definitely with darkness. Sweetness rises and falls between them, peaking somewhere in the middle, and a good roaster can move where a coffee is sweetest.

- **The coffee's own character, or the roast's.** Lighter roasts show more of what the coffee brings, which Easto calls its origin character: where it grew, its variety and its processing. By second crack, Hoffmann writes, much of the acidity is gone and a generic "roast" flavor develops from charring, whatever the coffee. He describes French and Italian roasts as very dark, usually heavy in body and bitterness, with many of the raw coffee's characteristics lost. Easto quotes the roaster George Howell: "Dark roast covers things like a heavy sauce." Which you prefer, she writes, is a matter of taste, like preferring the sauce or the meat.
- **Bitterness.** Most of it is made in the roaster. In light to medium roasts it comes mainly from compounds called lactones, and in darker roasts from harsher, lingering phenylindanes, as Thomas Hofmann's group found. Caffeine is a small part: Hofmann estimated about 15% of coffee's perceived bitterness. Our [chlorogenic acids note](/deep-brew/research/chlorogenic-acid-cascades/) explains where these compounds come from.
- **Sweetness.** By the end of first crack, Hoffmann writes, few or no simple sugars are left, so a roast's sweetness comes mostly from aroma and from less sourness and bitterness. The [sweetness note](/deep-brew/research/sweetness-paradox-roasting/) covers the evidence.
- **Color and body.** The brown polymers called melanoidins give roasted coffee its color, and in one study of a single coffee their amount in the brew rose from light to medium to dark roast. Our [melanoidins note](/deep-brew/research/melanoidins-body-color-health/) covers how much they do for body, which is less settled than often claimed.
- **Oils.** Second crack drives oils to the surface of the bean, Hoffmann writes. Jonathan Gagné adds that oils also migrate out of beans as they age, faster in darker roasts and decaf, over days to months. Shiny beans are dark, old or both.

**Light isn't the same as underdeveloped.** Easto warns that a roast can be too light, without enough time in the heat, and then taste of wood or bread. The SCA describes a related fault, a roast so fast that the outside of the bean is much darker than the inside. A good light roast is a choice; an underdeveloped one is a defect.

## Caffeine: about the same

Roasting barely changes the caffeine in a bean. Easto writes that caffeine is neither created nor destroyed in roasting. Adriana Farah's review says roasting doesn't alter it significantly, apart from small losses, though the share of caffeine can rise a little as other compounds are lost.

What changes is how much coffee you measure. Darker beans weigh less, so a gram of dark roast holds more beans: Easto counts about 90 more beans in a pound of dark roast than in a pound of light. Weighed out, the dark roast has slightly more caffeine; measured by the scoop, the light roast does, because light beans swell less and more of them fit. Either way the difference is small. The species matters far more: robusta has about twice the caffeine of arabica, as our [arabica vs robusta guide](/blog/arabica-vs-robusta-differences/) explains, and the [caffeine guide](/blog/caffeine-in-coffee-guide/) covers the rest.

## Brewing light and dark roasts

The usual explanation, which Easto gives, is that light roasts are less soluble and take more coaxing to get the flavors into the cup. Gagné's own measurements complicate that. Across the coffees in his filter-brewing logs, darker roasts gave lower average extraction yields, not higher, and the trend held within each origin and among brews of similar length. When he brewed six roasts of a single green coffee, the darkest gave the lowest yields, though the lightest were slightly lower too. He cautions that espresso, with its much shorter brew, may behave differently.

In practice, he brewed a light-roasted Gesha with kettle water at 210°F&nbsp;(99°C) and a darker Catimor at 194°F&nbsp;(90°C), to avoid bitterness in the darker one. Our Brew Guide follows the same pattern: for a V60, it starts light roasts at 205°F&nbsp;(96°C), medium at 201°F&nbsp;(94°C) and dark at 196°F&nbsp;(91°C), with a finer grind for light roasts and a coarser one for dark. Our [brewing temperature guide](/blog/whats-the-ideal-coffee-brewing-temperature/) explains why the kettle isn't the brew.

## How to read the roast on a bag

- **Find the roast date.** Hoffmann writes that coffee is at its best when used within a month of roasting. The Freshness tool shows where a bag is from its date.
- **Read the roast word against the roaster's own range.** "Light" from a roaster who sells mostly dark roasts can be darker than "medium" from a roaster who sells mostly light ones. Names such as City, Full City, French or Italian are roasters' own labels, not grades on a shared scale.
- **If there's an Agtron number, check the scale.** Higher means lighter, and in Hoos's figures a ground reading is well above a whole-bean reading of the same roast.
- **Read the tasting notes as a guide.** They describe what the coffee can taste like brewed well. Our guide to [reading a coffee bag label](/blog/how-to-read-coffee-bag-label/) covers the rest of the label.
- **Look at the beans.** A shiny, oily surface means a dark roast, an old one, or both.
