## What Kenyan coffee tastes like

These are notes a Kenyan coffee *can* show. Roasters' descriptions keep returning to the same words (the three listings below mention currant, citrus and berries), but no single bag will have all of them:

- **Bright, intense acidity.** Think grapefruit, blackcurrant or lemongrass rather than plain sourness.
- **Berry and fruit.** Blackcurrant is the classic Kenyan note. Grapefruit, tomato and tropical fruit come up too, depending on the lot.
- **A full, juicy body** with some weight in the mouth.
- **A dry, wine-like finish.**
- **Floral and tea-like notes:** jasmine, bergamot, black tea.

You might taste blackcurrant and grapefruit in the first sip, then pick up tomato leaf and jasmine as the cup cools. It's a coffee with presence rather than a mellow one. Hoffmann describes Ethiopia's coffees, by contrast, as ranging from citrus and florals to candied and tropical fruit (our [Ethiopian coffee guide](/blog/ethiopian-coffee-flavors-and-varieties/) covers them), while Indonesia's wet-hulled coffees tend toward lower acidity, more body and earthy, woody notes ([what Indonesian coffee tastes like](/blog/what-does-indonesian-coffee-taste-like/)).

### Is it the phosphoric acid?

You'll often read that Kenyan coffee gets its sparkle from phosphoric acid, the acid in cola. The best evidence we found doesn't support that. In a 2023 study, Christina Birke Rune and colleagues measured the acids in five light-roasted specialty coffees from Brazil, Bolivia and Kenya. Phosphoric acid levels came out about the same across all five. The two Kenyan coffees actually had *less* citric acid, and lower organic acid levels overall, than the Brazilian and Bolivian ones. In their taste tests, only citric acid was clearly detectable at the levels found in coffee, and coffee experts couldn't pick out any single acid added at its usual concentration.

Five coffees is a small sample, so this doesn't settle what makes Kenyan acidity feel the way it does. It does mean these measurements don't back the cola explanation.

## Why Kenyan coffee tends to taste this way

### The varieties: SL28 and SL34

Most of Kenya's best-known coffee comes from two varieties selected at Scott Agricultural Laboratories in Kenya in the late 1930s. Hoffmann writes that they make up the majority of high-quality Kenyan coffee.

- **SL28** was selected in 1935 from a single tree in a population called "Tanganyika Drought Resistant," according to World Coffee Research's variety catalog, which places it in the Bourbon-related group. Hoffmann notes that it's capable of a distinct fruit flavor "often described as blackcurrant."
- **SL34** came from a single tree on the Loresho Estate in Kabete. The tree was labeled "French Mission," a name usually taken to mean Bourbon, but World Coffee Research reports that genetic tests link SL34 to Typica instead.

World Coffee Research rates both varieties' quality potential at high altitude as "Exceptional," the top of its scale, and both as susceptible to coffee leaf rust and coffee berry disease. SL28 is the lower-yielding of the two.

Kenya has bred disease-resistant varieties to stand alongside them:

- **Ruiru 11,** released in 1985 after a coffee berry disease epidemic. It's a compact F1 hybrid that's highly resistant to leaf rust and resistant to coffee berry disease, with very high yield. World Coffee Research rates its quality potential "Good," and Hoffmann notes that specialty buyers never warmed to it.
- **Batian,** released in 2010 and bred from parents including SL28, SL34, Rume Sudan and the Timor Hybrid. It resists coffee berry disease, has intermediate rust resistance, and World Coffee Research rates its quality potential "Very Good." Hoffmann reports early skepticism about its cup, but growing positivity about its potential.

A bag that names the variety tells you more than one that doesn't. Our [coffee varietal guide](/blog/coffee-varietal-guide/) covers these families in more depth.

### Washing, soaking and slow drying

Kenya processes almost all its arabica by the washed method. An FAO annex on Kenyan post-harvest practice describes the usual sequence at a cooperative's "factory" (its wet mill):

1. The cherries are sorted, then pulped.
2. The parchment ferments in tanks for 1 to 4 days, depending on the weather, until the mucilage feels gritty enough to wash off.
3. It's washed in concrete channels. Kenyan studies, the FAO annex says, found that soaking the parchment under clean water for about 12 hours after fermentation improved both color and taste.
4. It dries on raised wire-mesh tables for about two weeks in the sun.

Hoffmann notes that washed coffees in general tend toward higher acidity, more complexity and a cleaner cup than other processes, which fits the Kenyan profile. Naturally processed Kenyan coffee, called Mbuni, is a low grade there, around 7 percent of the crop. Our [processing guide](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/) explains the methods themselves.

### Altitude

Kenyan coffee is high-grown. Hoffmann's regional tables put Nyeri at 1,200–2,300 m (3,900–7,500 ft) and most other regions somewhere between about 1,300 and 2,200 m (4,300–7,200 ft).

## Kenya AA, AB and PB: what the grades mean

Kenya grades all of its exported coffee. The grades combine bean size with quality, but the letters mostly describe size. As Hoffmann describes them:

| Grade | What it is |
|---|---|
| E | "Elephant" beans, the very largest, so lots are small |
| AA | Large beans, above screen 18 (7.22 mm); typically the highest prices |
| AB | A mix of A (screen 16, 6.80 mm) and B (screen 15, 6.20 mm); around 30% of production |
| PB | Peaberries: one round seed in the cherry instead of two |
| C, TT, T | Smaller beans, light beans and broken pieces; rarely high quality |
| MH/ML | Mbuni Heavy and Light: naturally processed coffee, sold cheaply |

**AA describes size, not flavor.** Hoffmann writes that AA lots are often the superior coffees, but that he has seen harvests where AB lots were more complex than many AAs. On a bag, a named region, washing station or estate, and variety tell you more than the grade alone. Peaberries are sorted separately; our [Tanzania peaberry review](/blog/stone-street-tanzania-peaberry-grind-and-brew/) looks at one.

## Kenya's growing regions

Hoffmann writes that central Kenya produces most of the country's coffee, and the best of it, with growing interest in western regions such as Kisii, Trans-Nzoia, Keiyo and Marakwet. Most coffee comes from smallholders who deliver cherry to a cooperative's washing station; estates are more common in Kiambu.

| Region | Altitude (Hoffmann) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Nyeri | 1,200–2,300 m | Central; red soils near Mount Kenya; mostly cooperatives |
| Kirinyaga | 1,300–1,900 m | Nyeri's eastern neighbor; mostly smallholders |
| Murang'a | 1,350–1,950 m | Central; around 100,000 coffee farmers |
| Embu | 1,300–1,900 m | Near Mount Kenya; almost all smallholders |
| Kiambu | 1,500–2,200 m | Mostly estates, many sold for housing as towns spread |
| Kisii | 1,450–1,800 m | Southwest, near Lake Victoria; cooperatives |

Hoffmann lists the same seasons for every Kenyan region: a main crop from October to December and a smaller fly crop from June to August. Importers publish slightly different windows, so treat the months as typical rather than fixed; our [coffee harvest calendar](/blog/coffee-harvest-calendar/) compares them. USDA's 2026 report notes that many coffee farms around Nairobi, Thika, Kiambu and Nyeri were uprooted for housing over the last decade, a trend that has slowed for now.

## How Kenya sells its coffee

Most Kenyan coffee goes through the Nairobi Coffee Exchange. USDA's 2026 report says the exchange, founded in 1935, handles over 95 percent of sales; the rest are direct contracts registered with the regulator. Roughly 80 percent of Kenyan coffee is sold through producer cooperatives.

The rules changed in 2026. In March, Kenya enacted a new Coffee Act that moves oversight to a revived Coffee Board of Kenya. It also writes into law reforms under way since 2022, including the Direct Settlement System, a digital platform that pays farmers directly.

The auction is meant to reward quality, and Hoffmann notes that buyers do pay high prices for excellent lots, though he also writes that corruption may keep those premiums from reaching farmers. Prices move with the world market: USDA reports that the exchange's average fell from about 375 US dollars per 50 kg bag in October 2025 to about 269 in April 2026.

Almost all of it is exported. USDA estimates that in 2025/26 Kenya produced 850,000 bags and exported 840,000, while the country drank 62,000 bags, a figure that includes imported instant coffee. The European Union takes more than half of Kenya's coffee exports, and the United States was the largest single destination in 2024/25.

## Brewing Kenyan coffee

Hoffmann notes that paper filters strain out fine particles and oils for the cleanest cup, so a pour-over shows off the clarity and acidity most people buy Kenyan coffee for. A French press's metal mesh lets more through, for a heavier cup. For a light-roasted Kenyan coffee in a V60, our Brew Guide starts at 1:16 with the kettle at 205°F&nbsp;(96°C), the hot end of its range for light roasts. If the cup tastes sour or harsh, change the grind first, one step at a time; our [V60 guide](/blog/ultimate-pour-over-coffee-guide-and-best-v60-technique/) walks through it.

For espresso, start at the Brew Guide's base recipe, 18 g of coffee to 36 g of espresso in 25 to 30 seconds, and adjust the grind from there. Try a Kenyan coffee black before adding milk or sugar; those soften the acidity and fruit you're paying for.

If a lot is worth keeping, freeze it in airtight portions while it's fresh. Most roasted coffee tastes best about 7 to 21 days after roasting, a typical window rather than a promise for your bag; our [guide to freezing coffee beans](/blog/how-to-freeze-coffee-beans/) covers the method.
