A steam wand does two jobs at once: it pushes air into the milk and it heats it. Good steamed milk comes from doing them one at a time, air first while the milk is still cold, then heat. The method below follows Scott Rao's *The Professional Barista's Handbook* and James Hoffmann's *The World Atlas of Coffee*, with Breville's own instructions for its Bambino where a maker's numbers help.

The goal is microfoam: bubbles so small they're almost invisible, so the milk turns glossy, elastic and pourable. Hoffmann likens great milk foam to liquid marshmallow.

## What you need

- **An espresso machine with a steam wand.** This method is for a plain wand. If yours has a frothing sleeve or an automatic milk setting, Hoffmann's advice is to follow the maker's instructions.
- **A stainless-steel pitcher.** Rao uses the smallest pitcher that comfortably holds the milk, filled a third to half full. Hoffmann says no more than 60 percent full.
- **Cold, fresh milk.** Hoffmann finds milk near its use-by date still foams, but the bubbles soon break down, fizzing as the air escapes.
- **A thermometer,** if you can. Rao recommends one for every barista, since touch is inconsistent.
- **A damp cloth** for the wand.

## How much milk to start with

Pour only as much milk as the drink needs, Rao says. Two published recipes in our Coffee Drinks tool give a sense of scale: the Italian certification cappuccino starts from 100&nbsp;ml of cold milk, which steams to about 125&nbsp;ml, and a Spanish specialty latte uses 180 to 240&nbsp;g of milk with 40&nbsp;g of espresso.

Then pick the pitcher. At Rao's third to half full, a 12&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz (355&nbsp;ml) pitcher takes about 120 to 175&nbsp;ml of milk, and a 20&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz (590&nbsp;ml) pitcher about 200 to 295&nbsp;ml (calculated). The headroom is where the foam goes.

## Stretching and texturing are two different jobs

**Stretching** adds air and volume. Rao has you finish it before the milk reaches 100°F&nbsp;(38°C), because once milk is warmer than that it's harder to make good foam. Listen as well as look: Rao describes a consistent, subtle sucking noise, Hoffmann a slurping sound as the wand starts to whip in air.

**Texturing** heats the milk and works the foam in. With the tip sunk a little and off to one side, the milk spins and churns, and the process goes quiet. Hoffmann's point is that with most steam wands you should tackle one job at a time: first create the bubbles, then, once you have the volume you want, focus only on heating.

Some wands, and machines with very high steam pressure, make foam too quickly with the tip at the surface. Rao's fixes are to aerate with the tip a little deeper, use partial steam pressure or turn the boiler pressure down.

## How much air for each drink

Rao's textures, for a classic 6 to 8&nbsp;oz cup with 1 to 1½&nbsp;oz of espresso, measured by letting the drink separate and pushing the foam back with a spoon:

- **Cappuccino:** very aerated milk, with about ½&nbsp;inch (about 13&nbsp;mm) of foam. It should be deep and plush, with no separate layer of unfoamed milk underneath.
- **Latte:** moderately aerated, with about ¼&nbsp;inch (about 6&nbsp;mm) of foam.
- **Flat white:** minimally aerated, with only a skin of foam.

Rao notes the depth also depends on how wide the cup is. Cimbali's recipes are similar: about 1&nbsp;cm (0.4&nbsp;inch) of foam on a cappuccino and half a centimeter on a flat white. The more foam a drink needs, the longer you stretch. Our [flat white vs latte](/blog/what-is-a-flat-white-latte-vs-flat-white-comparison/) and [cortado](/blog/what-is-a-cortado/) guides cover those drinks.

## When to stop: temperature

**Stay below 154°F&nbsp;(68°C).** Hoffmann says milk begins to degrade irreversibly in flavor and texture above that, as the heat denatures its proteins and creates new flavors, not always good ones.

**Aim for about 140–149°F&nbsp;(60–65°C).** That's the range Breville gives in its Bambino manual. Hoffmann finds a very hot cappuccino lacks the texture, flavor and sweetness of one made with milk heated only to 140°F&nbsp;(60°C). Rao's café target is higher, 150 to 160°F&nbsp;(66 to 71°C), and the top of that range is past Hoffmann's limit; go there only if you want a hotter drink.

**Know your cue.**

- **By hand:** hand cues vary. Hoffmann holds the bottom of the pitcher until it becomes uncomfortable to touch, at about 131°F&nbsp;(55°C), then steams three to five seconds more. Breville's manual says the milk is in range when the jug is hot to touch.
- **By thermometer:** Rao prefers this, because touch varies between baristas and dulls with repeated exposure to hot pitchers. A thermometer lags, but predictably. Rao turns the steam off 10°F early for 10&nbsp;oz of milk and 5°F early for 20&nbsp;oz.

Does steaming make milk sweeter? It adds no sugar, but temperature changes how sweet things taste. In a 2005 study in *Nature*, Talavera and colleagues found that mouse taste nerves responded much more strongly to sweet compounds as the temperature rose from 59°F to 95°F&nbsp;(15 to 35°C), and they suggest this underlies the stronger sweetness people perceive at higher temperatures. That range stops at body temperature, so it can't say how sweet a 150°F latte tastes. For hotter milk, Hoffmann's experience is the guide: once it's overheated, it loses sweetness rather than gaining it.

## Whole milk, skim and plant milks

Protein supports the bubbles, so milk foams well whether it's skim or whole, Hoffmann says. The fat changes the drink. In his experience a skim-milk cappuccino has an immediate, intense coffee flavor that doesn't linger, while whole milk makes the flavor less intense but longer-lasting, and he recommends whole milk for its texture. Lab work agrees that fat matters: Huppertz's review of milk foams finds skim milk foams can be very stable, while fat can hinder making and keeping foam, especially around 68°F&nbsp;(20°C), when milk fat is partly solid.

Barista plant milks steam with the same two phases. In Tarasov and colleagues' 2025 tests at 149°F&nbsp;(65°C), barista plant drinks steamed to a foaming capacity of 93 to 202 percent, against 197 percent for cow's milk, and the authors judged all of them workable alternatives for cappuccino. Our comparison of [oat milk and dairy](/blog/oat-milk-vs-dairy-in-coffee/) explains what barista formulations add, the [plant milk guide](/blog/best-plant-milks-for-coffee/) covers soy, pea, almond and others, and our [oat milk label test](/blog/your-6-dollar-oat-milk-is-wrecking-your-latte/) explains why soy can curdle in coffee.

## Timing the milk with the espresso

Rao times it so the steaming finishes a few seconds before the shot does: the milk needs about 5 seconds to settle after the steam goes off. If the shot isn't ready, keep the milk spinning in the pitcher to slow separation, but still pour within 30 seconds of steaming.

Some machines can't brew and steam at the same time. On those, Hoffmann brews the espresso first, then switches to steam. For the shot itself, see our [espresso dial-in guide](/blog/how-to-dial-in-espresso/); for the pour, our [latte art guide](/blog/latte-art-beginners-guide/) takes it from the pitcher to the cup.

## Fixing common problems

| What you see | Likely cause | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Big bubbles on top | Too much air, or the tip too high | Let the milk sit a few seconds, tap the pitcher to pop them, then swirl. Next time, keep the tip just under the surface. |
| Stiff, thick foam | Stretched too long | Skim some foam off the top with a spoon, swirl, and repeat until it's right. |
| Thin milk with little foam | Not enough air | You can't add air afterward. Steam a fresh pitcher and stretch a little longer. |
| Foam that fizzes and collapses | Milk near its use-by date | Use fresher milk. |
| Cooked, eggy taste | Heated past 154°F&nbsp;(68°C) | Stop earlier, and use a thermometer. |
| Foam billows up as soon as the steam opens | Strong steam with the tip at the surface | Aerate with the tip a little deeper, or at partial pressure. |
| Weak steam, slow spin | Blocked holes in the steam tip | Purge the wand; clear the holes as your machine's manual describes. |

These are common causes, not certainties: Rao and Hoffmann give the fixes for foam, and Breville's manual gives the one for a blocked tip.

## Cleaning the wand

Wipe the wand with a damp cloth and purge it as soon as you've finished. Breville's Bambino manual says to clean the wand every time you texture milk, and to run steam through it afterward to reduce blockages. If the holes in the tip still clog, it says to purge again, then unscrew the tip and clear the holes with the cleaning tool supplied with the machine.
