Most pour-over drippers look alike, but they differ in four ways that change how you brew: the shape of the coffee bed, the holes the water leaves through, the material, and the filter they take. I've brewed through all eight of the drippers in this guide, some for years, some long enough to know I'd never use them again. The comparison below sets out what each maker publishes and what Jonathan Gagné, an astrophysicist who studies brewing, measured or observed, grouped by what each dripper suits.

Our [flat-bottom vs cone guide](/blog/flat-bottom-vs-cone-pour-over/) explains what the shape changes, including bypass, the water that slips past the coffee instead of through it. In short, Gagné finds a cone more versatile across doses and more prone to bypass, and a flat bottom less prone to it, with brews that tend to taste a little sweeter in his experience.

## To learn the pour: a cone

### Hario V60 02

Hario describes the V60's cone shape as building a deeper layer of coffee, and says its spiral ridges and lack of flow restriction let you pour quickly for a delicate body or slowly for a heavier flavor. The plastic 02 is listed for 1 to 4 cups and made in Japan, at $13 in white or red and $14 in clear on Hario USA's store. The clear one is acrylonitrile-styrene (AS) resin; Hario lists the red and white ones as polypropylene; the porcelain version is $35.

Gagné's notes on the V60:

- **Bypass** can be significant, depending on your method, grind and filter.
- **Material:** ceramic and glass V60s give lower, less stable slurry temperatures than the better-insulating plastic one. In his measurements, a plastic V60 brewed with the kettle at 200°F&nbsp;(93°C) averaged 182°F&nbsp;(84°C) in the cone, and 191°F&nbsp;(88°C) with the kettle at 210°F&nbsp;(99°C).
- **Filters:** it probably has the largest filter area relative to the dose of any dripper, which helps trap fines, and many papers fit it.
- **Handling:** its round base makes the slurry easy to swirl.

It rewards a steady pour more than most. Our [V60 guide](/blog/ultimate-pour-over-coffee-guide-and-best-v60-technique/) gives a complete recipe.

To buy: Hario's own store, or the <a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Hario+V60+Plastic+Coffee+Dripper+Size+02&tag=jayarrcoffee-20" rel="sponsored noopener">Hario V60 plastic dripper, size 02</a> on Amazon.

### Origami Dripper

The Origami is a thin porcelain cone shaped like a pleated filter. Slow Pour Supply, which describes itself as an official distributor, listed the Small for 1 to 2 cups, holding up to about 25 g of grounds, and the Medium for 1 to 4 cups, at $39 (most colors; selected colors were $35) and $40.75 when checked. Both are Mino-yaki porcelain from Toki, in Gifu. The Small takes V60 01 cone filters or Kalita 155 wave filters; the Medium takes V60 02 or Kalita 185. Slow Pour Supply strongly recommends a holder, sold separately, to keep it steady on a mug.

Gagné finds it similar to a ceramic V60 in several ways, with differences:

- Its bottom hole is larger than a V60's, so it generally drains faster.
- Bypass is like a V60's, though pleated filters can cling to its walls a little more.
- Being light, it cools the slurry less than a ceramic V60 at first, but ceramic conducts heat, so it loses heat faster than plastic.
- Its base is awkward, and the dripper can slip off it when you swirl.

Choose it if you'd like to try cone and flat-bottom brewing in one dripper. Amazon's results for it are mostly lookalikes, so buy it from [Slow Pour Supply](https://www.slowpoursupply.co/products/origami-ceramic-dripper-small-for-1-2-cup-brewing) or another authorized seller.

## To let the dripper set the pace: a flat bottom

### Kalita Wave 185

Kalita describes a flat-bottomed coffee bed, three small holes and a 20-wave filter that touches the dripper very little, which it says stops water accumulating so "extractions are even and bitterness is dodged." The stainless-steel 185 is listed for 2 to 4 cups, made in Japan and dishwasher safe, at $42 on Kalita USA's store, with Wave 185 filters at $13 for 100. A glass 185 is $39.

Gagné's notes are on the smaller Wave 155, the same design. He finds it less susceptible to bypass, because of its flat bottom and gentle wall slope, and notes that its three small holes slow the water more than most drippers. Steel takes little heat at first but conducts it away, and the pleated filters trap insulating air in their folds. It's less versatile than a V60 across doses, because the bed depth changes more with the dose. Jessica Easto, in *Craft Coffee*, calls it versatile and forgiving and suggests brewing it in pulses.

To buy: Kalita USA, or the <a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Kalita+Wave+185+Stainless+Steel+Dripper&tag=jayarrcoffee-20" rel="sponsored noopener">Kalita Wave 185 stainless dripper</a> on Amazon, where lookalike "replacement for Kalita" listings appear among the results.

### Fellow Stagg [X]

Fellow describes a flat-bottomed, vacuum-insulated dripper with a steep slope, a hole-and-bump pattern for clog-free drainage and a ratio aid. It comes in two sizes, the [X] and the taller [XF]. When checked, Fellow listed the [X] at $59.95, sold out on its own, and in a pour-over set at $79.95; the [XF] was listed at $69.95.

Gagné calls the Stagg [X] and [XF] the most thermally stable pour-over drippers he has used. His other notes:

- **Flow** is relatively fast through ten small holes, provided the filter doesn't collapse onto them, which the shallow ridges make easy. He suggests lifting the filter walls slightly when rinsing.
- **Taste:** he gets slightly higher extractions and noticeably sweeter brews with it, and thinks low bypass is part of the reason.
- **Handling:** its rubber pad makes swirling awkward unless you remove it or set the dripper on a V60 base.

In his matched test against a plastic V60, both brews reached the same extraction, yet the Stagg cup tasted sweeter to him; our [flat-bottom guide](/blog/flat-bottom-vs-cone-pour-over/) has the details.

To buy: Fellow's store, or the <a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Fellow+Stagg+X+Pour-Over+Dripper&tag=jayarrcoffee-20" rel="sponsored noopener">Fellow Stagg [X] dripper</a> on Amazon.

### Blue Bottle Dripper

Blue Bottle describes a porcelain dripper with a flat bed for even saturation, forty interior ridges and a single 4.45&nbsp;mm nozzle meant to give one thin thread of coffee. It listed the dripper at $28 on its US store when checked, and its own filters at $15, and recommends hand-washing, or the dishwasher's top rack. It isn't among the example drippers in Gagné's book, so we have only the maker's description. Blue Bottle sells it, and its filters, on [its own store](https://bluebottlecoffee.com/us/eng/product/blue-bottle-coffee-dripper).

## For grocery-store filters: the simplest cones

### Melitta 1-Cup Pour-Over cone

Melitta's 1-Cup Pour-Over cone takes #2 or #4 cone filters, has a window so you can see into the mug, and is BPA free and top-rack dishwasher safe. Melitta's US store listed the plastic cone at $4.99 and a porcelain version at $17.99. Melitta's own instructions call for finely ground coffee and water just below a boil, and say that the slower you pour, "the bolder and more developed the cup will be." It's the cheapest way in, and its filters are easy to find. Amazon's top result is a cone-and-filters combo rather than the cone alone, so the simplest place to buy it is [Melitta's own store](https://shoponline.melitta.com/products/1-cup-pour-over-coffee-brew-cone-black).

### OXO Pour-Over Coffee Maker with Water Tank

OXO's brewer is a cone with a water tank that sits on top. You fill the tank, and its hole pattern spreads the water over the grounds at a set rate, so there's no pouring technique to learn. OXO lists markings up to 12 ounces, a lid that holds in heat and then serves as a drip tray, ten #2 cone filters in the box, and BPA-free, dishwasher-safe parts, and listed it at $19.99 on its store. Our [2020 OXO review](/blog/making-coffee-with-the-oxo-brew-pour-over/) covers it in more detail. To buy: OXO's store, or the <a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=OXO+Brew+Pour-Over+Coffee+Maker+with+Water+Tank&tag=jayarrcoffee-20" rel="sponsored noopener">OXO Pour-Over with Water Tank</a> on Amazon.

## To brew for several people: the Chemex

The Chemex is a glass cone fused to its own carafe. Chemex listed the Six Cup Classic at $49.50 and makes it from borosilicate glass; its instructions count a cup as 5 oz, so six cups is about 30 oz (calculated). It takes Chemex Bonded filters, which Chemex says are 20 to 30% thicker than most of the competition. Chemex says the glass carafe is dishwasher safe once the wood collar and rawhide tie are removed.

Gagné's notes:

- The filter sticks to the smooth glass, so bypass is less of an issue except at the spout, but the small effective filter area makes it easier to clog, especially if the filter collapses into the spout and seals the carafe.
- The thick paper filters oils and fines better but flows more slowly, which may call for a coarser grind than a V60.
- Glass insulates poorly, so the slurry runs cooler and less steadily.

Chemex also makes three-, eight- and ten-cup Classics. To buy: Chemex's store, or the <a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Chemex+Six+Cup+Classic+Series+Glass+Coffeemaker&tag=jayarrcoffee-20" rel="sponsored noopener">Chemex Six Cup Classic</a> on Amazon.

## What else you need

Whichever dripper you choose, you'll also want three things:

1. **A burr grinder.** It makes a more consistent spread of particle sizes than a blade grinder. Our guides compare [manual grinders](/blog/top-10-best-manual-coffee-grinders/) and [electric grinders](/blog/top-5-best-electric-coffee-grinders/), and the [grind size guide](/blog/coffee-grind-size-guide-what-grind-for-every-brew-method/) gives each brewer's starting range.
2. **A kettle you can control.** Gagné explains that a traditional kettle's wide spout makes a gentle, controlled pour difficult, and that a thin, long gooseneck helps. Easto calls the V60 more sensitive to brewing variables than other drippers, so a steady pour counts most there. Our [gooseneck kettle guide](/blog/best-gooseneck-kettles-pour-over/) compares kettles; the OXO's tank does the pouring for you.
3. **A scale.** Brewing by weight makes a recipe repeatable. Our [coffee scale reviews](/blog/5-coffee-scales-reviewed/) cover five options.

Our Brew Guide sets the kettle by roast: 205°F&nbsp;(96°C) for light roasts, 201°F&nbsp;(94°C) for medium and 196°F&nbsp;(91°C) for dark, and the [brewing temperature guide](/blog/whats-the-ideal-coffee-brewing-temperature/) explains why the coffee in the cone runs cooler than that. Use the [coffee-to-water ratio guide](/blog/coffee-to-water-ratio-guide/) to adjust strength, and see [how water hardness and alkalinity change the cup](/blog/why-water-matters-more-than-your-coffee-beans/).
