Before 1960, most Americans drank Colombian coffee without knowing it. A grower in a sombrero and his mule changed that, and the federation behind them turned a commodity into something shoppers asked for by name. This is that history, with dates from the FNC's own pages, its café company and the historians. For what Colombian coffee tastes like, region by region, see our [Colombian coffee guide](/blog/single-origin-colombian-coffee-what-flavor-notes-to-expect/).

## The FNC, founded in 1927

Colombia's coffee growers founded the Federación Nacional de Cafeteros in 1927, the FNC says, to represent them at home and abroad. Its funding came from a coffee export tax created by Law 76 of 1927, the tax that Law 11 of 1972 later repealed, while authorizing ten-year contracts between the government and the federation. In *The World Atlas of Coffee*, James Hoffmann describes the FNC as a private non-profit funded by a special tax on all exported coffee, owned and controlled by its coffee-growing members, about 500,000 when he wrote; its Café de Colombia brand now says it represents 556,000 coffee-growing families. It builds roads, schools and health centers in coffee areas, and, the FNC says, guarantees to buy growers' coffee every day of the year through its cooperatives.

Mark Pendergrast, in *Uncommon Grounds*, writes that the federation quickly gained enormous political clout, becoming in one observer's words "a private State within a not-very-public State." Its research center, Cenicafé, in Chinchiná, Caldas, followed in 1938: the FNC's glossary gives that year, and a history published by Cenicafé dates its creation to Agreement 12 of the ninth national coffee congress, on November 9, 1938. (Some summaries give 1939; the FNC and Cenicafé say 1938.)

The FNC was selling to a market dominated by a few big buyers. By 1927, Pendergrast notes, the A&P grocery chain alone was buying one-tenth of Colombia's coffee, roasting an average of 4,000 bags a week.

## Why Colombia needed a face

Colombian beans were already in American cans; they just weren't on the label. Pendergrast cites a 1963 analysis of "one of the finest blends," probably Folgers: 40 percent Colombian, 20 percent Brazilian, 30 percent Central American and 10 percent African robusta. A World Intellectual Property Organization case study of the FNC's branding reports that before the campaign only 4 percent of US consumers knew Colombia grew coffee, and that prices had fallen from 85 cents to 45 cents a pound in the late 1950s.

## Juan Valdez arrives, 1959 to 1960

The dates depend on what you count:

- **1958:** Hoffmann dates the creation of Juan Valdez to 1958.
- **1959:** the FNC's glossary says the idea of a character to represent its coffee was born in 1959, a man who would stand for the pride, roots and hard work of Colombia's growers. Campaign US and UPI date the character to 1959 as well.
- **1960:** the FNC says Juan Valdez and his mule Conchita went public in 1960, and WIPO says his name was registered as a trademark with the US Patent and Trademark Office that year.

The agency was in New York. WIPO says only that the FNC created him "with the help of a New York-based advertising agency"; Campaign US, in a 2018 report on DDB's return to Juan Valdez after 30 years, credits the agency's founder, Bill Bernbach, with the brand's original 1959 work, and UPI names Doyle Dane Bernbach as Juan Valdez's creator.

Pendergrast describes the launch:

- **January 1960:** the campaign broke in ten major US markets with full-page newspaper ads. The first caption read, "We don't know who's more stubborn—Juan Valdez or his mule." The copy gave Juan a finca 5,000 feet up in the Andes and explained shade trees and hand picking.
- **The first year:** the federation spent over 1 million dollars and took Juan Valdez onto television, picking beans and leading his mule down the mountain.
- **Five months in:** the number of consumers who named Colombian coffee as the world's finest had risen 300 percent.
- **1962 and 1963:** the campaign went to Canada and Europe, and by the end of 1963 it was on national US television, with a son, Ramón.
- **1964:** General Foods switched its premium Yuban brand to 100 percent Colombian coffee. Within five years of Juan Valdez's creation, more than forty US brands and over twenty European roasters sold all-Colombian coffee, with the federation supplying free advertising support and the Juan Valdez logo for each can.

"For once," Pendergrast writes, "advertising hype matched reality." Most Colombian coffee really was grown on small mountainside farms, by some 200,000 families at the time, and the beans often did start their trip down the mountain by mule. By 2004, WIPO reports, surveys found 91 percent or more of consumers in the US, Canada and Spain knew Colombia as a coffee-growing country.

## The three men who played him

- **José F. Duval,** a Cuban actor, played Juan Valdez from the start until 1969, according to UPI and *La República*. Pendergrast names him as the first.
- **Carlos Sánchez** took over in 1969 and held the role for 37 years, until 2006. UPI reported that he grew coffee as a young man before becoming a painter and actor; he died on December 29, 2018, at 83, and the FNC confirmed his death.
- **Carlos Castañeda,** a coffee grower from Andes, in Antioquia, was presented as the new Juan Valdez on June 29, 2006, after a selection process that drew more than 380 applicants, *La República* reports. The FNC announced his death on April 26, 2024.

After Castañeda's death, the FNC said it would look for a fourth Juan Valdez through an international agency (*La Patria*, July 2024). By June 2025 its manager, Germán Bahamón, said the federation hadn't decided whether to cast anyone at all (*La Patria*). We found no later announcement when we checked on October 18, 2026.

## The logo and "100% Colombian"

The character on the can became a certification. WIPO reports that the FNC registered the word mark "100% Colombian coffee" in 1969, and that in the early 1980s it designed and registered the Juan Valdez logo, the farmer and his mule, to license to roasters whose coffee was entirely Colombian and met the federation's quality standards.

The program still works that way. The FNC's Café de Colombia site says the logo on a bag guarantees that only 100 percent Colombian coffee is inside. A roaster registers, signs a one-year license contract, gets its packaging approved, and sends samples during the year so the FNC can check the coffee's quality and origin. The logo says where a coffee came from; it doesn't name a region, a farm or a variety. Hoffmann adds that the FNC's Supremo and Excelso grades describe only bean size, not quality.

## Protection in Europe, 2007

The FNC took the name further. WIPO reports that it applied for European protection in 2005, the first such application from a country outside the EU, and the European Commission registered "Café de Colombia" as a Protected Geographical Indication by Commission Regulation (EC) No 1050/2007 of September 12, 2007. The regulation took effect on the twentieth day after its publication on September 13, which is October 3, 2007 (calculated), so both September and October dates appear in summaries.

## Juan Valdez Café, 2002 to today

In 2002 the brand moved from the can to the café. Procafecol S.A., the FNC's company for its value-added businesses, opened the first Juan Valdez store at Bogotá's El Dorado airport in December 2002; its own timeline gives December 14, and the FNC's glossary December 12. From its timeline:

- **2005:** the first store outside Colombia, in Washington, D.C.
- **2009:** 147 stores, and packaged coffee in more than 2,340 supermarkets abroad.
- **2014:** the 200th store in Colombia.
- **2024:** 630 stores worldwide and a presence in 40 international markets; elsewhere on the same page it counts more than 370 stores in Colombia.
- **2025:** the first store in Brazil, and the brand's first large global campaign.

The FNC owns 94.29 percent of Procafecol; Almacafé S.A. holds 2.18 percent, the Fundación Manuel Mejía 0.39 percent, and coffee growers and other shareholders 3.14 percent (Procafecol's shareholder page, checked October 18, 2026). Procafecol says it returns value to growers by paying a premium for quality, by promoting Colombian coffee, and through the National Coffee Fund, which pays for the FNC's public programs.
