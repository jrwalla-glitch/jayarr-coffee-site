A coffee menu can read like a foreign language, partly because it is one: most of the names are Italian or Spanish, and the same name can mean different drinks in different places. This guide translates the menu, with one published recipe for each drink so you know roughly what will arrive, then covers size, strength, milk and temperature.

## The menu, decoded

Each recipe below is one published version, not a universal definition. Cafés vary, sometimes a lot.

| On the menu | What it is | One published recipe |
|---|---|---|
| Espresso | A small, strong coffee brewed under pressure, with crema | Italy's INEI: about 25&nbsp;ml from 7&nbsp;g of coffee |
| Doppio | A double espresso | illy: 50&nbsp;ml |
| Ristretto | A shorter, more concentrated shot | Breville: 15–20&nbsp;ml |
| Lungo | A longer shot, with more water run through the coffee | CoffeeGeek: 45–50&nbsp;ml |
| Americano | Espresso with hot water added | Lavazza: a shot plus 120–180&nbsp;ml of water |
| Long black | Water first, then the espresso on top | Lavazza: 100–120&nbsp;ml of water |
| Macchiato | Espresso "marked" with a little milk foam | illy: 20–25&nbsp;ml, foam from about 28&nbsp;ml of milk |
| Cortado | Espresso "cut" with a little warm milk | Café Sabora: about 30&nbsp;ml and 10&nbsp;ml of milk |
| Flat white | A small, strong milk drink with thin foam | La Cimbali: a double, 125–130&nbsp;ml of frothed milk |
| Cappuccino | Espresso and milk under a deep cap of foam | La Cimbali: a single, 125&nbsp;ml of frothed milk, about 1&nbsp;cm of foam |
| Latte | Espresso with the most milk and a thin layer of foam | Ineffable Coffee: a 40&nbsp;g double, 180–240&nbsp;g of milk |

## Espresso and its variations

Every milk drink above starts with espresso. James Hoffmann defines it in *The World Atlas of Coffee* as a small, strong drink made with finely ground coffee under high water pressure, with crema on top, and with about twice the coffee's weight in the cup. Our Brew Guide's double works that way: 18&nbsp;g of coffee to 36&nbsp;g of espresso in 25 to 30 seconds. The Italian Espresso National Institute's certified single is smaller, 7&nbsp;g of coffee for about 25&nbsp;ml in the cup.

A **ristretto** uses less water for the same coffee, so it's shorter and stronger; a **lungo** uses more, for a longer, weaker shot. Hoffmann finds most lungos taste bitter and ashy, though lighter roasts can make good ones. Our [ristretto and lungo guide](/blog/whats-different-about-ristretto-or-lungo-espresso-shots/) goes further.

An **americano** dilutes the espresso with hot water. Hoffmann's own method is to pour the water first, brew a double espresso on top, then scoop off the crema: it holds tiny pieces of ground coffee that add bitterness to a diluted drink. A **long black**, from Australia and New Zealand, is poured that way round, shot onto water.

## The milk drinks, roughly from least milk to most

**Macchiato.** The small one is an espresso marked with a dollop of foam. But Dunkin's Macchiato is milk topped with espresso, and Starbucks' Caramel Macchiato is a large, sweet milk drink. Our [macchiato guide](/blog/what-is-a-macchiato/) sorts out all three.

**Cortado.** Espresso cut with a little warm milk, from Spain. Café Sabora's common Spanish version is about 30&nbsp;ml of espresso with 10&nbsp;ml of milk; Starbucks' Cortado is three ristretto shots with steamed whole milk in an 8&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz cup. Our [cortado guide](/blog/what-is-a-cortado/) compares the recipes.

**Flat white.** Hoffmann describes it as a small, strong latte: usually a double ristretto or double espresso, topped up with hot milk to 150–175&nbsp;ml, with only a little foam. Whether it was invented in Australia or New Zealand is still argued, he writes, but it's undeniably from Australasia. See our [flat white vs latte](/blog/what-is-a-flat-white-latte-vs-flat-white-comparison/) comparison.

**Cappuccino.** A deep layer of dense, creamy foam over espresso and milk. Hoffmann calls the "rule of thirds" (equal parts espresso, milk and foam) a myth with no root in tradition; the drink with a long tradition in Italy is 150–175&nbsp;ml, made with a single shot. You can usually ask for more or less foam: Starbucks' app, for one, offers Light Foam and Extra Foam on its milk drinks. A "dry" cappuccino has more foam and a "wet" one more milk; our [bone-dry cappuccino guide](/blog/whats-a-bone-dry-cappuccino/) explains the terms.

**Latte.** Typically more liquid milk than a cappuccino and less foam, which makes it the mildest. Hoffmann always says *caffè* latte, because ordering a "latte" in Italy gets you a glass of milk. In Spain, a *café con leche* is a little smaller: Ineffable Coffee puts it at 40&nbsp;g of espresso with 130 to 180&nbsp;g of milk, against 180 to 240&nbsp;g for its latte.

## Size and strength: count the shots

A bigger cup doesn't always mean more coffee. Starbucks' Caffè Latte, for example, comes in four sizes:

| Size | Cup | Espresso shots |
|---|---|---|
| Short | 8&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz | 1 |
| Tall | 12&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz | 1 |
| Grande | 16&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz | 2 |
| Venti | 20&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz | 2 |

So a Venti latte has the same two shots as a Grande, with more milk. If you want a stronger drink, ask for an extra shot rather than a bigger size, or order a smaller drink with less milk, such as a flat white or cortado.

The caffeine comes from the shots. Our Caffeine Calculator puts a single shot at about 60–75&nbsp;mg and a double at about 120–150&nbsp;mg. An 8&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz cup of drip coffee is about 105–195&nbsp;mg by its estimate: more than a single shot, and overlapping a double. Espresso is more concentrated; drip comes in a bigger cup. Our [espresso vs drip guide](/blog/espresso-vs-drip-coffee/) explains why.

**Decaf and half-caf.** Decaf isn't caffeine-free, just much lower; our [decaf guide](/blog/how-is-coffee-decaffeinated/) gives the FDA's figures. Half-caf means some of the shots are decaf: Starbucks, for one, offers a half, a third or two-thirds decaf.

## Choosing your milk

Most cafés offer dairy and several plant milks. Starbucks' menu, for example, lists whole, 2% and nonfat milk, breve (half-and-half), heavy cream, and oat, soy, almond and coconut milks, with 2% as the latte's default. In October 2024, Starbucks announced that it would stop charging extra for non-dairy milks at its company-operated stores in the US and Canada from November 7 that year, and said a non-dairy swap was its customers' second most requested change, after an extra shot. Other cafés set their own prices, so check the menu.

For a hot, foamy drink, a café's barista plant milk is usually the one made for steaming. Our [plant milk guide](/blog/best-plant-milks-for-coffee/) compares soy, pea, almond, oat and others, including why soy can curdle in hot coffee, and our [oat milk vs dairy](/blog/oat-milk-vs-dairy-in-coffee/) comparison covers the most common swap.

## How hot

It's fine to ask for a drink "warm" or "not too hot." Starbucks' app even lists the options: Warm, Steamed Hot and Extra Hot. There's a flavor case for cooler milk, too: Hoffmann finds that a very hot cappuccino lacks the texture, flavor and sweetness of one made with milk heated only to 140°F&nbsp;(60°C), and says milk begins to degrade in flavor and texture above 154°F&nbsp;(68°C). Breville's home-machine manual puts steamed milk at 140–149°F&nbsp;(60–65°C); Scott Rao's café target in *The Professional Barista's Handbook* is a little hotter, 150–160°F&nbsp;(66–71°C). Our [milk steaming guide](/blog/how-to-steam-milk-for-lattes/) explains the difference.

## Iced drinks and cold brew

Most espresso drinks can be made iced: an iced latte is the same espresso and milk, poured over ice. Cold brew is a different drink: coffee steeped in cold water for hours (12 to 18 in our Brew Guide), then strained. It's often called less acidic, and the studies say that depends on what you measure; our [Japanese iced coffee vs cold brew](/blog/japanese-iced-coffee-vs-cold-brew/) comparison has the numbers.

## Drip and pour-over

Not everything on the menu is espresso. **Batch brew** or drip coffee is brewed in a large brewer and served by the cup. **Pour-over** is brewed by hand, one cup at a time, in a dripper such as a V60, and takes a few minutes. Our [V60 guide](/blog/ultimate-pour-over-coffee-guide-and-best-v60-technique/) shows how it's made.

You may also see **single origin** on the board: coffee from one place, where a blend mixes beans from several. Treat the origin as a starting clue to the taste, not a guarantee; our [single-origin guide](/blog/what-is-single-origin-coffee/) explains what the label can tell you. And if a café calls itself **third wave**, that's a name, not a standard: see our [third wave history](/blog/third-wave-coffee-history-movement/).

## How to actually order

**Start with the drink.** "Can I get a cortado?" or "A medium latte, please" is all you need.

**Then add the changes.** "With oat milk," "iced," "an extra shot," "warm, not too hot."

**Ask about anything that matters to you.** How many shots in this size? How big is your cortado? Is the macchiato the small one? Asking is the quickest way to get the drink you meant.

**Don't worry about the words.** Plain words like "small" and "medium" are fine even where the sizes have their own names. If you'd like sugar or syrup, ask for it: it's your drink.

## Three orders to start with

1. **A latte.** Familiar and mild, with the espresso softened by plenty of milk.
2. **A flat white or a cortado.** Less milk, so more of the coffee's own flavor comes through.
3. **A pour-over or the day's drip, black.** If the café has a single origin on offer, this is the way to taste it on its own.
