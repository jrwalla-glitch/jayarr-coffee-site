Dialing in means finding the grind, dose and yield that make the coffee in front of you taste good on your machine. Each new bag needs it, and the same bag can drift as it ages. The method is simple: hold everything steady, change one thing, taste, and write down what happened. If you're new to how espresso works, start with [what espresso actually is](/blog/what-espresso-actually-is/).

## What you need

- **A grinder that can grind fine enough for espresso,** in small steps. Rao advises putting the grinder ahead of the machine when you budget.
- **A scale that reads to 0.1 g.** In Rao's experience, a dose that varies by just 1 g can change a shot's time by several seconds.
- **A timer.** Most espresso scales have one.
- **A tamper that fits the basket.** Rao recommends one 0.25 to 0.30 mm smaller than the basket's inside diameter, such as a 58.4 mm tamper in a 58.7 mm basket. A loose tamper lets water channel down the side, which he found can cost about half a percentage point of extraction.
- **Somewhere to write.** A notebook or a notes app is enough.

## The starting recipe

Start at 18 g of coffee in and 36 g of espresso out, a 1:2 ratio, and expect the shot to take about 25 to 30 seconds, timed from the moment you start the pump. That's our Brew Guide's espresso default. It sits inside Scott Rao's ranges for a *normale* (14 to 22 g, a shot 1.5 to 2 times the dose, 25 to 40 seconds) and matches the example recipe in James Hoffmann's *World Atlas of Coffee*: 18 g to about 36 g in 27 to 29 seconds. Use the dose your basket is designed for; Rao suggests staying within about 10% of it.

- **Coffee:** 18 g, ground fine, about the texture of powdered sugar. The Brew Guide's illustrative band is about 200 to 300&nbsp;µm, but a size only becomes a setting on a particular grinder, so start where your grinder's maker suggests for espresso.
- **Water temperature, if your machine lets you set it:** the Brew Guide starts at 199°F&nbsp;(93°C) for a medium roast, 203°F&nbsp;(95°C) for a light roast and 196°F&nbsp;(91°C) for a dark one. All three sit inside Rao's range of 196–203°F&nbsp;(91–95°C) at the group head. If your machine doesn't let you set the temperature, that's fine; dial in with the grind.
- **Makes:** one double shot, 36 g. We give it by weight only, because crema changes the volume: Rao found the same 30 g shot can fill almost twice the volume when the beans are very fresh.
- **Time:** about 2 minutes to grind, dose and tamp, then 25 to 30 seconds for the shot; about 2½ minutes in all.

## Keep a shot log

Rao recommends that cafés keep a running record of the dose, the shot weight, the time and tasting notes (plus refractometer readings, if they have one), because a trend shows what a single shot can't. He also advises against changing the grind because of a single odd shot when the dose might have been different; change it when several shots trend the same way.

A log only needs six columns. Here's what one might look like for a new bag; the numbers are illustrative.

| Shot | In | Out | Time | Taste | Next change |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 18.0&nbsp;g | 36&nbsp;g | 21&nbsp;s | Sour, thin | Grind one step finer |
| 2 | 18.0&nbsp;g | 36&nbsp;g | 24&nbsp;s | Less sour, still sharp | Grind one step finer |
| 3 | 18.0&nbsp;g | 36&nbsp;g | 27&nbsp;s | Sweeter, a little sour | Pull to 45&nbsp;g |
| 4 | 18.0&nbsp;g | 45&nbsp;g | 31&nbsp;s | Balanced | Keep and save |

A longer yield takes longer to pour, so shot 4's 31 seconds isn't a slow shot: the 25- to 30-second target belongs to the 36 g recipe.

## Read the shot, then change one thing

On this recipe, the times below are common signs, not rules: the same time can taste right with one coffee and wrong with another. Read the time together with the taste, and change one thing per shot. Hoffmann's advice is to try the grind first, because if the grind is wrong, nothing else will fix the shot.

| What you taste | Shot time | One change to try |
|---|---|---|
| Sour, thin | Under 25&nbsp;s | Grind one small step finer |
| Sour | 25–&#8288;30&nbsp;s | Pull a longer shot: 45&nbsp;g instead of 36&nbsp;g (1:2.5) |
| Bitter, harsh, drying | Over 30&nbsp;s | Grind one small step coarser |
| Bitter | 25–&#8288;30&nbsp;s | Pull a shorter shot: 27&nbsp;g instead of 36&nbsp;g (1:1.5) |
| Bitter, but the shot ran fast | Under 25&nbsp;s | Grind finer first; a fast shot is usually under-extracted, so the bitterness may come from the roast or from channeling |
| Sour and bitter at once | Any | Keep the recipe and fix the puck: break up clumps, level the grounds, tamp straight |
| Too strong, otherwise balanced | 25–&#8288;30&nbsp;s | Pull a longer ratio, 1:2.5 |
| Balanced and sweet | 25–&#8288;30&nbsp;s | Keep it, and save the recipe |

The yields in the table are calculated from 18 g: 18 × 2.5 = 45 g and 18 × 1.5 = 27 g. If two steps finer still leave the shot running fast, add 0.5 g to the dose as a separate change; if it's still slow after you go coarser, take 0.5 g off.

Change the temperature last, and only if your machine lets you. For a light roast that stays sour once the time and the longer yield are right, try a couple of degrees hotter; for a dark roast that stays bitter on a shorter shot, a couple of degrees cooler.

## Why the grind comes first

You've fixed the dose on the scale, so when the shot runs too fast or too slow, the grind is the thing that's off. Rao advises adjusting in small steps, rarely more than one notch at a time. On a grinder with a chute between the burrs and the basket, he suggests using or discarding the first 5 g or so before judging a new setting, since old grounds sit in the chute.

Taste can mislead, though. Rao points out that extraction changes taste unevenly: one step finer can taste slightly worse before the next step tastes clearly better. That's one reason to keep the log and look for a trend, and why he recommends a refractometer to anyone who wants to measure extraction rather than guess it.

## Puck prep, briefly

- **Break up clumps.** Rao found that grinders that dose straight into the portafilter tend to leave clumps, and clumps make the bed unevenly dense. A WDT tool, a few fine needles stirred through the grounds, is one way to break them up; see our [WDT guide](/blog/best-wdt-tool-espresso-distribution/).
- **Tamp once, level.** Rao tamps with 20 to 30 lb (9 to 14 kg) and sees no need for a twist or a second tamp. Cameron and colleagues tried a range of tamping pressures and saw no appreciable change in shot time or extraction. Level matters more than force; our [tamping guide](/blog/tamping-pressure-doesnt-matter-myth/) explains why.
- **Don't tap the portafilter.** Rao and Hoffmann both warn that a tap on the side can break the seal between the coffee and the basket wall and leave a path for the water around the edge.
- **Watch the flow.** Coffee that spurts, or that runs or turns pale faster from one part of the basket (easiest to see with a bottomless portafilter), is a sign of [channeling](/blog/fix-channeling-uneven-extraction/).

## Preinfusion, if your machine has it

Some machines wet the puck at low pressure for a few seconds before full pressure. Rao's view is that preinfusion won't necessarily improve your best shot, but it makes great shots much more frequent: it reduces channeling, limits fines migration and lets you grind finer. With preinfusion you'll usually need a finer grind for the same flow, and Rao expects shots with a progressive preinfusion to run toward the middle of his 25- to 40-second range. So on a machine that preinfuses, a shot a little over 30 seconds may still be on target; let the taste decide. Our [pressure profiling guide](/blog/pressure-profiling-espresso-guide/) covers it in more detail.

## Fresh beans and crema

Very fresh beans release a lot of carbon dioxide during the shot. Rao explains that the gas pushes back against the water, so you end up grinding coarser and extracting less; it's one reason baristas rest beans for several days before using them for espresso. Our [bean-resting guide](/blog/bean-resting-for-espresso-freshness-trap/) goes into it.

Crema is not a reliable guide to a good shot. Hoffmann notes it tells you roughly how fresh the coffee is and how strong the shot is, and nothing about whether it tastes good.

## When it won't dial in

- **The grinder can't go fine enough.** If the finest setting still gushes, the grinder isn't built for espresso. Pre-ground coffee is ground for an average brewer and stales quickly, so it rarely dials in well.
- **Dull burrs.** Rao found that dull burrs make more fines and more large pieces at once, which slows the shot and lowers extraction.
- **The dose wanders.** Weigh every dose while you dial in.
- **The machine isn't fully warm.** Follow your machine's manual for heat-up time before the first shot.

## If you have a refractometer

A refractometer turns taste guesses into a number. Rao finds most espresso shots taste best at about 19 to 20% extraction with typical grinders, though large, sharp burrs can go higher and a bright, fruit-forward shot may sit lower. There's also a different approach to espresso entirely, with less coffee, a much coarser grind and a fast shot; see [turbo shots](/blog/turbo-shots-fast-espresso-science/).
