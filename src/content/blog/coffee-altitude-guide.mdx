When a specialty bag says "grown at 1,800 meters," it's telling you something real. James Hoffmann, in *The World Atlas of Coffee*, puts it bluntly: "Great coffee requires altitude," and he notes that many farms sit on steep mountain slopes. But altitude alone doesn't tell the whole story. A farm at 1,500 m in Colombia and one at 1,500 m in southern Brazil grow coffee in quite different conditions, and the variety, the processing and the weather all count too.

Here's what altitude does, what the research has found, how the grades work, and how to use the number on a bag.

## Why altitude matters

The mechanism starts with temperature. Air cools as you climb, normally by about 6.5 °C for every 1,000 m in the lower atmosphere, though the rate near the ground varies with weather and terrain. Cooler conditions slow the cherries down. Daniel Peterson of Hacienda La Esmeralda, the Panamanian farm that made Geisha famous, told Jeff Koehler that good attributes "seem to pronounce themselves as you go higher and cooler—higher acidity, more aromatics," and Koehler explains that cool nights slow the cherries' maturation.

Research on Réunion Island points the same way. Comparing 16 plots across the island's range of climates, Benoît Bertrand and colleagues (2012) found that the mean air temperature while the beans developed strongly shaped the coffee's sensory profile: acidity, fruity character and flavor quality went with cool climates, while warm, sunny plots produced more green and earthy defects.

There are limits. Koehler notes that growing too high brings its own risks: ultraviolet light can burn the leaves, wind and cold can bring disease, and frost kills the trees. Hoffmann notes that in Kenya's Nakuru region, some trees suffer dieback at high altitude and stop producing.

## What the studies found

These studies looked at altitude and coffee quality directly, each in one region with its own sample.

| Study | Where | Sample | What altitude did |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decazy and colleagues, 2003 | Honduras | 52 plots in 6 producing regions | High altitudes and annual rainfall under 1,500 mm favored sensory quality |
| Avelino and colleagues, 2005 | Costa Rica: Orosi and Santa María de Dota | Farms at 1,020–1,250 m and 1,550–1,780 m | Tasters' preference rose with altitude in both areas; at Dota, a chocolate taste grew stronger higher up |
| Tolessa and colleagues, 2017 | Ethiopia | Specialty coffee from mid and high altitudes, under different shade and picking times | High altitude, with open or medium shade and early-to-middle picking, gave the best quality; going about 400 m higher cut caffeine by 10% |
| Worku and colleagues, 2018 | Southwestern Ethiopia | Large plantations | Sucrose rose with altitude in washed coffee; acidity rose with altitude only under shade |
| Tirfe and colleagues, 2015 | Southwestern Ethiopia | 7 varieties at 3 research stations, 1,550–1,940 m | The highest station gave the best cups, the lowest the weakest |

Three things stand out. First, the direction is consistent: higher, or cooler, tended to mean better. Second, the effects were often modest and conditional. Tolessa's team found the changes in quality scores driven by altitude, shade and harvest time were small, though they could still move a large share of coffee from the 80–84.75 grade into 85-plus; Worku's team found that altitude raised acidity only for coffee grown under shade. Third, other things mattered as much in the same studies: in Avelino's, east-facing slopes gave more acidic coffees, probably because they catch the morning sun, and at Dota heavier crops gave less acidity; in Decazy's, rainfall mattered alongside altitude, and the best coffees came from Olancho and El Paraíso.

So treat altitude as a strong clue, not a rule.

## The latitude-altitude interaction

Here's where most altitude discussions fall short: a farm's height means little without its latitude. Temperature drops with altitude and also with distance from the equator, so a farm far from the equator can reach the same cool conditions lower down.

World Coffee Research (WCR) builds this into its variety catalog. For each variety it gives an optimal altitude, the height at which "quality and agronomic performance potential is maximized," and it scales the bands by latitude:

| Latitude | Low | Medium | High |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5°N to 5°S | 1,000–1,200 m | 1,200–1,600 m | Above 1,600 m |
| 5° to 15° N or S | 700–900 m | 900–1,300 m | Above 1,300 m |
| Beyond 15° N or S | 400–700 m | 700–1,000 m | Above 1,000 m |

These are WCR's bands for matching a variety to a farm, not quality grades, but they show the principle: "high" starts about 600 m lower beyond 15° than near the equator. Here are some well-known regions, with Hoffmann's altitudes and their latitudes:

| Region | Latitude | Altitude (Hoffmann) | WCR's "high" there |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nyeri, Kenya | 0.4°S | 1,200–2,300 m | Above 1,600 m |
| Huila, Colombia | 1.9°N | 1,250–2,000 m | Above 1,600 m |
| Yirgacheffe, Ethiopia | 6.2°N | 1,750–2,200 m | Above 1,300 m |
| Tarrazú, Costa Rica | 9.7°N | 1,200–1,900 m | Above 1,300 m |
| Montecillos, Honduras | 14.2°N | 1,200–1,600 m | Above 1,300 m |
| Huehuetenango, Guatemala | 15.3°N | 1,500–2,000 m | Above 1,000 m |
| Cerrado Mineiro, Brazil | 18.9°S | 850–1,250 m | Above 1,000 m |
| Kona, Hawaii | 19.6°N | 150–900 m | Above 1,000 m |

The latitudes are for a town in each region, rounded from GeoNames; Huehuetenango sits right at the edge of two bands. Brazil's Cerrado reaches WCR's high band at elevations that would count as low or medium near the equator, and Yirgacheffe, at about 6°N, falls in WCR's 5–15° band, so its high band starts lower than Nyeri's or Huila's. Kona is the outlier: it's below WCR's high band for its latitude, yet Hoffmann calls it one of the best-known growing regions in the world. ## Altitude grades: SHB and SHG

Several Central American countries grade coffee by the altitude it was grown at, and the cutoffs aren't the same from country to country. These are the definitions in Hoffmann's country chapters:

| Country | Top grade | Next grades down |
|---|---|---|
| Guatemala | Strictly Hard Bean (SHB): above 1,300 m (4,300 ft) | Hard Bean, 1,220–1,300 m; Semi Hard Bean, 1,050–1,220 m; Extra Prime, 900–1,050 m; Prime, 750–900 m |
| El Salvador | Strictly High Grown (SHG): above 1,200 m (3,900 ft) | High Grown, above 900 m; Central Standard, above 600 m |
| Honduras | Strictly High Grown (SHG): above 1,200 m (3,900 ft) | High Grown, above 1,000 m |

So "SHB" on a Guatemalan bag and "SHG" on a Salvadoran one don't mean the same height. If a seller uses the initials, check which country's definition they mean.

Hoffmann adds two warnings. In El Salvador, he writes, the grades have no relation to quality or traceability. In Honduras, there's some correlation between altitude and quality, but the grades are most often used to market less traceable lots. A specific altitude and a named farm or region tell you more than a grade. Our [guide to reading a coffee bag](/blog/how-to-read-coffee-bag-label/) covers the rest of the label.

## Altitude isn't the only thing that matters

**Processing.** How the cherry is handled after picking changes the cup dramatically. A natural-process coffee picks up fruit and fermentation notes that have nothing to do with altitude, and a mill's decisions can lift or ruin what the farm produced. Our [processing methods guide](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/) explains each method.

**Variety.** WCR rates every variety's quality potential at high altitude, and the ratings differ a lot. Panama's Geisha is rated "Exceptional"; IHCAFE 90, a high-yielding variety WCR describes as adapted to the lowest altitudes, is rated "Very Low." The plant sets what's possible; altitude and processing decide how much of it reaches the cup. Our [coffee varietal guide](/blog/coffee-varietal-guide/) gives the ratings.

**Shade, slope and harvest.** In the studies above, shade changed how altitude worked, east-facing slopes beat others, and early picking beat late picking.

**Roasting.** Hoffmann writes that a good roaster can bring out a coffee's sweetness in different ways, but that adjusting a roast "can never improve a poor-quality coffee." Our guide to [how coffee is roasted](/blog/how-is-coffee-roasted/) covers the process.

## Climate change and altitude

As the climate warms, the land suitable for coffee is projected to move uphill. Models project the biggest losses at low latitudes and low altitudes, and researchers warn that new coffee land higher up may come from clearing forest. Our research note on [coffee and climate change](/deep-brew/research/coffee-climate-change-data/) sets out the data, region by region, with each figure dated.

## Practical takeaways

**When shopping:** look for altitude in context. A specific figure, such as 1,650 masl (meters above sea level), tells you more than "high-grown," and it's most useful next to the country, the region and the process. Compare it with that country's growing band in Coffee Origins, since what counts as high depends on latitude.

**When brewing:** Hoffmann notes, writing about density and grind size, that if you switch from a coffee grown much lower, such as a Brazil, to one grown much higher, such as a Kenya, you may need to grind a little finer. Change one step at a time and taste. Our [grind size guide](/blog/coffee-grind-size-guide-what-grind-for-every-brew-method/) and [brewing temperature guide](/blog/whats-the-ideal-coffee-brewing-temperature/) cover the rest.

**When exploring:** use altitude as a rough map. If you like bright, complex coffees, try lots grown high for their latitude. If you prefer heavier, lower-acid cups, lower-grown coffees may suit you better: Hoffmann writes that better Brazilian coffees tend to be low in acidity, heavy in body and sweet. Then let your palate decide.
