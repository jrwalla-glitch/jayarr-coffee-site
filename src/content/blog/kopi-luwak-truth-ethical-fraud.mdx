Kopi luwak's selling story is a wild civet choosing the ripest cherries at night, and a farmer gathering the beans from the forest floor. Some kopi luwak may still be made that way. The trouble is that a label alone doesn't show it, and the studies of civet coffee plantations describe something else.

## What kopi luwak is

James Hoffmann describes it in *The World Atlas of Coffee*: kopi luwak refers to "coffees that are produced by collecting the droppings of civet cats that have eaten coffee cherries." The beans are separated from the droppings, then processed and dried. The animal is the Asian palm civet, *Paradoxurus hermaphroditus*.

Digestion does change the beans. Massimo Marcone's 2004 study, which describes itself as the first scientific investigation of the beans' properties, found surface pitting from gastric juices and digestive enzymes, and found that proteolytic enzymes had broken down storage proteins inside the beans. He linked that breakdown to differences in the browning reactions of roasting, and so in flavor and aroma, and an electronic nose could tell the civet coffees from control beans. Different, in that study, doesn't mean better; it means changed.

## What the welfare studies found

The most detailed evidence comes from civet coffee tourism plantations in Bali, where visitors watch caged civets and buy the coffee.

- **Carder and colleagues (2016),** in the journal *Animal Welfare*, assessed 48 wild-caught common palm civets at 16 plantations. They scored eight aspects of how the animals were kept, from mobility, hygiene and cage surfaces to shelter, noise, food, water and social contact, and interviewed a senior tour guide at each. They concluded that caged commercial production left the civets in inadequate conditions, and contrasted it with the traditional collection of droppings from wild civets. Reporting on the study, National Geographic said every plantation the researchers visited "failed basic animal welfare requirements," with some civets underweight on a diet of coffee cherries, others obese from lack of movement, and many without clean water.
- **Lewis-Whelan and colleagues (2024),** in the *Journal of Applied Animal Welfare Science*, assessed 29 plantations and 99 civets, and eight wildlife markets holding 136 more, which supply the plantations. They estimate that 400 to 800 civets are sold each year. Welfare standards didn't meet Indonesian laws and regulations, and "no civet met all of the five freedoms."

Journalists have reported the same pattern beyond Bali. In September 2013, a BBC undercover investigation said it had found coffee sold in Britain as "wild" that came from civets kept in battery-cage conditions in Sumatra, according to Agence France-Presse's report at the time. The founder of the Indonesian Kopi Luwak Association disputed that mislabeling was common, and argued that caged production is acceptable if it meets minimum standards and is sold as cage coffee, not wild.

These studies describe the plantations and markets they visited. They don't measure every producer, and the collection of droppings from truly wild civets isn't what they found fault with.

## Can you buy it ethically?

Not with any confidence. National Geographic reported in 2016 that "there's now no way to tell whether a bag of kopi luwak was made from wild or caged civets," and that "no certification scheme exists to ensure that coffee labeled 'wild' is actually that." The sources reviewed here don't establish an independently verified route for wild sourcing today, so a "wild" label alone rests on the seller's word. Lab tests can show whether beans passed through a civet, not whether it was caged.

For how the coffee certifications that do exist work, and what each one checks, see our [coffee certifications guide](/blog/coffee-certifications-decoded/).

## Fakes and blends

Authenticity is the second problem. Hoffmann writes that "the forgery of this coffee is quite commonplace. Several times more is sold than produced, and often low-grade Robusta is being passed off at high prices." National Geographic cited a warning from Tony Wild, the trader it says introduced kopi luwak to the West, in the Guardian: that it "has become increasingly industrialized, abusive, and faked."

Scientists have responded by building tests, because, as one team put it in 2013, "there is no reliable, standardized method for determining its authenticity."

- **Marcone (2004)** suggested protein analysis by SDS-PAGE as the most reliable way to confirm civet coffee, since digestion breaks down the beans' storage proteins.
- **Jumhawan and colleagues (2013),** in the *Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry*, profiled 21 coffee bean samples and picked out citric acid, malic acid and the ratio of inositol to pyroglutamic acid as markers. Tested on commercial products, the markers showed potential to tell original kopi luwak apart from fake kopi luwak, regular coffee and a blend containing 50 percent kopi luwak.
- **The same group (2015 and 2016)** showed a cheaper instrument could reproduce the screening across 37 samples, and that its model could estimate how much civet coffee is in a blend, a "proof of concept" prompted by concern about the "illegal mixture of cheap coffee into civet coffee."

These studies show that fakes and blends can be detected in a lab. They weren't surveys of what's on store shelves, so they don't say what share of bags is fake. No study we found has measured that, so treat any precise figure with suspicion.

## What it tastes like

Accounts disagree. Mark Pendergrast, the coffee historian, tried a cup and found it full-bodied, with "an unusual taste—earthy? pungent? gutsy?" that lingered, but wrote that he wouldn't pay the price for the beans. A chapter on Indonesia in *Coffee: A Comprehensive Guide* describes kopi luwak as having "balanced body, high acidity, and some bitterness, although the characteristics depend on the coffee's nature and handling." Hoffmann is blunter: "If you are interested in delicious coffee then it is a terrible waste of money."

Part of the disagreement may be that kopi luwak isn't one coffee. The same chapter notes that civets eat both arabica and robusta cherries, so two bags can start from very different beans, before any question of fakes.

## What to buy instead

Hoffmann's own suggestion is the practical one: "One-quarter of the money you might spend on a bag could instead buy you a stunning coffee from one of the very best producers in the world." He goes further: "I believe people should avoid all animal-processed coffees," which takes in any coffee processed by an animal, not only Indonesia's.

If what appeals is a coffee that tastes unlike any other, look at the varieties and processing that produce unusual cups on purpose: [Panama's Geisha](/blog/panama-coffee-guide-geisha-boquete/), and fermentation methods such as anaerobic and carbonic maceration in our [processing methods guide](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/). If it's Indonesia itself, Hoffmann's advice on the same page is that its traceable, fully washed coffees "are definitely worth trying."
