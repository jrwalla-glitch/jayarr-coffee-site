The label on your coffee bag says "washed" or "natural" or "anaerobic carbonic maceration", and you're supposed to know what that means. Processing is everything that happens between picking a ripe cherry and shipping a dry green bean. The cherry is a fruit, with a skin, a sweet and sticky flesh, a papery parchment and a thin silver skin around two seeds; our [cherry anatomy guide](/blog/coffee-cherry-anatomy-explained/) shows each layer. How and when those layers come off, and what's allowed to ferment along the way, changes the coffee.

It's worth knowing why most producers choose a method. James Hoffmann points out in *The World Atlas of Coffee* that for most of them the goal isn't a particular flavor: it's to avoid defects and protect the coffee's value. Cost, water and climate decide a lot. A small but growing number of skilled producers do steer processing for flavor, and those are the coffees with the long processing notes.

## Washed

**The process:** a depulper strips off the skin and most of the flesh. The coffee then goes into a tank or trough, where fermentation breaks down the rest of the flesh, which is full of pectin and clings to the seed, until it can be washed away. Then it's dried.

How long fermentation takes depends on altitude and temperature: the hotter it is, the faster it goes. Hoffmann describes two checks producers use: rubbed coffee squeaks once the flesh has broken down, and a stick stands up in the tank's gelatinous water. Left too long, the coffee picks up off-flavors.

**What it tends to do:** in Hoffmann's account, washed coffees tend toward higher acidity, more complexity and a "cleaner" cup, meaning one without off-tastes or harshness. That's why many specialty producers wash their coffee: removing the flesh before drying sharply reduces the chance of something going wrong.

**The cost:** it's the most expensive method, it uses water, and the wastewater can be toxic, which raises environmental concerns.

## Natural

**The process:** the oldest method. Whole cherries are spread out to dry in the sun on patios or raised beds and turned regularly to prevent mold, unwanted fermentation and rot. Once dry, the husk of skin and dried fruit is removed by machine.

**What it tends to do:** Hoffmann writes that the natural process often adds fruit flavors, usually described as blueberry, strawberry or tropical fruit, whatever the variety or place, and sometimes wilder notes such as ferment or barnyard. Carefully made naturals divide the coffee world: some love how fruity they can be, others find the wild flavors unpleasant.

**Where you'll see it:** where water is scarce it may be the only option, which is why Hoffmann names Ethiopia and parts of Brazil. Much natural coffee is cheap and low quality, but high-quality naturals cost more to make, because careful drying takes more labor.

## Honey and pulped natural

**The process:** the skin and some of the flesh are stripped off by machine, and the coffee goes straight out to dry with the rest of its sticky mucilage still on.

- **Pulped natural** is mainly Brazilian. Hoffmann says it came from experiments by the equipment maker Pinhalense, aiming for quality with less water than washing. With less fruit around the bean there's less risk of defects, but enough sugar remains for a noticeable increase in sweetness and body.
- **Honey** (*miel*, the Spanish word for the mucilage) is very similar and used in Central America, including Costa Rica and El Salvador. Depulpers can be set to leave a chosen share of the flesh on, so a coffee might be called "100 percent honey" or "20 percent honey". The more flesh left on, the higher the risk of fermentation defects in drying.

**The colors:** yellow, red and black honey aren't a standard measure. The importer Café Imports reports that some mills set the color by how much mucilage they remove before drying, while others leave all of it on and change the drying instead. Our [honey process guide](/blog/honey-process-colors-explained/) and our [Costa Rica guide](/blog/what-does-costa-rican-coffee-taste-like/) go further.

## Wet-hulled (giling basah)

**The process:** common in Indonesia. The coffee is depulped and dried only to 30 to 35 percent moisture instead of 11 to 12. Then it's hulled out of its parchment while still soft, and the bare beans are dried again. It's the one common method that doesn't keep the parchment on until just before export, and the second drying turns the beans what Hoffmann calls a deep swamp-green.

**What it tends to do:** lower acidity and more body, with flavors such as wood, earth, mustiness, spice, tobacco and leather. Hoffmann notes strong disagreement over whether that's desirable: many feel the process hides how Indonesian coffees really taste, which is why fully washed Indonesian lots, labeled as such, are worth looking for. Our guide to [Indonesian coffee](/blog/what-does-indonesian-coffee-taste-like/) goes further.

## What fermentation does

Every method involves microbes feeding on the fruit's sugars. What they make depends on the conditions, and some of it ends up in the bean. In a 2017 case study, De Bruyn and colleagues followed arabica cherries through wet and dry processing. Lactic acid bacteria dominated the wet processing, and several of their products formed in the mucilage (lactic acid, acetic acid and mannitol) were also found inside the bean. In dry processing, acetic acid bacteria and yeasts dominated, and their products built up in the drying outer layers of the cherry. Both methods changed the composition of the finished green beans.

That's what the newer methods try to steer: fewer variables left to chance, and more control over which microbes win. Cacao beans are fermented too; our sister site explains <a href="https://jayarrchocolate.com/blog/cacao-fermentation-science/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">cacao fermentation</a>.

## Anaerobic fermentation

**What it is:** a fermentation *environment*, not a processing method of its own. The importer Café Imports defines it as coffee "fermented in a low-oxygen environment for some period of time", and stresses that anaerobic coffees "are still processed as washed, honey, or natural." An "anaerobic natural" is a natural with an anaerobic step.

**How it's done:** fresh coffee goes into a container that's then sealed: a stainless-steel or thick plastic tank, a tied-off plastic bag such as GrainPro, or, on a budget, a food-service barrel or pail with a tight lid. Carbon dioxide from the fruit builds up and pushes out the oxygen; purpose-built tanks often have one-way valves that let gas out but not back in. There's no fixed time. Café Imports says pH, Brix, temperature and visual or sensory cues are likely more important guides than the clock, and that practices vary widely: coffee may be pre-fermented for 24 hours first, and yeasts or other modifiers may be added to the tank.

**What it tends to do:** Café Imports says producers and buyers are seeing "unique flavor profiles", but that "it's difficult to empirically say what anaerobic fermentation does to the final cup", because so many other variables change at the same time.

## Carbonic maceration

**What it is:** a form of anaerobic processing borrowed from winemaking. Taya Brown, a coffee production researcher at Café Imports, explained to *Barista Magazine* that in coffee the term currently means whole cherries sealed in a container with carbon dioxide actively pumped in. The CO₂ pushes oxygen out and, she says, is thought to favor a different metabolism than open-air fermentation and may reduce oxidation of the fruit and seed.

**What it tends to do:** Brown says carbonic maceration coffees often have "intense flavor profiles, with boozy and cooked fruit flavors", though some are sweeter and some more acidic, and when it goes wrong they risk astringency, dryness and bitterness.

**The cost:** sealable tanks, one-way valves and CO₂ are an investment, and Brown points out that experimenting is hard for producers who can't cup their own results.

**Why you've heard of it:** Saša Šestić won the 2015 World Barista Championship in Seattle with Sudan Rume coffee, and Sprudge's report at the time describes part of his routine as a washed carbonic-maceration coffee.

## Thermal shock and co-fermentation

**Thermal shock** is a name roasters use for lots from producers such as Diego Bermúdez at Finca El Paraíso, in Cauca, Colombia. As one roaster's education director describes it, coffee is warmed in its own fermentation liquid near the end of fermentation, then rinsed in cold water; it's one step in a longer, intensive process. He adds that scientific research is still needed to work out where its flavor impact comes from. There's no standard recipe.

**Co-fermented** and **infused** coffees are fermented or flavored with added fruit or other ingredients, which can make a coffee taste of something that didn't come from the coffee. Competitions have pushed back. In 2024 the Specialty Coffee Association of Panama disqualified Best of Panama entries it said had undergone fruit infusions or co-fermentation; its president called the terms "co-fermented" and "thermal shock" deceptive and proposed a separate category for "altered or infused coffees".

## Reading processing on a bag

Treat processing as a direction, not a quality score:

- **Washed:** expect clarity and acidity, with the origin and variety easier to taste.
- **Natural:** expect fruit, and accept more variation from lot to lot.
- **Honey or pulped natural:** expect more sweetness and body than a washed coffee, with less wildness than many naturals.
- **Wet-hulled:** expect body and earthy, woody flavors rather than bright fruit.
- **Anaerobic or carbonic maceration:** look for the base method too. Both Café Imports and Brown suggest the honest way to describe these coffees is the method plus the step and its length, for example "a natural fermented in an anaerobic environment for 48 hours". A label that just says "anaerobic" leaves the rest out.
- **Co-fermented or infused:** some of the flavor may come from what was added.

The most useful experiment is to taste one origin processed two ways, a washed and a natural from the same country, side by side.
