Organic coffee is one of those things that sounds simple until you look closely. You see the label, you pay the premium, you feel good. But after spending a lot of time researching what that label actually guarantees, and what it doesn't, I've found that the reality is more interesting than the marketing suggests.

Our 2020 video predates this revision. It says organic farmers must grow their coffee in shade and that organic coffee can improve your health; neither holds up, as the sections below explain. Follow the text where they differ.

## What the USDA organic label requires

Coffee sold as organic in the US must be produced and handled under the USDA's National Organic Program rule, 7 CFR part 205. For coffee, the parts that matter most:

- **What can't be used:** synthetic substances, unless the rule's National List allows them; natural substances the rule prohibits; "excluded methods" such as genetic engineering; ionizing radiation; and sewage sludge (§205.105).
- **Three years clean:** no prohibited substances applied to the land for the three years before the harvest, with buffer zones against drift from neighboring farms (§205.202).
- **Natural resources:** the farm must maintain or improve the natural resources of the operation, which the rule defines to include wetlands, woodlands and wildlife (§§205.2 and 205.200).
- **Every handler certified:** each operation that produces or handles a product sold as organic must be certified, except the exempt operations the rule lists (§205.100). Its organic system plan has to describe how it verifies its suppliers and the organic status of what it receives, and how it guards against organic fraud (§205.201).
- **Imports tracked:** since March 19, 2024, under USDA's Strengthening Organic Enforcement rule, each organic import has to be matched to an NOP Import Certificate issued through USDA's Organic Integrity Database, and the importer must check that the shipment hasn't contacted prohibited substances since export (§205.273). That rule also changed which businesses may handle organic products without being certified.

Our [guide to coffee certifications](/blog/coffee-certifications-decoded/) covers the annual inspections, the 95 percent rule for the word "organic", and the cost of certification, alongside Fair Trade, Rainforest Alliance and Bird Friendly.

### Shade isn't required

An earlier version of this page, like our video, said organic farmers must grow coffee in shade. They don't. The rule's crop requirements don't call for shade trees; the Smithsonian's Bird Friendly seal is the one that adds shade-cover and forest criteria on top of organic. Our [certifications guide](/blog/coffee-certifications-decoded/) explains what Bird Friendly requires.

### Organic isn't the same as residue-free

The rule builds in residue testing. Each certifying agent must sample and test at least 5 percent of the operations it certifies every year (§205.670). If testing finds a prohibited substance above 5 percent of the EPA's tolerance for it, the product can't be sold as organic (§205.671).

## Does organic coffee taste better?

Not by itself. Certification describes how the coffee was farmed and handled, not how it tastes. James Hoffmann, in *The World Atlas of Coffee*, makes the point twice. Writing about the Dominican Republic, where more and more export coffee is organic, he says that "while organic production is overall a good thing," it "does not necessarily make a better cup of coffee." Of Peru, he writes that its strong culture of organic production rarely yields higher cup quality, and that its organic coffees are often so cheap they bring down the prices paid to other farmers.

What does shape the cup is on the bag too: the [variety](/blog/coffee-varietal-guide/), the [process](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/), the roast and how fresh the coffee is. A carefully roasted, fresh conventional coffee can easily beat a stale organic one.

### Uncertified isn't the same as organic

You'll read that many small farms are "organic by default" because they can't afford farm chemicals. The uncertified coffee may or may not be grown that way; without certification, no one has checked. An earlier version of this page cited Hoffmann for the idea that much of Ethiopia's coffee grows under forest canopy without synthetic inputs. That isn't quite what he says. He describes three systems: forest coffees from wild trees with natural shade; garden coffees planted around homes, where "many producers use some fertilizer," which make up the bulk of Ethiopia's production; and plantations that use standard practices, including fertilizers.

## Residues and health

USDA is explicit that the organic label is not a health claim: "The USDA organic regulations do not address food safety or nutrition," its National Organic Program says.

On residues in coffee itself, the research we found is thin. In one lab study, K. Sakamoto and colleagues spiked green coffee beans with three organochlorine pesticides, at two concentrations, and with two other compounds, then roasted them. Two of them, γ-BHC and atrazine, disappeared; more than 90 percent of the chlordane was lost; and less than 1 percent of the heptachlor remained. That study tested a handful of compounds added to beans in the lab, not coffee as sold, and it didn't compare organic with conventional coffee. We didn't find a study that did. An earlier version of this page said organic coffee "typically has significantly lower pesticide residue levels" and that organic "eliminates the concern entirely"; we couldn't find the research behind either claim, so they're gone.

If you have a health reason to avoid a particular substance, ask your clinician.

Two of the video's nine are gone from this list: we couldn't find a current listing from the roaster for Real Good Coffee Company's organic dark roast or for Direct City Coffee.

### Everyday whole beans

**<a href="https://kickinghorsecoffee.com/pages/faq">Kicking Horse Coffee</a>**

As published by Kicking Horse: all its coffee is certified organic by ECOCERT, which audits it every year, and is 100 percent certified Fairtrade; it's roasted in Invermere, British Columbia. The video picked a Kicking Horse dark roast without naming it, and the company sells several. To find when a bag was roasted, Kicking Horse says to check the best-before date on the back: the beans were roasted 18 months before it.

**<a href="https://www.volcanicacoffee.com/collections/organic-coffee">Volcanica Coffee, organic range</a>**

As published by Volcanica: "We are a USDA Certified Organic Coffee plant," and it labels its organic coffees one by one, such as its Ethiopian Yirgacheffe and Sumatra Mandheling, grouping them in an organic collection. Its FAQ says it roasts every week to what it expects to sell, and that nothing ships with a roast date more than two weeks old. Our video said Volcanica roasts after each order is placed; its own FAQ describes the weekly schedule instead.

**<a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Subtle+Earth+Organic+Coffee+Whole+Bean&tag=jayarrcoffee-20" rel="sponsored noopener">Subtle Earth Organic Coffee</a>**

As published on Don Pablo Coffee's website: "100% USDA Organic," certified by CCOF, grown in Honduras, and sold in light, medium-dark and dark roasts, with notes of milk chocolate, honey, caramel and cocoa. Don Pablo describes it as "naturally low in acidity." Our video called it Cafe Don Pablo Subtle Earth; Burke Brands, the Miami roaster behind Don Pablo, lists Subtle Earth Organic Coffee as a brand of its own.

### For a strong cup

**<a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Death+Wish+Coffee+Dark+Roast+Whole+Bean&tag=jayarrcoffee-20" rel="sponsored noopener">Death Wish Coffee Dark Roast</a>**

As published by Death Wish: USDA organic and Fair Trade certified, a blend of arabica and robusta, with notes of baker's chocolate and cherry. Death Wish says a 6 oz serving, brewed to its package instructions, has approximately 180 mg of caffeine. Robusta carries about twice the caffeine of arabica, as our [caffeine guide](/blog/caffeine-in-coffee-guide/) explains; the guide also has the FDA's guidance on daily amounts.

### For cold brew

**<a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Bizzy+Organic+Cold+Brew+Coffee+Smooth+Sweet&tag=jayarrcoffee-20" rel="sponsored noopener">Bizzy Organic Cold Brew Coffee, Smooth & Sweet</a>**

As published by Bizzy: organic arabica, coarse ground for cold brew, with notes of caramel and hazelnut. Bizzy says to steep it 14 to 20 hours. That's longer than the 12 hours our Brew Guide uses for cold brew; with this coffee, follow the maker.

**<a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Tiny+Footprint+Coffee+Organic+Cold+Press+Elixir&tag=jayarrcoffee-20" rel="sponsored noopener">Tiny Footprint Coffee Organic Cold Press Elixir</a>**

As published by Tiny Footprint: a blend for cold brew of its light and dark roasts with its Ethiopian coffees, certified organic, with "subtle bright fruit and floral tones" in "a cocoa-like body." The video picked Tiny Footprint for its claim to be carbon negative. The company says producing and distributing a pound of its coffee releases about 4 pounds of CO₂, that it funds reforestation for every pound sold, and that over time those trees will remove 54 pounds. That last figure is the company's projection, and its page doesn't name an independent check.

**<a href="https://www.jocoffee.com/no-fun-jo-decaf.html">Jo Coffee, No Fun Jo Decaf</a>**

As published by Jo Coffee: certified organic and decaffeinated by the Swiss Water Process, which Jo describes as using water, temperature and time until the coffee is 99.9 percent caffeine free; sold whole bean, ground and in pods. ## Should you buy organic?

Buy it for what it certifies: a farm and a supply chain that keep prohibited substances out, under inspection, and a rule that asks farms to look after their soil, water and wildlife. Don't buy it as a substitute for freshness, good sourcing or proper storage; if you're choosing between a fresh specialty coffee without the seal and a stale organic one, buy the fresh one. My practical advice: buy organic when the option exists at a roaster you already trust for quality.
