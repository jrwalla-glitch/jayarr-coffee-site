Growers across Latin America call it *la roya*, and a 2025 study calls it the most significant constraint on arabica production worldwide. Its history shows why: it ended one island's coffee economy, reached the Americas a century later, and in 2012/13 caused what the International Coffee Organization called one of the worst epidemics ever recorded. This is the story of the fungus, its epidemics, and the breeders trying to stay ahead of it.

## What coffee leaf rust is

James Hoffmann's *World Atlas of Coffee* sums it up: a fungus that causes orange lesions on the leaves, impairs photosynthesis, then makes the leaves drop, and can eventually kill the tree. A 2025 study by World Coffee Research (WCR) scientists and their partners adds the biology. The rust lives only on coffee. Its spores travel on wind and splashing rain, on insects and on animals, people included. It prefers temperatures of 68–82°F&nbsp;(20–28°C), needs a wet leaf only while a spore germinates, and gets in through the leaf's breathing pores, the stomata. It can cut yields by 35 to 50 percent, and by more than 75 percent in severe cases.

Altitude used to be a partial shield. The 2025 study notes that rust was less severe above about 1,200 m (3,900 ft), but has been reported spreading above 1,600 m (5,200 ft).

## Ceylon, 1869: the first great epidemic

Hoffmann writes that the fungus was first documented in East Africa in 1861, but wasn't studied until it struck Sri Lanka, then Ceylon, in 1869, where it "pretty much destroyed" the coffee plantations over the next ten years.

Ceylon had become the world's greatest coffee producer within about fifty years, Jeff Koehler writes in *Where the Wild Coffee Grows*. It exported some 118 million pounds of coffee in 1870 and still 111.5 million in 1873, as planters fought the rust. Mark Pendergrast's *Uncommon Grounds* describes the struggle: the first year did real damage, then the disease seemed to come and go between good and bad years, and the planters tried chemicals and stripping the diseased leaves. Nothing worked.

In 1880, Koehler writes, Kew Gardens in London sent a 25-year-old Cambridge mycologist, Harry Marshall Ward, to the island. Over two years Ward proved that *Hemileia vastatrix* caused the disease and worked out its life cycle, but he could offer no cure. Losses mounted until coffee stopped paying. By 1886 exports were down 80 percent, and by 1890 nine-tenths of the land under coffee had been abandoned. The planters replaced it: Pendergrast writes that Ceylon "soon switched primarily to tea," and Koehler adds rubber, cinchona and some cacao.

The fungus moved on through Asia and Africa. Hoffmann writes that rust wiped out much of Indonesia's arabica in 1876, and that after Liberica coffee also succumbed, its planters switched to robusta, which resisted the disease. In India, he writes, rust combined with growing demand for tea to push many plantations into tea in the 1870s, though it also spurred breeding of rust-resistant varieties. By 1920, Koehler writes, rust had reached most of Central and East Africa's coffee countries; Ethiopia was the exception.

## How the Americas escaped, and then didn't

For a century, the Americas grew arabica without rust. Part of the reason is history. Koehler quotes the environmental historian Stuart McCook: before reaching the rest of the world, cultivated coffee passed through Yemen, whose dry climate created "an accidental but highly effective ecological filter against the rust." Pendergrast writes that Brazil even banned robusta plants for fear of bringing the disease in.

The rust arrived anyway. It was found in Bahia, Brazil, in 1970. Hoffmann suggests it may have come from Africa with a shipment of cacao seeds; Pendergrast thinks it most likely came on the clothing of visitors from Africa. Either way, it was already widespread when found: Koehler writes that it covered some 200,000 square miles in Bahia and two other states. Pendergrast describes a quarantine belt 40 miles wide and 500 miles long, burned clear of coffee, which the disease jumped.

Koehler traces the spread from there, drawing on the rust researchers Kushalappa and Eskes:

- **South America:** Paraguay and Argentina in 1972, Bolivia in 1978, Peru in 1979, Ecuador in 1981, Colombia in 1983 and Venezuela in 1984.
- **Central America and Mexico:** Nicaragua in 1976, El Salvador in 1979, Honduras and Guatemala in 1980, Mexico in 1981 and Costa Rica in 1983.

The region's coffee institutes responded together. WCR writes that PROMECAFE, Central America's regional coffee research network, was founded in 1978, with US and Brazilian support, to meet the threat. Pendergrast describes how growers were also urged to "technify," switching from shaded Bourbon and Typica to compact varieties grown in full sun with fertilizer and agrochemicals. For decades the disease seemed manageable.

## Colombia's epidemic, 2008–2011

A 2015 review led by Jacques Avelino counts three recent epidemics more intense than any before: Colombia from 2008 to 2011, Central America and Mexico in 2012–13, and Peru and Ecuador in 2013. Colombia's came first. The International Coffee Organization's March 2013 market report, summarizing research by CABI, says infestation passed 50 percent of the planted area by 2010 and production fell by almost 40 percent. Tests at Portugal's Coffee Rust Research Center found no new, more aggressive strain. Instead the outbreak was put down to heavy rain, cloudy skies, a narrower gap between day and night temperatures, and too little fertilizer, which farmers had cut back because of its cost. The Avelino review puts Colombia's production 31 percent lower on average during the epidemic years than in 2007.

Colombia replanted. Cenicafé, the research center of its coffee growers' federation, had released the rust-resistant Castillo in 2005, and Koehler writes that Castillo made up nearly 40 percent of Colombia's coffee by mid-2015. Castillo is a cross of Caturra and the Timor Hybrid line CIFC 1343, a Catimor-type parentage, not a Sarchimor. Our [Colombian coffee guide](/blog/single-origin-colombian-coffee-what-flavor-notes-to-expect/) covers Castillo, its successors and the debate over its cup.

## Central America, 2012–13

Then came Central America. When the International Coffee Council met in March 2013, its resolution called the epidemic the worst since rust reached the region in 1976, in a sector that gave more than two million Central Americans their living. In May 2013 the ICO's executive director reported back from visits to five affected countries, with figures from PROMECAFE for the 2012/13 crop:

- **Losses:** about 2.7 million 60 kg bags, worth about 500 million US dollars, a 17.1 percent drop from the region's 15.8 million bags in 2011/12.
- **Jobs:** about 374,000 lost, since the harvest labor wouldn't be needed.
- **Spread:** more than half of the region's coffee area was affected on average.
- **Emergencies:** Costa Rica, Guatemala and Honduras had declared phytosanitary emergencies, and Nicaragua and El Salvador were also badly hit.

The estimates moved as the season went on. In March the ICO had cited PROMECAFE's 2.3 million bags and about 441,000 direct job losses, and the Central American members' draft resolution gave 2.5 million bags and about 437,000 jobs.

The table sets PROMECAFE's share of coffee area affected beside USDA's crop estimates before and after:

| Guatemala | 70% | 4,410 → 3,515 (−20%) |

USDA crops are in thousands of 60 kg bags, from its production database (July 2026 download). Rust wasn't the only thing moving these numbers, and our [El Salvador guide](/blog/el-salvador-coffee-guide-pacamara/) measures the fall from 2010/11, which makes it about 70 percent.

Why then? The Avelino review found two drivers. Coffee prices were low, so farmers had cut back on management and their plants were more vulnerable. And the weather changed the disease: across 1,224 Guatemalan farms in 2012, daily minimum temperatures averaged 1.6°F&nbsp;(0.9°C) higher and maximums 2.2°F&nbsp;(1.2°C) lower than usual, which likely shortened the time between infection and new spores. The review puts Central America's production 16 percent lower in 2013 than in 2011–12, and another 10 percent lower in 2013–14. It calls these epidemics a warning, since they were "enhanced by weather conditions consistent with climate change."

The human cost went beyond the farms. The ICO warned in 2013 of food insecurity and of "increased migratory pressure towards North America." Koehler later reported that a US congressional report cited coffee rust as a major factor in the surge of unaccompanied minors crossing the US border in the mid-2010s.

The response started that spring. In April 2013, the ICO reports, WCR and PROMECAFE held the first International Coffee Leaf Rust Crisis Summit in Guatemala, with traders, coffee associations, governments and scientists.

## Why one fungus can threaten so much coffee

In its home forests, rust is in balance with coffee. Koehler writes that the fungus evolved alongside the plant in equatorial Africa, including Ethiopia's southwestern highlands, and quotes the geneticist Philippe Lashermes: "Rust is everywhere in Ethiopia, but it is not a big problem because of diversity."

Outside Ethiopia, there's little diversity to help. WCR writes that in Brazil, which grows about 40 percent of the world's coffee, 97.55 percent of coffee cultivars derive from just two old lines, Typica and Bourbon. Plantings that uniform give a fungus little variety to run into.

## The Timor Hybrid and the resistant varieties

The defense came from robusta. In the 1920s, WCR writes, an arabica and a robusta plant crossed naturally on the island of Timor. (Koehler gives 1927, noting that some say 1917.) Crossing the two species is normally fruitless: Koehler explains, quoting the breeder Benoît Bertrand, that the offspring are sterile. But the Timor Hybrid had arabica's chromosome count, so it could be bred with arabica, and its robusta genes resisted rust.

WCR's account of what followed:

- **1958 or 1959:** Portugal's Coffee Rust Research Center, CIFC, received Timor Hybrid seed.
- **From 1967:** its breeders crossed rust-resistant Timor Hybrid plants with compact varieties, Caturra to make the Catimors and Villa Sarchi to make the Sarchimors. (Koehler dates the first Catimor cross to 1959.)
- **1971:** CIFC sent its Sarchimor hybrid, H361, out to trial stations in many countries, including most of Central America, just as rust arrived there.

National programs then bred their own lines. WCR's ratings for some of the best known, checked October 18, 2026:

| Variety (group) | Where it was bred | Leaf rust (WCR) |
|---|---|---|
| Costa Rica 95 (Catimor) | Costa Rica | Susceptible (confirmed in Costa Rica) |
| Lempira (Catimor) | Honduras | Intermediate (confirmed susceptible in Honduras) |
| IHCAFE 90 (Catimor) | Honduras | Susceptible |
| Parainema (Sarchimor) | Honduras, 2004 | Highly resistant |
| Marsellesa (Sarchimor) | Nicaragua, 2009 | Intermediate resistance |

Castillo, which isn't in WCR's catalog, is Colombia's Caturra × Timor Hybrid composite. Our [coffee varietal guide](/blog/coffee-varietal-guide/) has the full family tree and each variety's ratings for yield and cup quality.

## Why resistance is breaking down

WCR's history of arabica puts it plainly. Resistant varieties protected many farmers for nearly three decades, but early in the 21st century experts in Central America saw historically resistant varieties being infected, notably Lempira in Honduras and Costa Rica 95 in Costa Rica. Because most resistant varieties got their resistance from the same parent, "it is believed by most experts that most existing rust-resistant varieties will no longer be resistant in the near-to-medium term."

The reason is that the fungus keeps changing:

- **Races.** More than 55 races of rust have been identified, the 2025 study says, each attacking different coffee genotypes; Cenicafé counts 20 detected in Colombia in 24 years.
- **Genes.** Coffee's known resistance genes are called *SH* genes. *SH6* to *SH9* came from robusta through the Timor Hybrid, and no single variety carries all of them, so a variety can resist some races and fall to others.
- **Evolution.** Varieties that resist well today may lose that ability as the pathogen evolves.

Bertrand put the stakes bluntly to Koehler: when rust overcomes the last resistance gene, "we have no more options." Older resistance has already gone this way. Hoffmann writes that India's S795, a cross of the rust-resistant Kent with another resistant selection, S288, is now considered to have lost much of its resistance.

## What's being done

- **Testing varieties everywhere.** WCR's International Multilocation Variety Trial, begun in 2015, compared varieties from breeding programs around the world. Its rust results, reported in June 2025, covered 29 varieties at 23 sites in 15 countries. No variety was fully immune; varieties with genes from other species generally resisted better than pure arabicas, and Parainema, Kartika 1 and IPR 107 combined high resistance with stable performance across sites. Resistance also depended on where a variety was grown.
- **New sources of resistance.** Cenicafé writes that until recently every commercial variety in Colombia drew its rust resistance from one Timor Hybrid line, and that Castillo 2.0, delivered to farmers in 2024, is the first to add resistance from other robusta and arabica sources.
- **Blends of lines.** Composite varieties such as Castillo mix lines with different resistance genes, a strategy meant to make resistance last.
- **F1 hybrids.** Crosses of distant arabica parents promise more yield, but most still get their rust resistance through the Timor Hybrid. Our [F1 hybrids article](/blog/coffee-f1-hybrids-future-of-coffee/) explains.
- **Fungicides.** Rust can be controlled, generally with copper-based fungicides, but not cured, Koehler writes. Spraying costs money: in 2013 the ICO cited WCR's estimate of about 125 million US dollars to treat all the affected areas of Central America, and Cenicafé estimates chemical control of susceptible varieties in Colombia at about 180 US dollars per hectare a year, citing a 2021 study. Koehler adds that certified organic farms may not be able to use them.

## Climate and the rust

The 2012–13 epidemic shows how weather can tip the balance, and the rust is now reaching higher farms. The 2025 study records it at higher altitudes, and in 2020 it was found in Hawaii, until then one of the last coffee regions free of it. Our Deep Brew research note on [coffee and climate change](/deep-brew/research/coffee-climate-change-data/) gathers the heat, harvest and suitable-land data.

## Where things stand now

Rust is a standing problem, not a past one:

- **Honduras:** USDA reported in April 2026 that national average incidence had risen to 8.44 percent, and higher in Comayagua; our [Honduras guide](/blog/honduras-coffee-guide-marcala-comayagua/) has the details.
- **Peru:** an outbreak that began in 2013 now affects nearly 40 percent of the crop, USDA says, as our [Peru guide](/blog/peru-coffee-guide-chanchamayo/) explains.
- **Guatemala:** USDA reports incidence of up to 20 percent in early 2025, and a third of arabica plantations renovated with rust-tolerant hybrids, as our [Guatemala review](/blog/guatemala-coffee-review-regional-differences-and-best-coffees/) notes.

For coffee drinkers, the variety on the bag is part of this story. A Catimor, a Sarchimor or most F1 hybrids usually means a farmer planting for rust resistance; a Bourbon or a Typica means one managing a susceptible tree. Our guide to [reading a coffee bag](/blog/how-to-read-coffee-bag-label/) covers the rest of the label.
