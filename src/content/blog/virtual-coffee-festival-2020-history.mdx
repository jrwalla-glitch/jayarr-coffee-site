In the first three weeks of March 2020, specialty coffee's annual calendar collapsed in public. The Specialty Coffee Association postponed World of Coffee Warsaw on March 13, pushing it from June into October. Re:co Symposium and the SCA Expo in Portland, scheduled for late April, were officially canceled on March 24. World of Coffee Warsaw was eventually canceled outright in early September. By the second half of March, every flagship industry gathering in Europe and North America — the rooms where green buyers met producers, roasters announced lots, and competition champions were crowned — had vanished from the calendar.

What replaced them, and what helped shape specialty coffee's event culture for the half-decade since, came from a one-person Amsterdam subscription company that decided, on short notice, to do the thing the institutions had not — move the event itself online. This is a brief history of the first Virtual Coffee Festival, held on March 29, 2020, the people who built it, and the format it made visible for later online coffee gatherings.

## What Got Canceled, and How Quickly

The acceleration matters because it explains why a small company moved faster than the institutions could.

The SCA's late-February and early-March public updates still described the 2020 event calendar as scheduled to proceed, with added health precautions and contingency planning. By March 24, the SCA had reversed course: Yannis Apostolopoulos, the SCA's CEO at the time, told membership that "no other viable dates were available for the show to take place in Portland in 2020," and both the Expo and Re:co Symposium were canceled. Exhibitors got a credit toward 2021 in New Orleans or a full refund. World of Coffee Warsaw — postponed eleven days earlier from June to October — would limp along for several months before being officially canceled on September 2.

That timeline left specialty coffee in an unusual position. The trade press was reporting cancellations at industrial scale. Roasters had cut greenlights. Sourcing trips were stalled. Competition season was suspended. And on Friday March 27, French trade outlet [Café Mag](https://www.cafemag.fr/2020/03/27/virtual-coffee-festival-evenement-en-ligne-cafe-de-specialite/) ran one of the first announcements of an event that contemporary coverage treated as having no obvious institutional precedent: a livestreamed, multi-speaker, hashtag-coordinated specialty coffee festival, scheduled for that Sunday, organized in days rather than months, free to attend, with optional donations to Médecins Sans Frontières.

The day before that Café Mag preview, on March 26, Barista Magazine had run its own piece — Katrina Yentch's [*"World's First Virtual Coffee Festival Lands This Sunday"*](https://www.baristamagazine.com/worlds-first-virtual-coffee-festival-lands-this-sunday/) — establishing the framing every subsequent linker would use. Shortlist, the UK lifestyle title, picked it up the same week. So did Slovak trade blog Blog o káve, Supper Magazine, and a multi-language version of Drinkmorning that ran the same article in English, Thai, Malaysian, and Taiwanese editions.

The phrase that traveled — "world's first online coffee festival" or "world's first virtual coffee festival" — appears, in those literal words, across editorial previews that ran in the days before March 29. Barista Magazine used it. Shortlist used it. Supper Magazine ran it as the article's subhead. The framing was widespread and defensible within the specialty trade press, but it is best read as a contemporary industry claim rather than an externally audited world record.

## Alex Kitain, The Coffeevine, and Freshdrip

The festival was organized by **Alex Kitain**, identified by Supper Magazine in its preview as the "founder of The Coffeevine and Freshdrip." Both companies were Amsterdam-based. Both predated the festival by years. Both supplied something the festival itself needed: a pre-existing audience of specialty coffee subscribers and a working logistical apparatus run by people accustomed to shipping product on tight timelines to small audiences in many countries.

The Coffeevine started in 2013 as Kitain's personal blog — he reviewed Amsterdam coffee bars, then wrote about roasters across Europe — and, in 2014, mailed its first subscription box. The product was three coffees from three different European roasters, a curatorial premise rather than a single-roaster club, and the operation grew across Europe through the mid-2010s. By the time of the festival, The Coffeevine had an international subscriber audience and had been profiled by European Coffee Trip, Sprudge ("Amsterdam's Hottest New Cafe Is The Coffeevine Bar"), and the Dutch press. Erica Armistead came in as creative director; the operation ran out of Amsterdam.

Freshdrip — Kitain's second company — launched in 2017 after a 2016 trip to Japan, where he encountered the single-serve drip-bag format that had been refined into a category there but had little European footprint. Freshdrip's product was a paper-and-foil sachet that hooked over the rim of a mug and held a single brew of pre-ground specialty coffee. The pitch was consumer-facing: better than instant, better than capsules, ready in three minutes wherever you were. By the time of the festival, Freshdrip was on supermarket and hospitality shelves in the Netherlands, and the brand would later be acquired by Moyee Coffee on August 1, 2025, putting the format inside a larger Dutch FairChain operation.

The festival itself was a third project — built by Kitain together with his partner **Michał Brzozowski**, the Coffeevine's [own retrospective on the festival's success](https://thecoffeevine.com/blog/news/the-right-thing-at-the-right-time-how-the-virtual-coffee-festival-became-a-hit/) credits Brzozowski as co-organizer. The Coffeevine subscriber list functioned as the launch audience. The Coffeevine's existing PR relationships placed previews in the trade press in the days before March 29. And Kitain's curatorial standing — six years of writing about specialty roasters, brewing equipment, and barista culture across Europe — gave the speaker invitations the credibility a livestreamed event without a venue or a ticket would otherwise lack.

Speaker invitations went out on short notice. Five accepted. Each was a working specialty professional with a credential the audience would recognize. Each agreed to deliver an unpaid online talk, with a real-time Q&A, on a topic Kitain had selected based on what subscribers had been asking for in The Coffeevine's customer surveys.

## The Format That Stuck

The architecture of the festival, as published in the run-up, was deliberately simple. Five sessions, sequenced through a single Sunday afternoon. Each session: a 20-to-25-minute presentation followed by a 20-to-25-minute Q&A. Free to attend. Donations encouraged but optional, with proceeds going to Médecins Sans Frontières. Broadcast live to Facebook and YouTube, with Zoom added in the second edition three months later. Q&A coordinated in real time through the hashtag **#virtualcoffeefestival** — viewers asked questions, the hashtag pulled them into a single feed, and the moderator surfaced them to the speaker as the talk concluded.

Four details about that format are worth dwelling on.

The talk-plus-Q&A balance — roughly equal time given to the speaker and to the audience — borrowed from academic-conference architecture rather than coffee-trade tradition. SCA Expo presentations had typically been talks with a brief Q&A appended at the end. The Virtual Coffee Festival inverted that ratio. Half the session belonged to the audience. Cafe Mag's preview noted the format precisely: "Each speaker will lead a 20-to-25-minute online session [...] followed by a Q&A session of 20 to 25 minutes."

The hashtag-as-coordination-layer was borrowed from outside coffee. Gaming streams, sports broadcasts, and political-debate coverage had been using hashtags for live-question aggregation for years. The Virtual Coffee Festival was an early specialty-coffee example of the same mechanism. Barista Magazine's Yentch flagged it explicitly: "the hashtag #virtualcoffeefestival will be utilized by participants to connect and share how others are enjoying the festival."

The multi-platform simulcast defied the conventional wisdom that you should concentrate an audience on one platform. The Coffeevine's reasoning was that different national audiences default to different platforms, particularly across the Asia-Pacific markets where The Coffeevine already shipped. Simulcasting cost roughly nothing technically, and let viewers pick whichever stream they were already logged into.

The optional-donation model lowered activation cost to nearly zero while giving the festival a way to do something with the inbound goodwill. By April 6, the Coffeevine reported, donations had reached €2,000 for Médecins Sans Frontières.

The brevity of preparation underwrote all of it. The Barista Magazine preview ran on March 26, Café Mag on March 27, the festival on March 29 — a trade-press window measured in days. The Coffeevine's own retrospective frames the operation as having been organized on "short notice." That speed was itself the proof of concept: if a one-person company in Amsterdam could organize an internationally-livestreamed multi-speaker industry event in days, the older institutional format — a calendar-year planning cycle for an in-person trade show — was no longer the only viable option.

## Who Spoke

The 2020 lineup — five professionals from four countries — read in retrospect like a snapshot of the European and Antipodean specialty scene at the moment the global event calendar collapsed. Each speaker's topic and credential is recorded in the previews Café Mag, Barista Magazine, Shortlist, and Supper Magazine published that week:

- **Ronny Billemon** (Belgium) — author of *Equilibrium in Water and Coffee* — on water chemistry and brewing
- **Joe McTaggart** (working with Comandante Grinders) — on precision grinding across brew methods
- **Talor Browne** (Australia) — founder of Talormade Coffee & Donuts in Oslo — on selecting and bringing out character in single-origin coffees
- **Matt Winton** (Australia/Sweden) — Swedish Barista Champion and Swiss Brewers Cup champion — on brewing fundamentals
- **Wendelien van Bunnik** (Netherlands) — Dutch Barista Champion and World AeroPress champion — on AeroPress technique

The lineup is worth pausing on for two reasons. First, none of the speakers were employed by the SCA or by any of the canceled events; they were working roasters, brewers, and authors who had built reputations independently. Second, the topics were practical-instructional rather than industry-political: water, grind, single-origin selection, brew technique, AeroPress. The Virtual Coffee Festival, in its first edition, was framed not as a discussion of where the industry was going, but as an open seminar on how to brew better coffee at home — a useful editorial choice when most of its audience had been stuck at home for two weeks and had a stockpile of specialty beans, freshly mail-ordered or freshly roasted, that they were now grinding through faster than usual.

The unpaid talks were the architectural concession. Speakers donated their time. The Coffeevine donated the platform. The audience donated, as much or as little as they wanted, to Médecins Sans Frontières. The economic model was charity-adjacent, and that frame mattered for the speaker invitations: nobody had to negotiate a fee, nobody had to weigh the appearance against a paid keynote elsewhere, nobody had a contractual conflict with a trade show that no longer existed. The talks were free because the festival was free.

## Two Thousand Viewers and What They Got

The Coffeevine's own retrospective reports that "over 2,000 people from around the world watched the event by April 6th, 2020" — roughly a week after the live broadcast, accounting for archived views on YouTube and Facebook. That is the cleanest version of the audience figure: more than 2,000 total viewers in the first week, not necessarily 2,000 simultaneous live attendees. Some later summaries blur the platform list with the June edition, but the March event was promoted as a Facebook-and-YouTube broadcast; Zoom was added for the second edition.

The geographic spread of that audience is implicit in the press coverage. Drinkmorning's multi-language editions — Morning Europe, Morning Thailand, Morning Malaysia, Morning Taiwan, Morning UK — covered the event in parallel, which suggests the festival's reach mapped onto the same Asia-Pacific specialty markets where The Coffeevine's subscription box was already shipping. Slovak coverage came from Blog o káve, French coverage from Café Mag, German and Italian specialty press in parallel through the week before broadcast.

What participants got was specialty coffee instruction at a speaker quality that would normally have required a paid Expo ticket plus a flight to Portland or Warsaw. Wendelien van Bunnik's AeroPress session — taught by the World AeroPress Champion — would have been a paid masterclass in any other format. Watching it from home on a Sunday afternoon, brewing along with the speaker, was a kind of access the in-person trade-show economy had never quite delivered.

## Why It Mattered — A Format-Innovation Story, Not a Pandemic Story

The temptation, in retrospect, is to read the Virtual Coffee Festival as a pandemic-coffee-industry story. The event ran in late March 2020. The SCA Expo had been canceled five days before it. Kitain's own quote — "Yes, we can get through this together" — was unmistakably pandemic-era language.

But the deeper story is a format-innovation one in which the pandemic was the context, not the protagonist. The Virtual Coffee Festival did not invent livestreaming, specialty coffee education, or the trade-press preview cycle. What it did was assemble those existing components — livestream, hashtag-driven Q&A, charity-adjacent ticketing, multi-platform simulcast, short-notice scheduling, audience pre-built through a subscription company — into a working template that other organizations could copy. The copying happened almost immediately.

The SCA later relaunched Re:co Symposium 2020 as a virtual event. By the time Re:co Symposium 2021 ran virtually on May 12–13, the SCA was using a format that mirrored the Virtual Coffee Festival's architecture in some key elements: speaker presentations, audience Q&A, digital delivery, and sensory kits mailed to participants. Tea & Coffee Trade Journal's coverage of Re:co 2021 noted the structure: "several speakers for each topic followed by discussion rooms where participants can choose which topic they would like to discuss in further detail." What the Virtual Coffee Festival had proven quickly, the SCA institutionalized at a larger scale over the following two years.

Other operators followed. Counter Culture Coffee's weekly Friday tasting program — Tasting at Ten, run out of the company's regional Training Centers — added virtual brewing classes participants could attend from their own kitchens. The London Coffee Festival ran a virtual edition in 2021. The Global Coffee Festival went online for its 2021 edition, October 29–31. Sprudge began running a weekly roundup of online coffee events. The hashtag-coordinated Q&A, the multi-platform simulcast, and the talk-plus-discussion architecture had become the default — not because subsequent organizers borrowed directly from the March 2020 broadcast, but because the format had been demonstrated in public to work.

Within [specialty coffee's third wave](/blog/third-wave-coffee-history-movement/), the moment also closed a long-running argument about geographic concentration. Trade shows had been held in expensive cities — Portland, Seattle, Milan, Amsterdam, Boston — and the cost of attending had functioned as a soft gate keeping specialty coffee's annual conversations in the hands of operators with travel budgets. The Virtual Coffee Festival did not abolish that gate, but it demonstrated that the same conversations could be held with a wider audience, in a format that allowed working baristas in cities without trade-show infrastructure to participate without taking a week off. The Coffeevine had already been making the parallel argument, structurally, with its mail-order subscription model: that specialty coffee's good things did not have to be tied to specific physical locations. The festival extended that argument to specialty coffee's events.

The European specialty cluster — figures like [Klaus Thomsen](/blog/klaus-thomsen-coffee-collective-copenhagen) at Coffee Collective in Copenhagen, Tim Wendelboe in Oslo, James Hoffmann at Square Mile in London, and the next-generation roasters at La Cabra and April — had spent the previous decade building reputations through competition wins, trade-show presence, and longform writing. The Virtual Coffee Festival opened a fourth channel.

## What Happened After 2020

The Coffeevine ran a [second edition of the Virtual Coffee Festival](https://www.comunicaffe.com/virtual-coffee-festival-is-back-on-28-june-2020-with-its-second-edition/) on June 28, 2020 — three months after the inaugural broadcast. The format was the same: five speakers, talk-plus-Q&A, livestreamed from Amsterdam, with Zoom added as a webinar layer alongside Facebook and YouTube. The hashtag carried over. The donation model carried over. The audience structure carried over.

The Coffeevine's own framing of the festival's purpose, expressed in Kitain's quote in Supper Magazine — "I would love the Virtual Coffee Festival to become a new recurring event — a great addition to the annual coffee festival calendar" — was an institution-building intention. Whether the festival itself recurred annually in subsequent years is harder to verify cleanly from public records: the press coverage of the 2020 editions is dense, the press coverage of subsequent years is thinner. What is unambiguously durable is the format. The talk-plus-Q&A structure, the charity-tied free-attendance model, the hashtag-coordinated audience layer, and the multi-platform broadcast template have continued to define online specialty coffee events through 2026.

The Coffeevine itself remains in business — as of early 2026, it ships monthly boxes globally and celebrated its 10-year anniversary in 2024. Freshdrip was acquired by Moyee Coffee in 2025 and continues to sell single-serve drip bags through retail, hospitality, and office channels. Alex Kitain was profiled in 2022 in *Standart* magazine on the strength of his combined entrepreneurial run with The Coffeevine and Freshdrip.

The Virtual Coffee Festival's domain — the URL trade publications around the world linked to in March 2020 — eventually lapsed. Much of the press coverage, however, did not. Shortlist's preview, Barista Magazine's preview, Supper Magazine's preview, Café Mag's preview, Drinkmorning's multi-language run, Slovak Blog o káve's coverage — many remain live and indexed. The literal phrase "world's first online coffee festival," or its near-twin "world's first virtual coffee festival," appears across that preview coverage. As an editorial framing, it has stuck.

## How to Read This in Context

The 2020 Virtual Coffee Festival was small. Five speakers, roughly two thousand viewers in the first week, €2,000 donated, a single Sunday afternoon. Compared to a flagship in-person Expo it was negligible.

What made it consequential was timing and architecture. It ran in the days when specialty coffee's institutions were canceling flagship events with no online equivalent on the calendar, and it demonstrated, in public, that the online equivalent could be built quickly, cheaply, and at high speaker quality. The format it assembled — five simple components, all already used elsewhere — became the template the SCA, the Counter Culture Training Centers, the London Coffee Festival, and dozens of smaller operators would adopt over the following two years.

In the rough lineage of specialty coffee's institution-builders, Alex Kitain's place is specific and small. He did not build the largest roaster, win the World Barista Championship, or write the most-cited industry book. What he and Michał Brzozowski did was demonstrate, on short notice in March 2020, that an entire category of industry event could be moved to a different medium without losing its substance. For readers who came to specialty coffee through subscription boxes, livestreamed brewing classes, or any of the countless online coffee events that have run since 2020, the Sunday afternoon of March 29 is roughly where that format was born.
