Ask most coffee drinkers about Hawaii and they'll name Kona. Ka'u, its neighbor to the southeast, has been growing coffee seriously for only about 25 years, on land that was sugarcane until 1996. In that time it has built a record in cupping competitions out of proportion to its size. Here's where it is, how it got started, what its coffee tastes like, and how it compares with Kona.

## Where Ka'u is

Ka'u is the southernmost district of Hawaiʻi Island, the Big Island. Its coffee grows on the slopes of Mauna Loa, mostly in the hills above Pahala; the Ka'u Coffee Mill sits upslope in Wood Valley. The Synergistic Hawaii Agriculture Council (SHAC), whose Hawaii-grown coffee site profiles each growing region, gives these figures for the district:

- **Farms:** 80 to 90, averaging 5 to 10 acres.
- **Coffee area:** about 830 acres.
- **Elevation:** 1,100 to 2,700 feet.

Other sources give narrower bands. Hoffmann's atlas lists 500 to 650 m (1,600 to 2,150 feet), and Perfect Daily Grind, reporting from Ka'u Specialty Coffee in 2017, put most farms at 1,500 to 2,100 feet. That's low for coffee by world standards. Shawn Steiman explains why it works in *Coffee: A Comprehensive Guide*: at 19 to 21 degrees north, Hawaii's latitude lowers temperatures enough to allow high-quality coffee at elevations considered low in most other producing countries.

## From sugar to coffee

Coffee came to Hawaii in about 1825, and Steiman writes that Typica arrived from Guatemala in 1892 and found favor with farmers. Then a crash in world coffee prices in 1899 pushed coffee out of most of the islands in favor of sugarcane; only in Kona, where the land didn't suit sugar machinery, did it stay. Hawaii Business magazine dates coffee in Ka'u to 1895, but there, too, cane took every arable acre.

The modern story starts with the end of sugar. Hawaii Business recounts it in a 2013 history:

- **1996.** C. Brewer and Co. closed its Ka'u sugar mill. The company then leased 5-acre plots on 15-year leases at 150 US dollars an acre a year, with the first five years free, and displaced sugar workers were trained in coffee.
- **2000.** Will Tabios, who had worked at the sugar mill, and his wife, Grace, started Rising Sun farm on 7 acres at 1,700 feet above Pahala.
- **2002.** Rusty and Lorie Obra, who had retired to Ka'u from New Jersey, picked the first crop of Rusty's Hawaiian, and the growers formed the Ka'u Coffee Growers Cooperative, with Rusty as its founding president.
- **2006.** About 40 farms were producing but struggling to find buyers. Rusty Obra died that year. Christopher Manfredi's Ka'u Farm & Ranch Co. bought 2,000 acres, including all 40 tenant farms, and around the same time investor Edmund C. Olson acquired 13,000 acres on the island, 8,000 of them former C. Brewer land.
- **2012.** The Ka'u Coffee Mill, financed by the Edmund C. Olson Trust II at a cost of 1.5 million dollars, opened on January 21. Before that, growers hauled their dried coffee to Kona or Puna.

The Ka'u Coffee Festival in Pahala was first held in 2009; Hawaii Business reported its fifth anniversary in 2013.

## The competition record

Ka'u's reputation was made in cupping competitions. These are the results we could confirm in dated reporting:

| Year | Competition | Ka'u result |
|---|---|---|
| 2007 | SCAA Roasters Guild cupping, Long Beach (104 coffees) | Rising Sun 6th, Aroma Farms 9th |
| 2009 | SCAA Coffee of the Year competition, Anaheim | Kailiawa Coffee Farm 7th |
| 2010 | SCAA Roasters Guild Coffee of the Year | Rising Sun the winner in the Hawaii–USA category, at 87.563 |
| 2010 and 2011 | Hawaii Coffee Association statewide cupping | Rusty's Hawaiian named Grand Champion both years |
| 2012 | SCAA Roasters Guild Coffees of the Year (250 entries) | Three Ka'u growers in the top 10 |
| 2026 | Hawaii Coffee Association, Best of Hawai'i | Miranda's Farms' natural Gesha took the top score, 87.84; Ka'u also won the two Microlot categories |

In 2026's Best of Hawai'i, the 17th edition of the statewide competition, Ka'u coffees won three of the five categories: Miranda's Farms in the Innovative Division for unpulped coffee, A Coffee Farm with a Catuai at 86.79 and the Ka'u Coffee Mill with a Red Bourbon at 85.70. Kona, Maui and Ka'u all had winners.

## What Ka'u coffee tastes like

SHAC lists chocolate, cherry, floral and citrus as Ka'u's tasting notes, and credits its "characteristic deep flavors" to a sunny climate and misty afternoons. In 2017 Malian Lahey of Ka'u Specialty Coffee described a typical Ka'u cup to Perfect Daily Grind as "fresh tropical and crisp citrus flavors" set against the sweetness of honey and caramel.

For balance, Hoffmann describes Hawaiian coffee in general as typically lower in acidity with a little more body, approachable but rarely complex and fruited, and writes that many coffee professionals feel its quality may not merit its price. These are what a Ka'u coffee *can* taste like, not a promise for any bag.

## Varieties and processing

Hoffmann lists Typica for Ka'u, but farms grow more than that. Joan Obra of Rusty's Hawaiian, writing in 2013, described the family farm's Red Bourbon, Typica, Yellow Caturra and Red Caturra, each processed differently to bring out its flavors; a "Kenya-style" Red Bourbon, developed over two years of experiments, won the Hawaii Coffee Association's 2011 statewide competition. The 2026 winners above were a Gesha, a Catuai and a Red Bourbon.

Ka'u has also tried newer processing. Perfect Daily Grind reported in 2017 that Ka'u Specialty Coffee was using three methods Lahey described as new to Hawaii: carbonic maceration, which Sasa Sestic, who won the 2015 World Barista Championship with a carbonic-macerated coffee, had been helping her with; a proprietary "freezer process"; and washing with mineral water from the Ka'u Forest Preserve. Our [processing guide](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/) explains how these differ from washed, natural and honey coffees.

## Pests and leaf rust

Steiman writes that Hawaii's remoteness and its quarantine on incoming coffee plants long kept serious pests and diseases away. Two have arrived since 2010:

- **Coffee berry borer.** The Hawaii Department of Agriculture (HDOA) confirmed the beetle on farms in Kona in September 2010, and its first detection on a Ka'u farm, in Pahala, in May 2011. Its larvae feed inside the bean, reducing yield and quality.
- **Coffee leaf rust.** HDOA confirmed the fungus in the Haiku area of Maui in October 2020, and on Hawaiʻi Island from samples collected near Holualoa, south of Kailua-Kona, on October 31, 2020. It has since been found on Maui, Hawaiʻi Island, Oʻahu and Lānaʻi. HDOA notes that rust can lead to dieback that hits the following year's yield, with some researchers estimating losses of 30 to 80 percent.

Our [leaf rust history](/blog/coffee-leaf-rust-hemileia-vastatrix-history/) covers the disease worldwide.

## How much Ka'u grows

USDA's National Agricultural Statistics Service reports Hawaii's crop for the state as a whole: 20.2 million pounds of cherry in 2025-26, or 3.93 million pounds of green coffee, from 6,500 bearing acres. That's about 30,000 60&nbsp;kg bags (calculated), a tiny crop by world standards. NASS doesn't split it by district. SHAC's 830 acres is the best current picture of Ka'u's share; Perfect Daily Grind put the district in 2017 at about 50 farming families producing 180,000 kg a year.

## The labeling law

Ka'u is one of the geographic origins Hawaii's labeling law protects, alongside Kona and the other districts and islands. Under Hawaii Revised Statutes §486-120.6, as amended in 2024:

- **"Ka'u Coffee" or "100% Ka'u Coffee"** may be used only if all the coffee in the package is from Ka'u.
- **A blend** must state the percentage of the named coffee, as in "10% Ka'u Coffee Blend," and list the rest by percentage below it, or as "Foreign-grown Coffee."
- **The minimum** share of the named coffee needed to use the name at all is 10 percent until June 30, 2027, and 51 percent from July 1, 2027.

The 51 percent rule comes from Act 198, signed July 3, 2024; HDOA's labeling guidelines say compliance is required by July 1, 2027. An earlier change, Act 211 of 2023, extended the rules to ready-to-drink coffee from July 1, 2024. So a Ka'u blend is legal, but its label has to say how much Ka'u is in it.

## Ka'u vs Kona

Kona, on the island's west side, is older and much bigger. SHAC's figures for the two districts:

| | Kona | Ka'u |
|---|---|---|
| Farms | 900–1,000 | 80–90 |
| Coffee area | 3,800–4,000 acres | about 830 acres |
| Elevation | 500–3,000 feet | 1,100–2,700 feet |
| Typical farm | 2–5 acres | 5–10 acres |
| SHAC's tasting notes | Floral, citrus, berry, nutty, a smooth finish | Chocolate, cherry, floral, citrus |

Hoffmann gives both districts an August-to-January harvest and lists Typica for each; he puts Kona at 150 to 900 m and Ka'u at 500 to 650 m. Kona has its own size-based grades, from Extra Fancy down through Fancy, Number 1, Select and Prime, plus two peaberry grades, though he notes the defect limits are generous and not a reliable guide to quality.

Both are expensive by world standards. Steiman explains Hawaii's prices by its remoteness, the cost of complying with US labor and environmental law, the cost of living, and the "paradise" image used to market Hawaiian products. In Steiman's account, Kona was Hawaii's only commercial coffee region for most of the 20th century, which is how it became synonymous with Hawaiian coffee; Ka'u's reputation is recent and was built largely in competition.

## Buying and brewing Ka'u coffee

Most Ka'u coffee is sold by the farms and mills themselves, and by roasters that carry Hawaiian coffees. On a bag, check:

- **The identity statement.** "100% Ka'u Coffee" or "Ka'u Coffee" means all Ka'u; "Ka'u Coffee Blend" means a percentage, printed beside it.
- **The farm.** Ka'u's competition record is a record of named farms, so a bag that names one tells you more than "Ka'u" alone.
- **The process and variety.** Café Imports lists washed processing for Hawaii, but Ka'u farms also make naturals and honeys and grow more than Typica, like the Gesha, Catuai and Red Bourbon that won in 2026.

The festival in Pahala is a chance to taste many of the district's coffees side by side; dates are on the [Ka'u Coffee Festival site](https://www.kaucoffeefestival.com/). To brew a Ka'u coffee, any good method works; a pour-over shows a washed coffee's sweetness and a natural's fruit clearly.
