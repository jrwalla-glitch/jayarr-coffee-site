Pour a little hot water on fresh grounds and the bed swells, domes and fizzes. That's the bloom. It looks like the coffee breathing out, and in a sense it is. But the gas is only half the story, and the half that matters more is the water going in.

## What happens when the water hits

Jonathan Gagné, an astrophysicist who writes about the physics of coffee brewing, explains the bloom (he calls it preinfusion) in *The Physics of Filter Coffee*. Dry coffee resists water far more than wet coffee, so the first water runs through whatever patches it wets first. If the main pour starts while some of the bed is still dry, water keeps taking those wet paths, over-extracting the coffee along them and leaving the rest under-extracted. The bloom's job is to get every particle wet before the bulk of the water arrives.

Two things carry the water through the bed. Gravity pulls it down, and capillary action, the same effect that wicks water up a towel, spreads it sideways and draws it into the particles themselves. As water enters a particle through its pores, carbon dioxide stored in the coffee cells during roasting is pushed out through other pores. That's the fizzing: Gagné notes that bubbles appear every time water touches a dry particle.

So the bloom is both things at once: wetting and degassing. James Hoffmann, in *The World Atlas of Coffee*, is candid that there isn't much science behind the practice, and suggests releasing some of the gas may make the coffee easier to extract. Jessica Easto, in *Craft Coffee: A Manual*, puts it the same way: if the gas isn't released, it pushes the water away from the coffee. Gagné's account explains why: the gas and dry pockets get in the way of an even flow.

Getting water all the way into each particle takes longer than wetting the bed. In Gagné's measurements, the coffee kept absorbing water for about 2 minutes, and coarser particles absorbed it more slowly. He also notes that lighter roasts have smaller pores between their cells, which slows the water down. That's part of why bloom advice varies with the roast.

## How much bloom water

Enough to wet all the grounds without much running through. The recipes we use land between 2 and 3 times the coffee's weight (calculated from each recipe):

- **Easto** gives a rule of thumb of twice the dose: 28 g of water for 14 g of coffee.
- **Our Brew Guide** blooms its V60 with 60 g of water for every 23.5 g of coffee, from Easto's continuous-pour recipe, about 2.6 times the dose. At 22 g of coffee that's 56 g.
- **Gagné's V60 recipe** uses three times the dose: 66 g of water on 22 g of coffee, as in our [pour-over guide](/blog/ultimate-pour-over-coffee-guide-and-best-v60-technique/).

Easto warns that pouring too much at once can trap the gas and defeat the point. Weigh the bloom water: tare the scale after adding the coffee, so the reading is water only.

## How long to bloom

- **Hoffmann:** typically 30 seconds.
- **Easto:** 30 to 45 seconds, depending on the coffee's freshness, roast and dose. Fresh, light-roasted coffee and bigger doses need longer.
- **Gagné:** his 2018 V60 recipe starts the next pour at 0:30. In his 2021 book he says he normally blooms for 45 seconds and that lighter roasts might benefit from longer.
- **Our Brew Guide:** the V60 and Chemex start the main pour at 0:45, the Kalita Wave at 0:35.

Much longer blooms are an experiment, not a default. Gagné reports that, compared with his usual 45 seconds, 2- to 3-minute blooms raised his average extraction yields by as much as 1% for several light-roasted coffees, and improved their taste. With darker roasts he saw no gain, and the long blooms made the coffee bitter and harsh.

## Why swirl

A swirl knocks dry clumps off the paper and into the water and lets the bed settle flat. In his 2018 V60 recipe, Gagné has you pour the bloom and swirl within 15 to 20 seconds. Gagné also writes that a less effective bloom, one that leaves some coffee dry, shows up as large bubbles after the main pour starts. Those dry grounds lag behind the rest, and rising bubbles open easy paths for the water. If you see that, make the next bloom more thorough: wet every ground and swirl.

Use the same hot water as the rest of the brew. Gagné notes that hotter water soaks into the particles faster, because it flows more easily.

## Is the bloom a freshness test?

Only a rough one. Fresh coffee holds more gas, so it usually bubbles more. Easto treats little or no bubbling as a good sign the coffee has gone stale, and Gagné's 2018 recipe says the same: no bubbles at all and your coffee might be stale.

But the size of the bloom also depends on things other than age:

- **Roast.** Gagné, citing measurements by Samo Smrke and colleagues at the Zurich University of Applied Sciences, notes that darker-roasted and faster-roasted beans tend to hold more carbon dioxide, and also lose it faster.
- **Your water.** Gagné explains that water with higher total alkalinity makes more bubbles in the bloom, because its bicarbonate reacts with the coffee's acids and releases carbon dioxide. That could explain Hoffmann's finding that harder water bubbled more.
- **The coffee itself.** Rao notes that different coffees make very different amounts of bloom.

So a bed that barely moves is a reason to check the roast date, not a verdict. Our [coffee freshness guide](/blog/coffee-freshness-roast-to-stale/) covers what changes as beans age.

## Very fresh coffee

Coffee a day or two off the roaster blooms hard; in his 2008 handbook, Rao writes that beans roasted less than two days earlier make too much turbulence in a drip brewer. The sources differ on how much this matters for filter coffee:

- **Gagné** writes in his 2018 V60 guide that very fresh beans degas so much that they're harder to brew properly, and in his book that more gas may call for more thorough agitation during the bloom.
- **Scott Rao**, in *Espresso Extraction* (2013), says that in filter brewing most of the gas escapes into the air above the slurry: it stirs the bed up but doesn't significantly affect extraction or flavor, and he recommends very fresh beans for filter.
- **Our Freshness tool** errs on the side of caution and rests filter beans through day 3.

If a just-roasted coffee blooms violently, a thorough bloom with a good swirl is the first thing to try.

## Espresso has no bloom, but the gas still matters

You can't pause an espresso shot to let it bloom, and the gas behaves differently under pressure. Rao explains in *Espresso Extraction* that very fresh beans release gas during the shot, which pushes back against the water. To get a normal shot time you have to grind coarser, and a coarser grind lowers the extraction. Fresh beans also make more crema, so a shot looks bigger than it weighs. That's why espresso beans are usually rested longer; our [fresh bean trap guide](/blog/bean-resting-for-espresso-freshness-trap/) explains how long.

The nearest thing to a bloom on an espresso machine is preinfusion: a short, low-pressure wetting of the puck before full pressure. Rao writes that it lets the grounds swell and settle before the pressure rises, and lists fewer channels among its benefits. Our [pressure profiling guide](/blog/pressure-profiling-espresso-guide/) covers it.

## Blooming other brewers

- **Cones and flat-bottom drippers** (V60, Kalita Wave, Chemex): bloom and swirl as above. Gagné finds the finger nest helps in flat-bottom drippers too.
- **Brewers that start as an immersion** (the AeroPress, the [Clever Dripper](/blog/the-clever-dripper-better-coffee-than-pour-over/), the Hario Switch, the French press and the siphon): Gagné writes that these don't need a bloom, because the whole brew acts like one. A stir or swirl as soon as the water is in can still help every ground start extracting at once. In a French press, Rao stirs at 15 to 20 seconds to sink the frothy layer; our [French press guide](/blog/how-to-use-a-french-press-the-most-amazing-coffee-youve-ever-had/) has full recipes.
- **Drip machines:** Rao's batch-brew method stirs the grounds 5 to 10 seconds after the water starts, which he says acts as a sort of preinfusion. Some home brewers have a bloom or pre-infusion setting of their own. Our [batch brew guide](/blog/batch-brew-drip-coffee-guide/) has the details.

## If you skip it

On a pour-over, the main pour then meets a bed that is partly dry. Following Gagné's account, the water runs through the wet paths, and the dry pockets catch up late or not at all. That uneven extraction can make the same cup taste sour and bitter at once; our guide to [channeling and uneven extraction](/blog/fix-channeling-uneven-extraction/) covers the signs and fixes. Gagné adds that, in his experience, higher extraction tends to taste better when it comes from extracting every part of the bed, with no under-extracted pockets left behind.
