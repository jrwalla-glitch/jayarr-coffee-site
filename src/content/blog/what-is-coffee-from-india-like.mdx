India is the world's seventh-largest coffee producer in USDA's July 2026 forecast (ranked from USDA's table), but most of its crop is robusta, so its arabica and its specialty grades get less attention. Here's what to expect in the cup, where the coffee grows, and what the labels mean.

## What Indian coffee tastes like

Our 2019 video describes Indian coffee as having a pronounced body with mild acidity and hints of subtle earthiness or spice. In a cup of Indian single-origin coffee you might pick out nutmeg, pepper, clove or cardamom, or even hints of tropical fruit. The video calls India's single origins arguably among the best shade-grown coffees in the industry.

The sources describe the same direction more plainly. The Coffee Board of India calls its coffees typically mild and not too acidic, with a full body and a fine aroma. Hoffmann writes in *The World Atlas of Coffee* that the best coffees from India tend to be heavy, creamy and low in acidity, but rarely particularly complex. These are what an Indian coffee *can* taste like, not a promise for every bag.

The video also likens India's coffees to Indonesia's. Both countries grow mostly robusta, but there's no single Indonesian taste to match: our [Indonesian coffee guide](/blog/what-does-indonesian-coffee-taste-like/) shows how much there depends on the island and the process.

## How much India grows

The Coffee Board publishes estimates by state and district. Its figures, in tonnes of green coffee, as published when we checked on October 18, 2026:

| Estimate | Arabica | Robusta | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2025-26 final | 111,000 | 262,000 | 373,000 |
| 2026-27 post-blossom | 120,000 | 284,000 | 404,000 |

Robusta is about 70 percent of both (calculated). The 2025-26 crop is about 6.2 million 60&nbsp;kg bags (calculated). By state, the 2025-26 final estimate puts Karnataka at about 69 percent of the crop, Kerala at 22 percent and Tamil Nadu at 5 percent, with Andhra Pradesh, Odisha and the northeast making up the rest (all calculated from the Board's table).

USDA's July 2026 forecast for 2026/27 is lower than the Board's: 6.14 million bags, about 25 percent arabica (calculated), or about 3 percent of the world crop (calculated). USDA expects India to drink about a quarter of what it grows, 1.58 million bags, and export most of the rest.

## A short history

Indian coffee's origin story is a legend. Hoffmann tells it this way: a pilgrim named Baba Budan, returning from Mecca through Yemen in 1670, smuggled out seven coffee seeds, whose export was strictly controlled, and planted them in what is now the Chikmagalur district of Karnataka. The hills there are called Bababudangiri, and they're still an important growing area. Our video dates the same story to about 400 years ago.

Plantations under British rule grew in the 19th century: Sunalini Menon, a former director of quality control at the Coffee Board, dates systematic British planting to 1792, and Hoffmann describes plantations flourishing by the middle of the century. In the 1870s, he writes, rising demand for tea and the spread of leaf rust pushed many estates over to tea, but rust also spurred research into resistant varieties. The Coffee Board was set up by law in 1942 and pooled growers' coffee for sale; Menon writes that the market was liberalized in January 1996, after which growers could sell all their coffee themselves.

## Where it grows

Coffee grows mainly in the hills of the three southern states, Karnataka, Kerala and Tamil Nadu; the Coffee Board calls the Eastern Ghats and the northeastern states newly developed areas. The Coffee Board's growing-conditions table puts arabica at 1,000 to 1,500 m and robusta at 500 to 1,000 m, but its regions range more widely:

| Region (state) | Elevation | Main coffee |
|---|---|---|
| Bababudangiris (Karnataka) | 1,000–1,500 m | Arabica |
| Chikmagalur (Karnataka) | 700–1,200 m | Arabica and robusta |
| Coorg, or Kodagu (Karnataka) | 750–1,100 m | Arabica and robusta |
| Manjarabad (Karnataka) | 900–1,100 m | Arabica and robusta |
| Biligiris (Karnataka/Tamil Nadu) | 1,500–2,000 m | Arabica |
| Wayanad (Kerala) | 600–900 m | Robusta |
| Travancore (Kerala) | 400–1,600 m | Robusta |
| Nilgiris (Tamil Nadu) | 900–1,400 m | Arabica and robusta |
| Pulneys (Tamil Nadu) | 600–2,000 m | Arabica |
| Shevaroys (Tamil Nadu) | 900–1,500 m | Arabica |

Robusta dominates the two biggest districts. In the Board's 2026-27 post-blossom estimate, Kodagu (Coorg) is expected to grow more than five times as much robusta as arabica, and Chikkamagaluru about a third more robusta than arabica (both calculated); Wayanad grows only robusta.

Hoffmann gives an October-to-February harvest for every Indian region. Our [harvest calendar](/blog/coffee-harvest-calendar/) has India beside the other origins.

## Grown under shade

The Coffee Board writes that India grows all of its coffee under a two-tier mixed shade canopy of evergreen leguminous trees, with nearly 50 kinds of shade tree found on plantations, and calls India the only country that does so. The shade, it says, protects sloping soil from erosion, recycles nutrients, buffers the coffee from swings in temperature and shelters wildlife, and pepper, cardamom, vanilla, orange and banana are grown alongside the coffee. Shade isn't always diverse: Hoffmann notes a trend toward monocultures of shade trees on large farms in the Shevaroys, where silver oak is extremely common.

## The varieties

The Coffee Board names four arabicas as the most important:

- **Kents,** the earliest Indian arabica selection, made by an English planter of that name in the 1920s and popular until the 1940s because it was less susceptible to rust. A few areas still grow it.
- **S.795,** released in the 1940s from Kents and by far the most popular arabica selection, with high yields, bold beans and some tolerance of leaf rust. World Coffee Research lists it as an introgressed variety, a cross of S.288, a line carrying genes from *Coffea liberica*, with Kents. WCR rates its quality potential Very Good and its yield High; it showed high rust resistance in WCR's global trials but is considered susceptible in India, which has the world's largest concentration of rust races.
- **Cauvery,** popularly known as Catimor, a descendant of a Caturra × Timor Hybrid cross.
- **Selection 9,** from a cross of the Ethiopian arabica Tafarikela with the Timor Hybrid.

For robusta, the Board lists S.274, CxR and Peridenia. Our [varietal guide](/blog/coffee-varietal-guide/) explains where these families sit.

## Indian robusta

Robusta is the bulk of the crop, and Hoffmann writes that it's in many ways better suited to India than arabica: the lower altitudes and the climate give high yields. He also writes that more care goes into Indian robusta than in most countries, which puts it at the premium end of the market. Even the best still carries robusta's woody flavors, but the relative lack of unclean flavors makes Indian robustas popular with roasters who still use robusta in espresso blends. The Coffee Board's premium robusta grade, Robusta Kaapi Royale, is made from washed robusta from Coorg, Wayanad, Chikmagalur and Travancore, and the Board describes its cup as full-bodied, soft, smooth and mellow. Our [arabica vs robusta guide](/blog/arabica-vs-robusta-differences/) covers how the species differ.

## Monsooned Malabar

India's best-known specialty began by accident. Hoffmann writes that coffee shipped from India to Europe in wooden boxes during the British Raj sat through the wet monsoon months and absorbed a great deal of moisture, which changed how it tasted; when shipping improved, the demand remained, so the process was recreated on the west coast. Menon writes that the Coffee Board launched Monsooned Malabar and Monsooned Basanally as early as 1972.

The Coffee Board's own description of the process:

- Only dry-processed (natural) coffee is used, arabica and robusta, and only its A grade.
- It's done at curing works on the west coast from June to September, when the southwest monsoon is at its strongest.
- The coffee is spread in thick layers in airy warehouses open on all sides and raked often, then packed loosely in sacks stacked with space for the monsoon air to move between them.
- It's re-bagged often, to prevent mold and keep the monsooning even. The beans swell to nearly double their size and turn pale yellow or straw-colored.
- After 12 to 16 weeks the coffee is polished, graded by size and fumigated. The top grade is Monsooned Malabar AA.

In the cup, the Board describes a mellow but aggressive, musty flavor with reduced acidity and a heavy, syrupy flatness reminiscent of aged coffees, and says monsooned coffee is used mainly in blends to mellow and enrich rougher, more acidic coffees. Hoffmann writes that it usually loses its acidity but often gains pungent, wild flavors that divide the coffee industry: some love its richness and intensity, while others think the flavors come from defective processing. He adds that the brittle beans are hard to roast evenly, so a bag often holds beans broken in packing, which isn't a sign of a low grade.

"Monsooned Malabar" is a protected name. India's Geographical Indications Registry lists **Monsooned Malabar Arabica Coffee** (application 85, certificate 54) and **Monsooned Malabar Robusta Coffee** (application 114, certificate 55), both registered to the Coffee Board on March 28, 2008, for Karnataka and Kerala. The arabica registration runs to April 4, 2027 and the robusta to October 11, 2027.

## Reading an Indian bag

- **Species and process.** Hoffmann explains India's own grade names: "Plantation" means washed arabica, "Cherry" means natural coffee, and "Parchment" means washed robusta. Size grades run AAA, AA, A and PB (peaberry); he notes that bigger isn't necessarily better.
- **Monsooned.** Look for Monsooned Malabar AA if you want the classic style.
- **Mysore Nuggets Extra Bold,** the Board's premium washed arabica grade from Chikmagalur, Coorg, the Biligiris, the Bababudangiris and the Shevaroys, which it describes as full in aroma with medium to good body, good acidity and a hint of spice.
- **An estate or region.** Hoffmann writes that 98 percent of India's 250,000 producers are small growers, so coffee traceable to a single estate is less common and often worth seeking out.

Hoffmann notes Indian robusta's popularity in espresso blends, and the Coffee Board says monsooned coffee goes mainly into blends, so espresso is a natural way to try either.
