Most pour-over drippers are either a cone or a flat-bottomed basket. The shape changes how deep the coffee sits, how fast the water leaves and how much of it can sneak around the coffee instead of through it. This page compares the two shapes. For a full V60 recipe and technique, see our [pour-over guide](/blog/ultimate-pour-over-coffee-guide-and-best-v60-technique/).

## The two shapes

**A cone (Hario V60).** Hario says the V60's cone shape builds a thicker layer of coffee and sends the water toward the center, and its spiral ribs keep the wet paper off the wall so air can escape. It drains through one large hole instead of several small ones. Gagné notes its walls slope at 60 degrees, and Hario says that changing how fast you pour lets you make the cup stronger or lighter.

**A flat bottom (Kalita Wave).** Kalita describes a flat-bottomed coffee bed, three small holes and a fluted "wave" filter that touches the dripper very little. It says the flat base helps the water wet the grounds evenly even when the pour is uneven, and that the design stops water pooling, so, in Kalita's words, extractions are even and bitterness is dodged. Those are the maker's claims; the sections below say what has been measured.

**Bed depth.** In *The Physics of Filter Coffee*, Gagné explains that a cone makes a small dose of coffee sit deeper than it would in a flat dripper. Past a dose of about 10 g, a cone's bed depth also changes less when you change the dose slightly, which makes cones more versatile across dose sizes. A flat-bottom dripper like the Kalita is less versatile that way, because its bed depth changes more with the dose, though its angled walls help.

## Bypass: what Gagné measured

Bypass is water that leaves the dripper without passing through the whole bed of coffee. Gagné describes two kinds:

- **Complete bypass:** water runs between the filter and the dripper wall and never meets the coffee. It dilutes the cup and can lower the average extraction, because less water did the extracting. In his opinion that isn't necessarily a problem if you still get the extraction and strength you want.
- **Partial bypass:** water passes through the top of the bed, then leaves through the paper partway down the wall, along the ribs. That leaves the bottom layers under-extracted compared with the top.

His evidence comes from logging the drip rate during brews with no agitation. In a V60, the drip rate followed the textbook flow law (Darcy's law) while the water was low, but rose much faster than it predicted once the water stood above about 6 cm, in a way that matched a model of the paper cone draining freely, as if the dripper had no walls. He didn't see it in every V60 brew; bypass seemed to start at random, depending on the water's weight, the bed's resistance and how the filter sat. The same test with the bottom of an AeroPress used as a dripper followed the law, with no sign of bypass.

Two of his practical notes: bypass matters less when the filter clings to the dripper walls, for example after a thorough rinse under a strong tap, if the filter isn't moved afterwards; and pouring only into the center doesn't reduce it at all.

Flat-bottom drippers aren't immune. Gagné writes that bypass can also happen, to a lesser extent, in flat-bottom drippers with prepleated filters when the water stands tall above the coffee and the filter walls don't cling. He rates the Kalita Wave less susceptible to bypass than a V60, because of its flat bottom and gently sloped walls.

## Does a flat bottom taste sweeter?

Gagné thinks it tends to, and he's careful about why.

- **His suspicion:** partial bypass in ridged cones like the V60 is, he suspects, the major reason coffee from flat-bottom drippers seems to come out at slightly higher average extraction and tastes sweeter.
- **His side-by-side test:** in a 2020 post he brewed the same decaf coffee in a plastic V60 and in a Fellow Stagg [X], a vacuum-insulated flat-bottom dripper: 25 g of coffee at 1:17, four pours, with the kettle set so both slurries averaged 191°F&nbsp;(88°C). Both brews measured 1.34% TDS, an extraction of exactly 19.9%. Yet he found the V60 cup had almost no sweetness and an ashy aftertaste, and the Stagg [X] cup was jammy, sweet and fruity. With the slurry temperatures matched, he concluded there was something more to it than better insulation; elsewhere in the post he credits a more even flow through the whole bed. He suspected that a V60 set up to brew in the Stagg's shorter time (5:01, against the V60's 5:40) would show a slightly lower extraction.
- **Repeatability:** over his own brewing, his two-pour V60 brews repeated within about 0.2 percentage points of extraction, against 0.3–0.4 points for the Stagg [X], which he found harder to set up identically.

The matched test was one brew of each, and the Stagg [X] is insulated, unlike a steel Kalita. So the fair summary is: the flat-bottom cup tasted sweeter to Gagné at the same measured extraction, and less bypass is a plausible reason, not a proven one.

Modeling points both ways. Gagné cites a 2019 study by Moroney and colleagues which found that, in an idealized brew with no agitation and no bypass, a flat-bottom bed extracts more evenly from side to side but much less evenly from top to bottom, because the bottom always meets more concentrated water. A cone partly makes up for that, since more water passes each particle near its narrow bottom.

Easto's description in *Craft Coffee* is practical rather than measured. She writes that many professionals consider the Kalita Wave versatile and forgiving, that its flow-regulating design helps make up for user error, and that its shallow, flat bed discourages channels. By contrast, she calls the V60 more sensitive to brewing variables than other drippers, and perhaps the hardest method to master, because water can rush out of its large hole unless you pour slowly.

## Flow, grind and time

The Kalita's three small holes slow the water more than most drippers, Gagné notes, while the V60's single large hole leaves more of the pace to your grind and your pour. Easto adds that the Kalita tends to use a coarser grind than other pour-over drippers, and she recommends a pulse method for it, since its flow regulation makes a continuous pour hard near the top of its capacity.

Our Brew Guide reflects both points. It uses the same 1:16 ratio for each dripper, but grinds the Kalita and the Chemex a little coarser than the V60 and pours the Kalita differently:

| | Grind | Pour | Done |
|---|---|---|---|
| V60 | Medium-fine, about 450–650&nbsp;µm | One continuous pour after the bloom | Around 3:00–4:00 |
| Kalita Wave | Medium, about 500–700&nbsp;µm | Six short pulses after the bloom | Around 3:45 |
| Chemex | Medium, about 550–750&nbsp;µm | One continuous pour after the bloom | Around 3:30–4:30 |

The Brew Guide's recipes are adapted from Easto's, at its own ratio; its Kalita pulse times are its own schedule, since Easto gives the pulse sizes but not the times. On these recipes, a drain well outside the target time is a common sign, not a rule: read it together with the taste, and change the grind one step at a time.

## Where the Chemex fits

The Chemex is a glass cone fused to its own carafe, with smooth walls and no ribs. Gagné explains that its filter sticks to the glass, so the effective filter area is very small. That makes bypass less of an issue, except where the spout is, but it also makes the Chemex easier to clog, especially if the filter collapses into the spout and seals the carafe so air can't escape. Chemex tells you to put the three-layer side of the filter against the spout, which it says adds stability and reduces the risk of that seal.

Chemex says its paper is 20–30% thicker than most of the competition and designed to remove fats, bitterness, acidity and sediments. Gagné agrees the thick filters filter oils and fines better, with a slower flow that may call for a coarser grind than a V60. Easto adds that the Chemex isn't very forgiving: grind too fine and the water may stall. In the Brew Guide it runs a little longer than the V60, at around 3:30–4:30.

## Does the material matter?

Mostly through heat. Gagné's measurements show heavy, conductive drippers such as ceramic and glass take heat out of the slurry, while a light, insulating plastic V60 keeps it hotter; light metal drippers take little heat at first but lose it quickly. He notes that the Kalita's pleated filters trap insulating air between the folds, which helps a steel Wave. Our [dripper material comparison](/blog/dripper-material-comparison/) covers this in detail.

## Which shape to choose

| | Cone (V60) | Flat bottom (Kalita Wave) |
|---|---|---|
| Bypass (Gagné) | Can be significant, and unpredictable | Less susceptible |
| Range of doses (Gagné) | More versatile | Less versatile |
| Flow | One large hole; your grind and pour set more of the pace | Three small holes slow the water |
| Cup (Gagné's experience) | Clean; can taste less sweet | Tends to taste a little sweeter |

- **Choose a cone like the V60** if you want one dripper for a wide range of doses and you're happy to control the pour. Its sensitivity is what makes it rewarding to learn, and our [pour-over guide](/blog/ultimate-pour-over-coffee-guide-and-best-v60-technique/) walks through it.
- **Choose a flat bottom like the Kalita Wave** if you'd like the dripper to regulate more of the flow for you. Easto suggests it as a good choice for those starting out, brewed in pulses. Use Kalita's own Wave filters, sized to your dripper.
- **Choose a Chemex** if you want a heavily filtered cup and a carafe to serve from, and you'll grind a little coarser. Chemex makes it in several sizes, including the <a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Chemex+Six+Cup+Classic+Series+Glass+Coffeemaker&tag=jayarrcoffee-20" rel="sponsored noopener">Six Cup Classic</a>.

Whichever you pick, the grinder and a steady kettle matter too. Our guides to [gooseneck kettles](/blog/best-gooseneck-kettles-pour-over/), [grind size in microns](/blog/coffee-grind-size-micron-chart/) and [pour-over drippers](/blog/best-pour-over-coffee-makers/) go further.
