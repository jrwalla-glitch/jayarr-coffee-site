## What matters in a hand grinder

### How far each click moves the burr

The smaller the step, the smaller the change you can make. That matters most for espresso, where Baratza, writing about its own grinders, notes that "very small changes to the grind size" are needed to fine-tune a shot. Baratza's Encore ESP, for example, uses small steps across its espresso range and larger ones across its filter range.

Remember what the number measures: burr movement, not the size of the grounds. A 12.5 µm click on a JX-Pro and a 20 µm click on a K-Ultra are each maker's figure for its own mechanism, so you can't compare grind sizes across grinders by click count. Our [grind size micron chart](/blog/coffee-grind-size-micron-chart/) explains why.

### Finding zero

Click counts start from a zero that each maker defines in its own way:

- **Comandante.** Hold the grinder level and close the burrs: click zero is the first setting where the handle no longer falls.
- **1Zpresso.** The zero point is "where the handle starts to spin with resistance instead of the tightest position".
- **Timemore.** Turn the dial clockwise to the end; that's the start point.

### Capacity

Most hand grinders hold one or two doses: 20 g in a standard Timemore C3, 30–35 g in a JX-Pro, 40 g in a Kinu M47. If you brew for four every morning, that's several refills, and an electric grinder makes more sense.

### Burr material

Steel burrs are standard on the 1Zpresso, Timemore, Kinu and Comandante grinders here. Hario uses ceramic, and says its conical ceramic burrs transfer less heat and shave the beans rather than cracking them like a blade grinder.

## The grinders in more detail

### Timemore Chestnut C2 and C3

The C2 and C3 are Timemore's entry models. For the C2, Timemore's Chestnut C manual gives 6–12 clicks for espresso, 15–24 for pour-over and 24 for French press, counted from the start point, and calls the table "for your reference only". Note the overlap with its warning against grinding at 0–6 clicks: start at the coarse end of the espresso range.

For the C3, Timemore publishes the burr and dial specs above but no method chart that we could tie to the standard model, so the Grind Calibrator doesn't cover it. Pair either with a [coffee scale](/blog/5-coffee-scales-reviewed/) and you have the basics for weighing every brew.

### 1Zpresso JX and JX-Pro

The JX and JX-Pro share a 48 mm burr. 1Zpresso groups the JX with its pour-over grinders and the JX-Pro with its espresso grinders, and says the JX-Pro "is suitable for both pour-over and espresso". The difference is the dial: the JX-Pro moves its burr 12.5 µm per click, 40 clicks to a turn.

1Zpresso's JX-Pro chart counts in numbers, with 10 numbers to a turn and 4 clicks to a number: espresso at 12–16, AeroPress, moka pot and drip at 24–30, siphon and pour-over at 32–44, and French press at 42–50.

If I could own only one grinder, hand or electric, and I brewed both espresso and filter, the JX-Pro is one I'd seriously consider. If espresso is your main focus, check that your machine matches it; our [budget espresso machine guide](/blog/best-espresso-machines-under-500-2026-7-picks-for-every-budget/) covers that side.

### 1Zpresso K-Ultra

The K-Ultra has an external adjustment ring, 10 numbers and 100 clicks to a turn, moving the burr 20 µm per click. 1Zpresso says it suits pour-over, espresso and other methods.

### Kinu M47 Classic

Kinu's M47 Classic has 47 mm conical burrs and a stepless dial that Kinu says adjusts "as fine as 5 microns". With no clicks to count, returning to a setting means noting where the dial sits. Kinu lists it at 1,175 g, with a 40 g capacity.

### Comandante C40 MK4

Comandante makes its Nitro Blade burrs from a high-nitrogen stainless steel and publishes no burr diameter or click size. Its FAQ gives starting points, with the advice to trust your taste buds:

- 3–8 clicks for ibrik;
- 7–13 for espresso;
- 14–20 for a stovetop pot;
- 18–35 for pour-over, depending on dose and brewer;
- 25–35 for cupping and French press.

Comandante also notes that its burrs can touch at finer settings by design, because the inner cone "floats" on the axle. Pair it with a pour-over setup; our [V60 guide](/blog/ultimate-pour-over-coffee-guide-and-best-v60-technique/) covers the brewing.

### Hario Mini-Slim+

Hario's Mini-Slim+ is a compact ceramic-burr grinder that holds 24 g of grounds. Hario calls it "a great grinder for beginners" and an upgrade from the original Mini-Slim, with a new handle and shaft coupling. With no published chart, finding your setting is trial and error.

## Hand grinder or electric?

**Choose a hand grinder** if you brew one or two cups at a time, want portability, or enjoy the ritual.

**Choose an electric** if you brew for several people every day, or want speed: our [electric grinder comparison](/blog/top-5-best-electric-coffee-grinders/) covers those.

**What I actually do:** an electric grinder on weekday mornings when I'm in a rush, and a hand grinder on weekends when I want to slow down and engage with the process. Different grinders, different burrs, different cups; it isn't redundant.
