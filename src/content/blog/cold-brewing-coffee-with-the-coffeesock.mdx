## How long a CoffeeSock lasts

CoffeeSock's FAQ says a filter "can last up to a year or more," and that the company typically suggests replacing it after 9 to 12 months. Its care page puts the range at "several months to over a year," depending on how often you brew, and gives the sign to look for: "When the fabric thins or flavor is impacted, it's time to replace." Its warranty covers workmanship within what it calls the filters' "expected 9 month lifespan."

Care decides where in that range yours lands; the care steps are below. A worn-out filter can go in the compost: CoffeeSock suggests cutting it into 1-inch pieces so it breaks down faster.

On cost, CoffeeSock's 64-ounce kit (the filter and a Ball mason jar) was listed at about $30, and a replacement half-gallon filter at about $17, on its website when checked October 15, 2026. CoffeeSock says one filter can replace "500+ paper filters."

## Cold brew in a CoffeeSock: the maker's recipe

CoffeeSock's instructions call for 3 ounces of coffee by weight (about 85 g, or 1 cup dry) for every 32 ounces, ground coarse; its FAQ allows medium-coarse to coarse. For the 64-ounce jar that's 6 ounces, about 170 g (calculated, by doubling).

- **Coffee:** 170 g (6 oz), coarsely ground
- **Water:** cold and filtered, enough to fill the jar. A 64-ounce jar holds 1,893 g of water when full, and a little less with the coffee in it. That puts the ratio at about 1:11 at most (calculated: 1,893 ÷ 170).
- **Time:** about 5 minutes to set up, then 12 to 16 hours in the fridge, so up to about 16 hours in all. That's CoffeeSock's steeping chart for a 64-ounce cold brew; it gives 6 to 10 hours for a 32-ounce jar, and its brew guide says to steep in the fridge.
- **Makes:** up to about 1,550 g of cold brew, about 52 US fl oz. That's calculated: at most 1,893 g of water, less about 340 g held back by the grounds at roughly 2 g per gram of coffee, our Brew Guide's working figure.

1. If the filter is new, boil it in fresh water for 10 minutes first, to clean it and let it shrink.
2. Fit the CoffeeSock over the lip of a wide-mouth 64-ounce jar and add 170 g (6 oz) of coarsely ground coffee.
3. Wet the coffee and let it sit for 30 seconds, so the water soaks in from the center.
4. Add cold filtered water slowly, or in batches, until the jar is full.
5. Gather the top of the filter, wrap the strap around the neck and pass it through the ring. Cover the jar.
6. Steep in the fridge for 12 to 16 hours.
7. Lift the filter out and let it drain into the jar. Empty the grounds and rinse the filter straight away.

CoffeeSock says the bloom in step 3 avoids dry pockets and a weak brew. On timing, it writes that "long cold soaks produce rich and smooth brews," while warmer, shorter brews come out brighter and less extracted.

### How strong it is, and how to serve it

At about 1:11, this is lighter than a cold brew concentrate. Our Brew Guide estimates cold brew strength assuming 15–20% extraction, and on that assumption a 1:11 brew comes out around 1.3–1.8% dissolved solids (calculated: 15–20% of the coffee's weight, dissolved in about 11 times its weight of water). That's an estimate, not a measurement. It's roughly the strength of the Brew Guide's concentrate after it has been mixed one to one, about 1.2–1.6%, so you can drink it as it is, over ice, or with a splash of milk. CoffeeSock doesn't give a dilution.

Keep it covered in the fridge; CoffeeSock's brew guide says to store it refrigerated. Our [cold brew concentrate guide](/blog/cold-brew-concentrate-guide/) covers how long cold brew keeps and what the research found about longer steeps.

### If you'd rather make concentrate

The CoffeeSock works for concentrate too. Jessica Easto's recipe from that guide, 96 g of coffee to 600 g of cold water for 12 hours, fits the 32-ounce kit. Doubled, 192 g to 1,200 g, it fits the 64-ounce jar with room to spare (1,200 g of water in a jar that holds 1,893 g). Mix it one to one with water or milk to serve; the guide has the yields and serving strengths.

## Cloth, paper or metal: what passes through

CoffeeSock places its cloth filter between paper and metal mesh, but hasn't lab-tested where. Its FAQ says its filters "will remove more of the coffee oils than you would find in a French Press brew, but less than paper," and adds that "Lab testing has not been done."

That matters most for cafestol and kahweol, the coffee oils behind unfiltered coffee's effect on LDL cholesterol. Paper filters hold them back, which is why paper-filtered coffee doesn't raise cholesterol while boiled and plunger (French press) coffee do, according to a review by Rob Urgert and Martijn Katan. CoffeeSock says it can't say with certainty that its filters reduce cafestol, that it assumes the level is "somewhere in between" paper and metal, and that "No clinical tests have been performed." If you're watching your cholesterol, paper is the filter with evidence behind it; ask your clinician whether it matters for you.

## Cleaning and care

These steps are CoffeeSock's own, from its instructions, care page and FAQ.

- **After every batch:** empty the grounds promptly; don't leave them in the filter longer than it takes to drain. Compost them, turn the filter inside out to brush off the rest, rinse it well under hot water until the water runs clear, wring it out and hang it to dry. Let it dry fully between brews.
- **No soap or detergent,** and no washing machine: CoffeeSock says the residue flavors the coffee.
- **Every 6 to 8 weeks,** or when oils build up, boil the filter in fresh water for 10 minutes. A teaspoon of baking soda or distilled vinegar in the water freshens it.
- **Between uses:** there's no need to keep it in the fridge. Hang it somewhere with good airflow; CoffeeSock warns against storing it damp or in an airtight container, which can cause off-flavors, odors and mold.
- **If it does go moldy:** boil it for 10 minutes in fresh water, change the water, wring it out and boil it again, with a teaspoon of baking soda in the first boil and a teaspoon of distilled white vinegar in the second. Some stains may remain.

The first boil tightens the weave, and CoffeeSock says the filter shrinks slightly over its first few uses.

## Our verdict (2020)

The CoffeeSock made rich, smooth cold brew with a clean finish. It's more work than a paper filter: you rinse it, dry it and boil it now and then. We expected that to send it to the back of a shelf, but for big batches of cold brew it earned its place, and we were less keen on it for single cups of hot coffee, where the extra work is harder to justify.
