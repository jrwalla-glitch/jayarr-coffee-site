How much coffee does a new grinder need before it settles down? The most careful answer we know of comes from Jonathan Gagné, an astrophysicist who studies coffee. He sampled his grind every 2 pounds while breaking in a new grinder in 2019, and later summarized what he found in *The Physics of Filter Coffee*.

## What happens to new burrs

Gagné describes seasoning as the mirror image of wear. Over a burr set's working life, its teeth slowly blunt and start to crush rather than cut: the grinder makes more fines and a less even grind, and you gradually have to set it finer to get the same average size. He puts the start of that wear at roughly 4,000 to 10,000 kg of coffee, depending on the burrs and how they're used.

At the start of a burr set's life, the opposite happens. Its efficiency and grind quality get better over the first few kilograms, which Gagné thinks is likely due to small defects in the freshly made burr shapes wearing out and smoothing. In his measurements, that showed up two ways:

- **The grind got coarser at the same setting.** He attributes this to the first wear on the burr teeth. In practice, you should expect to change your best setting often after installing new burrs or a new grinder, and mostly in the finer direction.
- **The grind got more even.** The spread of particle sizes narrowed as seasoning went on, and later, he notes, it widens again as the teeth blunt.

## What Gagné measured on his EG-1

In May 2019, Gagné broke in a new EG-1 (Weber Workshops' single-dose grinder, the v2 with SSP's 80 mm ultra-low-fines burrs) with 24 pounds of coffee from his local roaster's bad roast batches. He seasoned it at a fine setting, 250 microns of burr spacing for most of it, raising the motor speed along the way so it wouldn't stall as he fed it so much coffee. After every 2 pounds, he purged the grinder and ground a small sample of one reference coffee, a washed Burundi Bourbon, always at the same setting (425 microns) and motor speed. He photographed and measured every second sample: 12 photos and about 15,000 particles each.

What he found:

- **The distribution moved to coarser sizes,** fast at first and then more slowly, like a curve approaching a plateau. By the time he stopped at 24 pounds, the shift had leveled off: he found it stopped being detectable, at his level of precision, within about 4 pounds of where he stopped.
- **The spread first widened, then narrowed.** To compare samples of different sizes, Gagné divided the average particle surface by the spread of particle surfaces, a ratio he calls the Q-factor; higher means more uniform. His EG-1's Q-factor hovered around 1.55 while the grind was shifting coarser, then climbed.
- **One sample was off-trend.** The 16-pound sample didn't fit the curve; Gagné suspects he forgot to turn the motor back down to 700 rpm before grinding it. An earlier version of this page put the plateau at 16 pounds, which misread that point.

He also listed the Q-factors he'd measured on other grinders:

| Grinder | Q-factor |
|---|---|
| A friend's Mahlkönig EK43, aligned with shims (stock burrs) | 1.45 ± 0.02 |
| Baratza Forté BG (stock burrs) | 1.53 ± 0.01 |
| His EG-1 with SSP burrs, before seasoning | 1.59 ± 0.02 |
| An older EK43 model, carefully aligned (stock burrs) | 1.61 ± 0.01 |
| His EG-1 with SSP burrs, after seasoning | 1.76 ± 0.02 |

Seasoning raised his EG-1's Q-factor by about 11 percent (calculated from 1.59 and 1.76). Gagné is careful about how far this goes: the ratio seemed roughly constant across grind sizes, at least near filter settings, and he couldn't say whether the same thing happens with all burrs and all grinders. It's one grinder, measured carefully.

## The beans moved too

After the 24 pounds, Gagné sealed some of the same Burundi coffee in an opaque, valved bag with an oxygen absorber and came back to the grinder 23 days later. Grinding a few more pounds through it changed the distribution very little, as you'd expect from burrs that were already broken in. But comparing the 23-day-old beans with his last sample from the first session, the grind had become much finer and slightly wider.

His best explanation is the beans, not the burrs: as coffee ages, it degasses and dries, and its structure may weaken, so it shatters more easily and makes more fines. He compares it with decaf, which he dialed in 0.5 coarser on his EG-1's dial (375 against 350 microns of burr spacing) than several regular coffees.

That matters if you judge seasoning by your dial: a bag getting older pushes the grind finer while new burrs push it coarser. Compare settings with similar coffee at a similar age since roast.

## How much coffee it takes

There isn't one number. Gagné writes that the amount is usually not quoted by manufacturers and depends on many variables, much like the rate of wear. In his experience:

- **Baratza Forté BG, 54 mm steel flat burrs:** about 12 pounds (5.4 kg), ground at an espresso setting.
- **Weber Workshops EG-1 v2, 80 mm SSP ultra-low-fines burrs:** 24 pounds (10.9 kg). He went that far because he'd heard SSP burrs were particularly hard to break in.

A few makers do give a figure, as published by the maker:

- **Niche** says the Niche Zero's grind quality is good from the start and reaches peak consistency after the burrs have ground about 10 kg (about 22 lb, calculated).
- **Turin** says new burrs in its DF54, DF64 Gen 2 and DF83 Gen 2 need about 10 lb (about 4.5 kg, calculated) to stabilize. It doesn't recommend wasting coffee on seasoning: use the grinder as normal and expect the inconsistency to even out.

If your grinder's manual says something, follow it. We found no measurements for ceramic burrs, coated burrs or conical burr sets, so we can't say whether they need more or less.

## How to break in new burrs

You have two reasonable choices, and both end in the same place.

1. **Just use it.** Brew as normal, as Turin suggests, and expect to change your setting over the first several bags. Nothing gets damaged; your early cups simply come from a grinder that is still changing.
2. **Grind cheap coffee through it first.** That's what Gagné did, with stale or reject beans. He seasoned at a fine setting. Grind in batches: his book warns that quickly grinding a large amount of coffee indoors can generate dangerous amounts of carbon monoxide, so do it somewhere well ventilated. Turin's guide adds that its grinders' thermal cutout can trip after heavy use; if it does, let the grinder cool for 15 to 30 minutes.

Either way, you're done when the setting that tastes right stops drifting. Dial in your usual brew, note the setting, and check it with each new bag of a similar coffee at a similar age. Early on it will creep finer; when it holds, the burrs have settled. With a refractometer you can be more precise: Gagné's test is the highest extraction yield you can reach before the cup turns astringent. When that stops rising, the burrs are done.

## Where seasoning fits among grinder variables

Seasoning is one of several things that move a grinder's output, and Gagné's book covers the others in the same chapter:

- **Alignment.** Burrs that aren't concentric and parallel grind a wider spread of sizes, Gagné writes, which is a different problem from break-in. Our [burr alignment guide](/blog/burr-alignment-guide/) covers the marker test and when shimming is worth it.
- **Wear.** Blunt burrs make more fines and need a finer setting. Niche, for one, rates the Niche Zero's burrs for more than 750 kg.
- **Motor speed.** On his EG-1, a faster speed gave a slightly finer grind at the same setting.
- **The beans.** Age, roast and decaffeination change how coffee shatters, as the 23-day test showed.
- **Burr shape and design.** Our [conical vs flat burr guide](/blog/conical-vs-flat-burr-grinders/) explains what Gagné found across 24 grinders.

If a new grinder still gives wildly different shots after its maker's break-in amount, look at those, and at technique, before grinding more coffee through it. Our guide to [dialing in espresso](/blog/how-to-dial-in-espresso/) walks through the recipe side.
