A gooseneck kettle won't change your coffee as much as your grinder does. Jonathan Gagné, an astrophysicist who studies brewing, says as much in *The Physics of Filter Coffee*, but adds that a better kettle gives you more control over how the water reaches the coffee, which helps you repeat a brew and control its time and agitation. This page covers what the kettle actually changes, then compares four kettles from their makers' published specs, grouped by what each suits.

## What a gooseneck changes

**It stops the water clinging to the spout.** Gagné explains that a traditional kettle's wide spout makes a gentle, controlled pour difficult: water tends to run along the spout and the kettle's outside instead of falling cleanly, the aptly named teapot effect. A thin, long gooseneck with a well-shaped tip reduces it for all but the slowest pours. A water-repellent coating on the tip removes it entirely; when he wrote in 2021, he knew of only Fellow's pour-over kettles using one.

**It keeps the stream smooth for longer.** Any stream eventually breaks into droplets. Gagné calls the distance before that happens the breakup length, and found that each kettle has a pour rate at which it's longest. Thinner goosenecks reach their longest breakup at slower pour rates, and smoother tips give longer breakups overall.

**It controls agitation.** A smooth stream that lands just before it would break up stirs the coffee bed most deeply. His practical method: pick a pour rate, start low, raise the kettle until you begin to hear a slight splatter, then lower it a little.

A 2025 study from the University of Pennsylvania looked inside the cone. Ernest Park, Margot Young and Arnold Mathijssen replaced coffee with transparent silica gel particles in a glass cone, filmed with a laser sheet and a high-speed camera, and found that even a gentle pour-over jet set off small avalanches of particles that mixed the bed strongly at various pour heights. Brews with real coffee, measured for dissolved solids, led them to suggest that extraction can be tuned through flow rate and pour height. Penn's report quotes Park: with a regular kettle, "it's a little bit hard to control where the flow goes."

## How fast a gooseneck can pour

None of the four makers below publishes a flow rate. The only independent measurement we found is Gagné's. He poured room-temperature water at different rates, weighing it live on an Acaia Pearl S scale, raised the kettle until he heard the stream start to splatter, and measured the drop from the spout's tip to the water below. The usable pour ranges he found were:

| Kettle (as Gagné measured it) | Usable pour rate |
|---|---|
| Fellow Stagg EKG, 0.6 L | 1–18 g/s |
| Brewista Artisan, 0.6 L | 2–35 g/s |
| Brewista Artisan, 0.6 L, with a flow restrictor | 2–15 g/s |

His data suggested the Stagg EKG gave a slightly smoother stream. He notes that breakup lengths may shorten with hotter water than he used, and his Stagg EKG was the 0.6-liter model, not the 0.9-liter EKG Pro that Fellow now sells.

For scale, the Brew Guide's V60 at 22 g pours from 56 g at 0:45 to 352 g by 2:30, which averages about 2.8 g a second (calculated: 296 g in 105 seconds). That sits near the slow end of the Stagg EKG's measured range, the relatively slow pours at which, Gagné explains, thin goosenecks like the Stagg's keep their stream smooth longest.

## Temperature: what the kettle setting means

The water cools on its way into the coffee, so the slurry in the cone is cooler than the kettle. In Gagné's measurements, a plastic V60 brewed with the kettle at 200°F&nbsp;(93°C) averaged 182°F&nbsp;(84°C) in the cone, and 191°F&nbsp;(88°C) with the kettle at 210°F&nbsp;(99°C). Our Brew Guide starts pour-over at 205°F&nbsp;(96°C) for light roasts, 201°F&nbsp;(94°C) for medium and 196°F&nbsp;(91°C) for dark, and our [brewing temperature guide](/blog/whats-the-ideal-coffee-brewing-temperature/) explains the gap.

Three of Gagné's points apply to any kettle:

- **Check the display once.** He recommends putting a thermometer in the kettle at least once to see how reliable its temperature reading is and how steady the water stays after you lift the kettle off its base.
- **Water near boiling cools fastest,** so insulation matters most when you brew close to the boil. Larger kettles hold heat better, but a heavier kettle is harder to pour steadily.
- **Mind your altitude.** Water boils at a lower temperature higher up, about 203°F&nbsp;(95°C) at 5,000 feet in the NOAA figures he reproduces, so don't set a kettle above your local boiling point.

Hario gives the Buono's practical capacity; the three electric kettles can also hold the water at temperature.

### For a stovetop: Hario V60 Buono

Hario says the Buono's slim spout makes it easy to control how much water you pour and how fast. The 1.2-liter Buono has a practical capacity of 800 ml, and Hario's manual says to fill it only to that level, since its narrow spout and opening can let boiling water overflow. Its body and lid are stainless steel and its handle phenol resin, and it's made in Japan. The manual calls it compatible with all heat sources except a microwave or an oven, with notes for gas and induction. Hario's store listed it at $65, and a 1-liter Buono, with a 600 ml practical capacity, at $60.

It has no thermometer, so pair it with one if you brew to a set temperature. To buy: Hario's own store, or the <a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Hario+V60+Buono+Drip+Kettle+1.2L&tag=jayarrcoffee-20" rel="sponsored noopener">Hario V60 Buono 1.2 L</a> on Amazon.

### For simple presets: COSORI Original Electric Gooseneck Kettle

COSORI's CO108-NK holds 0.8 liters and draws 1,200 W. Its five presets are 170, 180, 195, 205 and 212°F, and it can hold the water at temperature for up to 60 minutes. COSORI says the kettle, lid and spout are food-grade stainless steel, so the water doesn't touch plastic, and that it shuts off if less than about ½ cup (118 ml) of water is left. Its store listed it at $69.99.

The 205°F preset matches the Brew Guide's light-roast start. For its medium and dark starts, 201°F and 196°F, the nearest presets are 205 and 195°F, or boil and let it rest. To buy: COSORI's store, or the <a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=COSORI+Electric+Gooseneck+Kettle+CO108-NK&tag=jayarrcoffee-20" rel="sponsored noopener">COSORI gooseneck kettle (CO108-NK)</a> on Amazon.

### To set any temperature: Greater Goods Electric Gooseneck Kettle

Greater Goods lists a 1-liter capacity, 1,200 W, exact temperatures in °F or °C, a timer and a hold function, a counterbalanced handle and a stainless-steel build. Its store listed it at $79.99. Greater Goods' own page doesn't give the temperature range or how long it holds. Its Amazon listings give a 0.8-liter capacity, so we can't confirm they're the same kettle; buy it from [Greater Goods' store](https://shop.greatergoods.com/products/gooseneck-kettle-onyx-black) if the capacity matters to you.

### For the most features: Fellow Stagg EKG Pro

Fellow's US store now sells the Stagg EKG as the Pro and the Pro Studio Edition. Fellow lists a 0.9-liter (30 oz) capacity, 304 stainless steel, 1,000–1,200 W and a range of 104–212°F (40–100°C), set to the degree. The hold keeps water hot for 15, 30, 45 or 60 minutes. It can also be scheduled to heat at a set time, and it has a brew stopwatch, preset temperatures by brew method and an altitude setting to prevent boiling over. Fellow listed the Pro at $179.95 in matte black or white and $199.95 with wooden accents, with the Studio Edition from $199.95. A refurbished Stagg EKG, sold as Rebrew, was listed at $132.

Gagné's pour measurements above were made on an earlier, 0.6-liter Stagg EKG, so they show how that gooseneck behaved, not a published spec for the Pro. To buy: Fellow's store, or the <a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Fellow+Stagg+EKG+Pro+Electric+Kettle&tag=jayarrcoffee-20" rel="sponsored noopener">Fellow Stagg EKG Pro</a> on Amazon.

## Choosing

- **Stovetop and a thermometer** if you want the simplest kettle and don't mind checking the temperature yourself: the Hario Buono.
- **Presets** if your recipes sit near 195 or 205°F: the COSORI.
- **To the degree** if you brew to a recipe's exact temperature: the Greater Goods or the Fellow Stagg EKG Pro, which adds scheduling, a timer and a choice of hold times.

Pair any of them with a [burr grinder](/blog/top-5-best-electric-coffee-grinders/), a [scale](/blog/5-coffee-scales-reviewed/) and a [dripper that suits you](/blog/best-pour-over-coffee-makers/).
