Ask which coffee has the most caffeine and most people say espresso. Per sip, they're right. Per drink, they usually aren't, because a shot is about an ounce and a mug of drip is eight or more. Keeping the amount in the cup apart from how concentrated it is clears up most of the confusion.

## How much caffeine is in each cup

The amounts below are our [Caffeine Calculator](/tools/caffeine/)'s estimates, rounded to the nearest 5 mg as on the calculator's own page; decaf is the FDA's range, in whole milligrams. It starts from the grams of coffee the Brew Guide would use for that cup, assumes arabica beans unless you choose robusta or a blend, and gives a range rather than a single number. The last column is calculated from the first two, so you can compare strength.

| Drink | Serving | Caffeine | Per 100 ml |
|---|---|---|---|
| Drip machine | 8 fl oz | 105–195 mg | 45–80 mg |
| Pour-over | 8 fl oz | 115–210 mg | 50–90 mg |
| French press | 8 fl oz | 105–195 mg | 45–80 mg |
| Cold brew, diluted 1:1 | 16 fl oz | 205–445 mg | 45–95 mg |
| Moka pot | 2 fl oz | 50–90 mg | 85–155 mg |
| Espresso | 1 shot | 60–75 mg | 200–255 mg |
| Double shot or latte | 2 shots | 120–150 mg | depends on the milk |
| Decaf | 8 fl oz | 2–15 mg | about 1–6 mg |

Pour-over covers the V60, Chemex and Kalita Wave. In milliliters, 8 fl oz is 237 ml, 16 fl oz is 473 ml and 2 fl oz is 59 ml; a shot is about 1 fl oz (30 ml). The moka figure is the coffee itself, before any water or milk goes in.

These ranges are higher than some published averages. The USDA's figures for brewed coffee, 40 mg per 100 g, work out to about 95 mg in an 8 fl oz cup (calculated). The FDA's table puts a 12 fl oz cup of regular brewed coffee at 113 to 247 mg, which is about 75 to 165 mg for 8 fl oz (calculated). The calculator assumes the Brew Guide's dose, about 16 to 18 g of coffee for an 8 fl oz cup. If you brew with less coffee, expect less caffeine, in proportion.

A café cup can be larger and stronger than any of these. If a chain publishes the caffeine for your drink, the calculator's "I know the mg" option takes that figure as it is.

## Espresso versus drip: amount and concentration

Espresso is far more concentrated than brewed coffee. By the USDA's figures, espresso carries 212 mg of caffeine per 100 g and brewed coffee 40 mg, so espresso is about five times as concentrated (calculated). But a single shot is about an ounce, so it holds less caffeine than a mug of drip.

The arithmetic matters if you're cutting back. On the calculator's numbers, a 16 fl oz drip coffee carries about 215–390 mg and a single espresso 60–75 mg. Swapping one for the other cuts that drink's caffeine by roughly two-thirds or more (calculated). A latte or cappuccino made with a double shot lands at 120–150 mg: the milk adds volume, not caffeine. Our guide to [ristretto and lungo shots](/blog/whats-different-about-ristretto-or-lungo-espresso-shots/) covers how shot length changes the cup.

## Cold brew depends on the dilution

Cold brew is usually made as a concentrate with a lot of coffee and then diluted, so its caffeine depends on both steps. The calculator's figure assumes the Brew Guide's concentrate, 1 part coffee to 6.25 parts water, diluted one to one. At that strength, a 16 fl oz glass comes out at 205–445 mg, more than most single drinks in the table, mostly because it's a big glass. Per 100 ml it's in the same range as drip.

Drink the concentrate less diluted and you get more; dilute it further and you get less. Our [cold brew concentrate guide](/blog/cold-brew-concentrate-guide/) covers the ratios.

## Light or dark roast?

Jessica Easto writes in *Craft Coffee* that roasting doesn't change how much caffeine is in a bean. What changes is how you measure: darker beans are lighter, so a weighed dose holds a few more of them, while lighter beans are smaller, so a scoop holds more. Lab studies don't all agree with the first point: in a 2011 study, Hečimović and colleagues measured the most caffeine in a light-roasted robusta and less as it was roasted darker. Either way, weighing your coffee and keeping the dose steady matters more than the roast. The calculator doesn't adjust for roast.

Easto also notes that robusta beans carry about twice the caffeine of arabica. Adriana Farah's tables put arabica at 0.9–1.3% of the bean's weight and robusta at 1.5–2.5%, and the calculator uses those figures. It assumes arabica unless you set Beans to robusta or a blend: an 8 fl oz drip coffee comes to 105–195 mg with arabica, 175–375 mg with robusta and 115–220 mg with a blend of 15% robusta.

## Decaf isn't zero

The FDA says "decaf coffee typically has 2 to 15 milligrams in an 8-fluid-ounce cup," and the calculator uses that range. Easto gives a narrower figure, 3 to 6 mg per 6 fl oz, about 4 to 8 mg per 8 fl oz (calculated), which sits inside the FDA's. Either way, several cups add up. Our guide to [how coffee is decaffeinated](/blog/how-is-coffee-decaffeinated/) explains the processes.

Coffee isn't the only source in a day, either. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) lists a 50 g bar of plain chocolate at about 25 mg of caffeine. Chocolate also carries theobromine, a related compound; our sister site explains <a href="https://jayarrchocolate.com/blog/cacao-percentage-what-it-means/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">what the cacao percentage on a bar tells you</a>.

## How much caffeine is too much?

These are the agencies' own words. They describe populations, not a personal limit, and none of them is medical advice for you.

- **Most adults (FDA):** "For most adults, the FDA has cited 400 milligrams a day — that's about two to three 12-fluid-ounce cups of coffee — as an amount not generally associated with negative effects." The FDA adds that "there is wide variation in both how sensitive people are to the effects of caffeine and how fast they eliminate it from the body."
- **In one sitting (EFSA):** "Single doses of caffeine up to 200mg … do not raise safety concerns for the general healthy adult population."
- **Over a day (EFSA):** "Intakes up to 400mg per day … consumed throughout the day do not raise safety concerns for healthy adults in the general population, except pregnant women."
- **Pregnancy:** The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) says that "moderate caffeine consumption (less than 200 mg per day) does not appear to be a major contributing factor in miscarriage or preterm birth," and that "the relationship of caffeine to growth restriction remains undetermined." EFSA says intakes up to 200 mg a day "do not raise safety concerns for the foetus." The World Health Organization recommends that pregnant women with "high daily caffeine intake (more than 300 mg per day)" lower it, "to reduce the risk of pregnancy loss and low birth weight neonates."
- **Children and teens:** The FDA reports that "medical experts advise against energy drinks for children and teens," citing the American Academy of Pediatrics. EFSA proposes 3 mg per kilogram of body weight a day as a safety level for children and adolescents.

If you're pregnant, trying to become pregnant or breastfeeding, take medicines, or have a heart, anxiety or sleep condition, ask your clinician what's right for you. The FDA makes the same recommendation for pregnancy and for anyone "concerned about a condition or medication."

## How long it stays in you

Caffeine is absorbed quickly and leaves gradually. A 2001 Institute of Medicine review reports that about 99% is absorbed within 45 minutes and that blood levels peak between 15 and 120 minutes after drinking. After that, the body clears a fixed share each hour, so the amount halves over a set time, the half-life. That time varies from person to person. The calculator uses 5 hours as typical and lets you pick 3 or 8 hours instead. On that model, a 200 mg coffee at 3 pm leaves about 100 mg at 8 pm and about 50 mg at 1 am (calculated with the calculator's model).

Why people clear caffeine at such different speeds is in our [caffeine metabolism guide](/blog/caffeine-metabolism-explained/). What that means for your last cup of the day is in [coffee and sleep](/blog/coffee-and-sleep-when-to-stop/).
