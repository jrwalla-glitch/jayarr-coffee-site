Peruvian coffee is easy to drink without noticing. For years its mild cup and fairly low price made it, as the importer Café Imports puts it, "an appealing blend component," and James Hoffmann noted in 2018 that some Peruvian coffee is bought, blended with other coffee and resold on its way to the coast for export. Single-farm and competition lots are now changing that picture. Here's where Peru's coffee grows, what it tends to taste like and what the "organic" on so many Peruvian bags does and doesn't tell you.

## How much coffee Peru grows

USDA forecasts 4.78 million 60 kg bags for 2026/27 (Peru's coffee year runs April to March), all of it arabica and practically unchanged from 2025/26. That's about 2.5 percent of world production and the ninth-largest total on USDA's table (both calculated). Peru exports almost all of it: about 4.55 million bags, with the United States taking 32 percent of exports in 2024/25, Germany 16 percent and Belgium 11 percent. Peruvians drink only about 6 percent of the crop, three-quarters of that as instant coffee.

According to USDA's Lima office, an estimated 223,000 families rely on coffee as their main income, in 16 of the country's 25 regions, and more than 90 percent of the coffee comes from smallholders on plots under 5 hectares. Most of it is shade-grown, hand-picked and sun-dried.

## A short history

Hoffmann writes that coffee reached Peru between 1740 and 1760 but was drunk locally for about a century; exports, to Germany and England, began only in 1887. In the early 1900s Peru defaulted on a British loan and handed over two million hectares in central Peru, a quarter of which became plantations, coffee among them. Laws under Juan Velasco's government in the 1970s crippled growth. With sales and prices guaranteed under the International Coffee Agreement, little infrastructure had been built, and when state support was withdrawn the industry fell into disarray; the Shining Path's guerrilla war then destroyed crops and drove farmers off their land. Hoffmann credits non-government organizations such as Fair Trade with filling the gap since.

## Growing regions

Peru's coffee grows on the eastern slopes of the Andes. USDA lists the main regions by share of production, and Hoffmann gives the altitudes for four of them; he puts the harvest in all four at March to September.

| Region | Share of production (USDA) | Altitude (Hoffmann) |
|---|---|---|
| Cajamarca | 22% | 900–2,050 m (3,000–6,750 ft) |
| San Martín | 20% | 1,100–2,000 m (3,600–6,600 ft) |
| Junín (including Chanchamayo) | 19% | 1,400–1,900 m (4,600–6,200 ft) |
| Amazonas | 15% | — |
| Cusco | — | 1,200–1,900 m (3,900–6,200 ft) |

USDA adds that about 75 percent of Peru's coffee grows between 1,000 and 1,800 m.

### Chanchamayo and Junín

Chanchamayo is a province of the Junín region. Coffees from Peru, the coffee brand of Peru's export promotion agency, PromPerú, writes that coffee reached Chanchamayo in the 19th century and calls it "the coffee-growing area par excellence." Hoffmann writes that Junín's coffee grows among the rainforest and produced 20 to 25 percent of Peru's coffee when he wrote; USDA now puts it at 19 percent. The region suffered from guerrilla activity in the 1980s and 1990s, when neglected trees let disease spread, and he writes that its coffee industry had to be restarted almost from nothing in the late 1990s.

### Cajamarca

Cajamarca covers the northern end of Peru's Andes, and it's now the largest producer. Café Imports says it grows "far and away the best-cupping lots" in Peru. Hoffmann notes that its producers, mostly smallholders, are often well organized: one producers' organization there, CENFROCAFE, works with about 1,900 families. Peru's first Cup of Excellence, in 2017, was won by Juan Heredia Sánchez of Jaén, Cajamarca, with a washed Caturra and Bourbon grown at 1,800 m, and the 2023 winner was a Geisha from the Alto Mirador farm, also in Jaén.

### San Martín

In San Martín, on the eastern side of the Andes, Hoffmann writes that many farmers work plots of 5 to 10 hectares. It was once Peru's main coca-growing area, and cooperatives there now promote growing other crops, such as cacao and honey, alongside coffee.

### Cusco

In Cusco, in the south, Hoffmann describes coffee as in some ways the legal alternative to coca, grown mostly by smallholders. Cusco growers have won the Cup of Excellence too: Dwight Aguilar in 2018 and 2021, and Hilda Leguia in 2020.

Our [coffee harvest calendar](/blog/coffee-harvest-calendar/) uses Hoffmann's March-to-September window for Peru and gives Café Imports' typical US arrivals, January to June. USDA's Lima office puts the harvest at April to September, with the peak in June and July and about 85 percent picked by the end of July.

## Varieties and leaf rust

USDA reports that more than 70 percent of Peru's coffee is Typica, about 20 percent Caturra, and the rest other varieties. Hoffmann lists Bourbon, Typica, Caturra, Pache, Mundo Novo, Catuai and Catimor in every region. World Coffee Research (WCR) rates Typica "Very Good" for quality potential at high altitude and Caturra, Pache and Mundo Novo "Good," and rates all four susceptible to leaf rust; for Peru, it recommends Pache above 1,400 m and Mundo Novo above 1,500 m.

That reliance on Typica matters. Hoffmann linked Peru's growing rust problem partly to widespread Typica, and USDA reports that an outbreak that began in 2013 now affects nearly 40 percent of the crop, along with a coffee berry borer infestation since 2020, worst below 1,500 m. In USDA's series, Peru's crop fell from 4.25 million bags in 2013/14 to 2.90 million in 2014/15, about a third lower (calculated), before recovering. Our [coffee varietal guide](/blog/coffee-varietal-guide/) explains how these varieties are related, and our history of [coffee leaf rust](/blog/coffee-leaf-rust-hemileia-vastatrix-history/) covers the epidemic.

## Processing

Peru's coffee is mostly washed. USDA describes how it usually works: farmers pulp, ferment and dry the coffee themselves, or sell the cherries to local processing centers, then sell the dried parchment to local buyers, cooperatives or exporters. Hoffmann sees infrastructure as Peru's main obstacle to top quality: few mills sit close to farms, so coffee can travel longer than it should before processing. Our [guide to coffee processing methods](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/) explains what washing does to the cup.

## Organic and Fair Trade

USDA calls Peru the world's leading exporter of organic coffee, with about 90,000 hectares certified, roughly a quarter of the 375,000 hectares planted with coffee (calculated). It adds that many more hectares are effectively organic because small growers can't afford chemical inputs, and that producers also pursue Fair Trade, Rainforest Alliance and Starbucks' C.A.F.E. Practices certification. Fair Trade, Hoffmann notes, can only apply to coffee from a cooperative.

Organic isn't a quality grade, though. Hoffmann writes that Peru's strong organic culture "rarely yields higher cup quality," and that its organic coffees are often so cheap they pull down the prices paid to other farmers. Treat the label as a statement about farming, and look for a region, cooperative or farm to judge the coffee. Our guide to [organic coffee](/blog/the-truth-about-organic-coffee-and-best-coffees/) explains what the certification requires.

## What Peruvian coffee tastes like

- **Hoffmann:** Peruvian coffees have typically been clean but a little soft and flat: sweet and fairly heavy-bodied, but not very complex. He adds that more distinctive, juicier coffees are increasingly available.
- **Importers:** the Apex Coffee Imports description Coffee Origins uses lists stone fruit, nuts, chocolate, caramel and citrus, mainly for washed specialty lots.
- **Roasters' notes:** across 15 Peruvian lots that roasters described from January to March 2026, our Coffee Origins sample found raisin, caramel and orange most often (four lots each), then brown sugar, pecan and other nuts, and cherry.
- **The top end:** the 2017 Cup of Excellence jury described the winning Cajamarca lot with notes from green apple and pineapple to apricot and kumquat, and a silky body.

So an everyday Peruvian coffee can be a sweet, nutty, chocolatey cup that's easy to drink black, while a named lot from Cajamarca or Cusco can be far brighter. These are what Peruvian coffee *can* taste like, not a promise about any one bag.

## What to look for on a bag

- **A region:** Cajamarca, San Martín, Junín (or Chanchamayo), Amazonas or Cusco, rather than just "Peru."
- **A cooperative or farm.** Hoffmann says the best Peruvian coffees can be traced to a producer group or a single estate.
- **The variety,** if it's listed. Most Peruvian coffee is Typica or Caturra.
- **Certifications as farming claims,** not taste claims: organic and Fair Trade are common.
- **A recent roast date.** Our guide to [coffee freshness](/blog/coffee-freshness-roast-to-stale/) explains how long a bag stays at its best.

Neighboring Bolivia grows a tiny fraction of Peru's coffee on the same Andean slopes; our [Bolivian coffee guide](/blog/bolivia-coffee-rare-hidden-gem/) compares them.
