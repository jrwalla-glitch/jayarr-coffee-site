The coffee shelf has more choice than ever: single origins from dozens of countries, unusual processing, and bags covered in tasting notes. It can feel paralyzing. But here's what I've learned: finding your ideal coffee isn't about memorizing a hundred flavor descriptors. It's about building a simple personal framework: what do I like, what don't I like, and what should I try next?

In May 2021, James Hoffmann published [“A Beginner's Guide to Buying Great Coffee”](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O9YnLFrM7Fs) on his YouTube channel. Overall I thought it was very good and informative, but I didn't think it did a very good job of explaining the differences in coffee notes, or how a preference for some flavors might translate into coffees to try. So our June 2021 video uses the SCA flavor wheel to group the flavors people tend to prefer and seek out. The SCA Flavor Wheel is the best tool I've found for doing exactly that.

## What Hoffmann recommends

Hoffmann opens by disclosing his biases: a strong preference for specialty coffee, and part ownership of a coffee company, whose products aren't in the video. His guide has three parts.

**The golden rules** (from 1:50 in his video):

- **Buy fresh.** Specialty roasters usually print a roast date; supermarkets want a best-before date instead, often 12, 18 or even 24 months after roasting. Very fresh coffee is harder to brew as espresso, so many people rest it 7 to 10 days or longer; for filter, a day or two is fine, and a coffee can be at its best anywhere from about a week to six weeks. He always advocates whole beans over ground, which he says has a day or two at its best once the bag is open. His final guidance: light to medium roasts are generally fine four to eight weeks after roasting, depending on storage, and darker roasts four to six weeks at most.
- **Buy traceable**, from as specific a place as you can: a farm, a cooperative or at least a region. Keeping a coffee separate from farm to cup costs money, so it only happens with coffee good enough to earn a premium, which makes traceability a "nice little shortcut to quality".
- **Buy seasonally.** Countries harvest at different times, and a roaster may carry a coffee for weeks or months. Look for sellers whose range changes reasonably often: it means the green coffee is fresh.

**Where to buy** (from 8:03): the supermarket is convenient and usually cheapest, but freshness is the trade-off; a coffee shop offers advice and coffee that's often five to seven days old, with a chance to taste before you buy; buying online from a roaster gives the most variety and often coffee roasted to order. He likes subscriptions, plus a second bag from somewhere else, because tasting different coffees is fun.

**Decoding the bag** (from 12:52): supermarket "strength" numbers stand in for roast degree, and a specialty bag that says nothing about roast is light to medium. The lighter the roast, the more acidity; the darker, the more bitterness and the less acidity. Tasting notes do two jobs, describing the coffee and selling it, and roasters arrive at them by tasting coffees against each other, which is why the notes can be hard to find in a cup tasted on its own. The most expensive coffees, he adds, tend to be the most unusual.

He can't know what you'll like, he says, but he can help you avoid what people often dislike, and this is the most useful part of the video. His rules of thumb for reading a bag:

| If the bag says… | Hoffmann expects… |
|---|---|
| Nothing about texture | A medium body |
| Delicate, elegant, tea-like or floral | A light body |
| Creamy or rich | A fuller body |
| Berries or citrus | A lot of acidity |
| Apple or pear | A good amount of acidity |
| Cooked fruit: jammy, candied orange | A little acidity |
| No fruit: caramel, treacle, nuts, chocolate | Very low acidity |
| Natural or dry process; tropical fruit, pineapple, mango, strawberry | Fermented-fruit flavors |

In his experience, around 40% of people dislike those fermented-fruit flavors, and he suggests they avoid natural-process coffees unless the bag promises a clean cup.

## Our response: start from the flavors you like

Hoffmann's rules help you avoid a bad bag. The flavor wheel helps you look for a good one. Roasters describe coffee with a fairly standard set of words, and the SCA and World Coffee Research organized them on the Coffee Taster's Flavor Wheel, redrawn in 2016 from WCR's Sensory Lexicon. The most general words are at the center, such as fruity, sweet, roasted and spices, and they get more specific toward the rim, out to notes like cherry, cinnamon or even peapod.

The SCA's own guide says to start at the center and work outward, stopping wherever you like. Fruity first; then, if berry and citrus don't fit, other fruit; then perhaps apple. Our guide to [training your palate](/blog/train-your-coffee-palate/) explains the wheel and the lexicon behind it.

In the video, I grouped the wheel into three sets of notes, plus a few warning signs.

### Crowd pleasers: nutty, chocolate, sweet

The nutty and cocoa part of the wheel holds hazelnut and chocolate, and you'd be hard pressed to find anyone offended by them in a coffee. The sweet notes are close behind. One of them, vanillin, isn't quite vanilla; the lexicon describes it as an extremely sweet aromatic associated with vanilla, cotton candy and marshmallows. Cereal notes often pair well with chocolatey and sweet ones.

On Hoffmann's rules, a bag that lists only these non-fruit words should have low acidity. In the video I tried Peet's Coffee Finder with these notes and a French press, since I think immersion brewers do well with them, and it suggested a Brazil peaberry described with milk chocolate and hazelnut, among the notes I'd picked.

### Bright and acidic: citrus and sour

This set is more of an acquired taste: the sour and citrus-fruit area of the wheel. The sour category holds the acidic notes, such as acetic and malic acid, and citrus fruit holds the flavors people most often connect with acidity: lemon, lime, grapefruit. In Hoffmann's terms, these are the fresh-fruit words that signal a lot of acidity.

In the video I tried Crema.co's recommender, chose pour-over, since it's a great way to enjoy high-acidity coffees, and a light roast, as darker roasts tend to destroy acidic flavors. I chose no milk, since milk often doesn't pair well with acidity, then a fruity coffee with citrus acidity at a high acidity level, and it suggested several coffees with citrus acidity. Hoffmann's book says paper filters give the cleanest cup, and to us that suits these flavors.

### Unusual notes: woody, musty, earthy

Many coffee drinkers assume woody or musty and earthy notes are bad, but some coffees, such as Sumatra, are known for them. We reviewed seven [Sumatra coffees](/blog/7-sumatra-coffees-reviewed-best-and-worst/) and judged which was best; Peach Coffee Roasters' Sumatra Lintong, with cedar and sage up front, came out on top. Hoffmann writes that semi-washed, or wet-hulled, coffees like these have lower acidity, more body, and flavors such as wood, earth, mustiness, spice, tobacco and leather, and that the industry strongly disagrees about whether that's desirable. Our [Indonesian coffee guide](/blog/what-does-indonesian-coffee-taste-like/) explains why.

### Warning signs

In the video I flagged a few notes that can point to a problem. Smoky and ashy flavors can be fine, but a coffee that tastes like an ashtray was probably over-roasted. Petroleum and medicinal notes almost always point to a mistake in processing; the SCA's current cupping standard counts phenolic flavors, which include medicinal ones, as one of only three defects. Vegetative and under-ripe notes can mean the cherries were picked before they were ripe, or the coffee was under-roasted.

## Putting the two together

1. **Pick a group of notes** you think you'll like: nutty and chocolate, bright and citrus, or woody and earthy.
2. **Read bags with Hoffmann's rules.** Fruit words mean acidity; natural process means fermented-fruit flavors; texture words tell you the body.
3. **Buy two coffees from that group** and taste them side by side, brewed the same way. Hoffmann's point about comparative tasting cuts both ways: the roaster's notes came from comparing coffees, so you'll find them more easily if you compare too.
4. **Change one thing next time:** the same origin with a different process, or the same process from a different country.
5. **After five or six coffees, try a neighboring group,** such as a coffee with both chocolate and fruit notes. Later, try the group you've been avoiding.

Our guides to [reading a coffee bag label](/blog/how-to-read-coffee-bag-label/) and [cupping at home](/blog/how-to-cup-coffee-at-home/) cover the rest of the label and a way to compare several coffees at once.
