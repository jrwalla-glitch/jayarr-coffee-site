James Hoffmann includes Vietnam in *The World Atlas of Coffee* even though most of its coffee is robusta, because of the impact it has had on every coffee-producing country in the world. This page is about Vietnam as an origin. For the brewing method, see our [Vietnamese phin guide](/blog/vietnamese-phin-guide/).

## How Vietnam became a coffee giant

The French brought coffee to Vietnam in 1857, Hoffmann writes. Robert Thurston's *Coffee: A Comprehensive Guide* says it was planted by French landowners or, in some accounts, missionaries. The importer Sucafina says the first coffee planted was arabica, and that robusta didn't arrive until 1908. Hoffmann writes that coffee didn't become a commercial success until around 1910, and that the Vietnam War interrupted it. After the war the industry was collectivized, and production stayed small, at around 5,000 to 7,000 tonnes.

The change came with the Doi Moi reforms of 1986, which allowed private businesses in commodity crops. Several things then pushed Vietnam into coffee, according to Thurston:

- **The market.** The International Coffee Agreement, which had set export quotas, collapsed in 1989. Bad weather in Brazil drove prices up in 1994 and again in 1997, which drew the government and farmers further into coffee.
- **State support.** After 1991, state importers brought in fertilizer below world prices. Farmers got subsidized land and loans at rates as low as 1 percent, and in some cases seedlings and irrigation. Most land moved from state farms to private farmers on long leases.
- **Low costs.** Vietnam had some of the world's lowest production costs and highest robusta yields, and plenty of cheap labor.

The World Bank is often blamed for the boom. Thurston quotes the Bank's 2004 reply: Vietnam's expansion began well before 1994, the Bank resumed rural lending only after 1996, and none of its investments was designed to promote coffee. He concludes that the Bank's contribution "should be placed fairly far down on any list."

The numbers moved fast:

- **1996–2000:** Hoffmann writes that production doubled.
- **1997/98:** Thurston has Vietnam fourth in the world, at 5.8 million bags.
- **2000:** Vietnam was second, behind only Brazil, at a high point of about 900,000 tonnes (Hoffmann).

The boom had a human cost. Most of the new coffee was planted in the Central Highlands. There, Mark Pendergrast writes in *Uncommon Grounds*, the indigenous peoples, among them the Rhadé, Jarai, Bahnar, Stieng, Koho and Mnong, lost their land. Many ended up working for very low wages on coffee farms run by the state or by Vietnamese who had moved to the highlands. Thurston notes that the government also saw coffee as a way to move more ethnic Vietnamese onto the land.

## The price crash

All that new robusta arrived in a market that didn't need it. Hoffmann writes that Vietnam's surge caused a global oversupply and a price crash, and that it hit arabica too, because the largest buyers wanted a commodity product rather than a quality one. Thurston adds that the largest importers had learned to take some of the bitterness out of robusta by forcing steam through the beans, so they could use more of it.

Pendergrast describes what followed. Green coffee fell below a dollar a pound in 1999, then to 50 cents a pound by 2001, far below what it cost to grow. Farmers across the coffee world abandoned their trees, and prices didn't climb back above a dollar until the end of 2004. Thurston dates the slump from early 1998 to a low point in 2002–04, and calls Vietnam's increase a significant contributor rather than the only cause.

That history is part of why coffee prices swing so hard. Our piece on [why coffee is so expensive now](/blog/why-is-coffee-so-expensive-now-the-9-coffee-crisis/) covers the latest cycle.

## Vietnam's coffee today

USDA's July 2026 forecast for 2026/27:

| | Thousand 60 kg bags | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Robusta | 31,400 | About 97% |
| Arabica | 1,100 | About 3% |
| Total production (a record) | 32,500 | About 17% of the world total of 189,667 |
| Bean exports | 25,400 | |
| Domestic consumption (a record) | 5,000 | |

The shares are calculated from USDA's figures. Brazil, at 71.9 million bags, is the only bigger producer, and Vietnam grows more robusta than any other country. USDA puts the growth down to more area and higher yields, as recent high prices let farmers spend more on fertilizer.

## Why robusta

Robusta is a different species, *Coffea canephora*. Hoffmann explains that it grows and fruits at lower altitudes and higher temperatures than arabica, resists disease better and is much cheaper to produce. The catch, in his view, is the taste: a woody, burnt-rubber quality, very little acidity and a heavy body. There are grades of quality within robusta, he adds, and robusta has long been a staple of Italian espresso, but most of the world's robusta goes into instant coffee.

Robusta also has about twice the caffeine of arabica. Our Caffeine Calculator assumes 1.7–2.5 percent caffeine by weight in roasted robusta beans and 0.9–1.3 percent in arabica. Our guide to [arabica vs. robusta](/blog/arabica-vs-robusta-differences/) goes further.

Vietnam's farms are built for yield. In World Coffee Research's robusta catalog, TR4, a clone selected from a mother tree on a farm in Đắk Lắk by Vietnam's Western Highlands Agroforestry Science Institute (WASI), has a yield potential of 5,000–7,000 kg of green coffee per hectare in its breeders' trials. Thurston adds that production depends on intensive irrigation and heavy use of fertilizer, and that water is becoming scarce and expensive.

## Where coffee grows in Vietnam

Hoffmann notes that because there has been little demand for traceable Vietnamese coffee, its growing regions aren't well-known names. He describes three areas:

| Area | Mostly | Altitude; harvest |
|---|---|---|
| Central Highlands: Đắk Lắk, Lâm Đồng, Gia Lai, Kon Tum | Robusta, some arabica | 600–1,000 m (2,000–3,300 ft); Nov–Mar |
| South: Đồng Nai, northeast of Ho Chi Minh City | Robusta | 200–800 m (650–2,600 ft); Nov–Mar |
| North: Sơn La, Thanh Hóa, Quảng Trị | Arabica | 800–1,600 m (2,600–5,200 ft); Nov–Mar |

- **Central Highlands.** The industry centers on Buôn Ma Thuột, the capital of Đắk Lắk. Hoffmann writes that Đắk Lắk and Lâm Đồng between them grow about 70 percent of Vietnam's robusta.
- **Lâm Đồng.** Arabica has grown around Đà Lạt for about a century. Vietnam's Intellectual Property Office describes the province's arabica at 1,200 m to over 2,200 m (3,900 to over 7,200 ft) and its robusta at 700–850 m (2,300–2,800 ft). In 2022, arabica was about 10 percent of Lâm Đồng's coffee area. The province won a geographical indication for its coffee in October 2025.
- **The north.** There's enough altitude for arabica to grow well, Hoffmann writes, but high-quality coffees from there are still rare. Sucafina lists Sơn La and Điện Biên as the northern arabica provinces, and Catimor as the main arabica variety, with some Typica and Bourbon.

Harvest dates differ by source. Hoffmann gives November to March across the country. Sucafina gives November to January for the Central Highlands and October to January for the north, and Coffee Origins shows robusta running October to February.

## How it's processed, and how it tastes

Sucafina says natural processing is the most common: the cherries are dried in the sun, often on plastic tarps. Thurston notes that ripe and unripe cherries are often picked together, and that money for better patios and dryers is short. Sucafina describes change under way: more selective picking, tighter quality control after harvest and, on small lots, experiments with yeast, enzyme and anaerobic fermentation.

Hoffmann's verdict on most Vietnamese coffee is blunt: very little high-quality coffee is available, so most of it tastes flat and woody, and lacks sweetness. These are notes Vietnamese coffee *can* show, from commodity to specialty:

- **Commodity robusta:** heavy body, low acidity, dark chocolate, peanut, woody and earthy notes, and harsh or rubbery ones when it's carelessly processed. This is what goes into instant coffee and many commercial blends.
- **Carefully processed robusta:** chocolate, brown sugar and a full body without the rubbery edge. Sucafina describes its natural Gánh robusta from Gia Lai as chocolate, honey and caramelized nuts.
- **Lâm Đồng arabica:** citrus, stone fruit and caramel, with moderate acidity. The geographical indication's own description lists apricot, lemon, peach, orange, almond, brown sugar and chocolate, with the acidity of orange, lemon, apricot and peach.

## The specialty side

Sucafina writes that growth in specialty coffee, together with low commodity prices, is pushing Vietnamese farmers toward better robusta and into arabica. Catimor gives high yields and resists disease, but it isn't prized for cup quality, so producers are trying other varieties. The IP Office's announcement of Lâm Đồng's geographical indication notes that the province's companies have won specialty coffee awards in national competitions run by the Buôn Ma Thuột Coffee Association.

Vietnam drinks a lot of its own coffee, too. USDA expects domestic consumption to reach a record 5.0 million bags in 2026/27. Thurston dates the first Vietnamese-owned gourmet coffee shop to 1998, when Đặng Lê Nguyên Vũ opened one in Ho Chi Minh City; by 2004 his franchise network employed more than 6,000 people. The traditional way to brew is the phin, often over sweetened condensed milk; our [phin guide](/blog/vietnamese-phin-guide/) shows how.

## Climate: robusta isn't as robust as assumed

Robusta is often treated as the climate-proof species, but a 2020 study suggests otherwise. Kath and colleagues analyzed 10 years of yields from 798 farms across Southeast Asia and found robusta's optimum mean annual temperature was below 69°F&nbsp;(20.5°C). That's 2.7 to 16°F&nbsp;(1.5 to 9°C) lower than the 72–86°F&nbsp;(22–30°C) range previously assumed from its native Congo basin. During the growing season, each 1.8°F&nbsp;(1°C) rise above an average minimum of 61°F&nbsp;(16.2°C) and maximum of 75°F&nbsp;(24.1°C) went with a yield drop of about 14 percent. For a country that grows mostly robusta, that matters.

## Buying Vietnamese coffee

- **Look for a region,** such as Lâm Đồng or Đà Lạt, or Sơn La, and the species. A bag that says only "Vietnamese coffee" is most likely robusta, since robusta is about 97 percent of the crop.
- **Check the process.** Washed and honey lots are less common than naturals; the bag should say.
- **Expect to hunt.** Hoffmann notes that some large estates, often owned by multinational companies, offer good traceability, but that finding high-quality lots is still hard.
- **Try a robusta on purpose.** If you're curious, look for a roaster that names the farm and process of its robusta, rather than one using it as filler in a blend.
