Cold brew concentrate is coffee steeped in cold water at a high ratio of coffee to water, then thinned before you drink it. That makes two separate choices: the **brew ratio**, which sets how strong the concentrate is, and the **dilution**, which sets how strong your glass is.

## Jessica Easto's French press cold brew

This is the cold brew from Jessica Easto's *Craft Coffee: A Manual*, in her numbers. It's also the recipe our Brew Guide is built on. She steeps it in a French press; a jar with a lid works too, and then you pour the concentrate through a paper or cloth filter instead of the press's mesh.

- **Coffee:** 96 g, ground medium-coarse
- **Water:** 600 g, cold from the fridge or the tap. That's 1:6.25 (calculated: 600 ÷ 96); Easto calls it about 1:6.
- **Time:** about 5 minutes to set up, 12 hours in the fridge, then 10 to 15 minutes to stir, settle and pour; up to about 12 hours 20 minutes in all
- **Makes:** about 408 g of concentrate, about 13¾ US fl oz (408 ml), and about 816 g, or 27½ US fl oz, once it's mixed one to one. Those are calculated: 600 g of water, less about 192 g held back by the grounds at roughly 2 g per gram of coffee, which is the Brew Guide's working figure. Easto's recipe heading says it "Makes 600 grams" of concentrate, which is the water you start with, so expect less.

1. Grind 96 g of coffee medium-coarse (Easto uses setting 25 on a Baratza Virtuoso). Put it in a French press, shake it level and add 600 g of cold water.
2. Set the plunger so the mesh just rests on the grounds and keeps them under the water; do not press. Refrigerate for 12 hours.
3. Take off the lid and stir three times, just until the crust starts to sink. Leave it for 5 to 10 minutes so the fine particles settle.
4. Lower the plunger only until the mesh rests on top of the coffee, without pushing it down, then pour the concentrate gently into a clean container.
5. To serve, mix it one to one with fresh cold water, or to taste. Keep the rest in an airtight container in the fridge for one to two weeks.

Easto's reason for not pressing: pushing the plunger down stirs the settled fines back into the coffee, and she wants them out of the concentrate so they don't keep extracting.

## The ratio makes concentrate; the dilution makes the drink

A cold brew ratio counts the water you steep with, just as a pour-over ratio does. It isn't the strength of what you drink, because you add water or milk afterwards. The Brew Guide offers three strengths of concentrate. Here they are for Easto's 96 g of coffee, each mixed one to one:

| Brew Guide strength | Water to steep → concentrate → served one to one | Strength once mixed |
|---|---|---|
| Stronger, 1:5.5 | 528 g → about 336 g → about 672 g | about 1.4–1.8% |
| Classic, 1:6.25 (Easto) | 600 g → about 408 g → about 816 g | about 1.2–1.6% |
| Lighter, 1:7 | 672 g → about 480 g → about 960 g | about 1.1–1.4% |

The amounts are calculated with the Brew Guide's 2 g of water kept per gram of coffee. The last column is the Brew Guide's strength estimate (it labels it our estimate), as total dissolved solids (TDS), assuming the 15–20% extraction it uses for cold brew; it's an estimate, not a measurement. For comparison, the SCA's brewing chart uses 1.15–1.45% as its reference range for brewed coffee. Our guide to [strength and extraction](/blog/coffee-strength-vs-extraction/) explains why diluting changes strength but not extraction.

## Serving: how much concentrate per glass

Once you have concentrate, every glass can be different. Here are three ways to make about 240 g (8 US fl oz) from Easto's concentrate, before any ice. The strengths are calculated from the Brew Guide's estimate for the concentrate itself, about 2.4–3.2% TDS.

| Mix | Concentrate + water | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| One to one (Easto, Brew Guide) | 120 g + 120 g | about 1.2–1.6% |
| Lighter: 1 to 1½ | 96 g + 144 g | about 1.0–1.3% |
| Stronger: 2 to 1 | 160 g + 80 g | about 1.6–2.1% |

At one to one, a batch makes about three and a half of these glasses (calculated: 408 ÷ 120). Easto says a batch makes upward of five cups once diluted; her cups are smaller.

**Milk** can replace some or all of the water; the Brew Guide suggests water or milk. The strengths above assume water.

**Ice** dilutes further as it melts, so a glass served over ice ends up weaker than the table says. Easto points out that cold brew is already cold, so ice dilutes it less than it dilutes hot coffee poured over ice.

**Caffeine** follows the dilution too. The Caffeine Calculator puts a 16 fl oz glass of the Brew Guide's concentrate, mixed one to one, at 205–445 mg, about 45–95 mg per 100 ml, much the same per 100 ml as drip coffee; the undiluted concentrate carries twice that per 100 ml (calculated). Our [caffeine guide](/blog/caffeine-in-coffee-guide/) has the full table.

## Steep time: 12 hours, and what longer changes

Easto steeps this recipe for 12 hours and writes that cold extraction can take "as long as 12 to 15 hours"; her version for the Clever Dripper steeps for 15. The Brew Guide's window is 12 to 18 hours, with the recipe at 12.

Two studies show what happens with time, though neither tested Easto's recipe:

- **Caffeine levels off early.** Megan Fuller and Niny Rao steeped 35 g of coffee in 350 ml of water at room temperature, 70–77°F&nbsp;(21–25°C). Caffeine and one chlorogenic acid reached a steady level after about 400 minutes, roughly 6 to 7 hours. That's two compounds, not everything that dissolves.
- **The rest keeps coming, and more isn't always better.** Cordoba and colleagues steeped 60 g of coffee in 700 g of water at 68°F&nbsp;(20°C). With a coarse grind, the brews' dissolved solids rose from about 1.5–1.6% at 14 hours to about 2.0% at 22 hours. Their three trained tasters still gave the coarse 14-hour brews the higher overall scores.

So stay near the recipe's time, and change it a few hours at a time. Easto steeps in the fridge; the Brew Guide accepts cold or room-temperature water, and both studies steeped at room temperature. Steeping in the fridge keeps the coffee cold throughout, as the storage advice below asks.

## Grind

Easto grinds medium-coarse: setting 25 on a Baratza Virtuoso. Baratza's own chart for the current Virtuoso+ puts the Chemex at 20 and the French press at 28, so her setting sits between them. The Brew Guide calls for a coarse grind, like kosher salt, about 800–1,100&nbsp;µm, between its Chemex and French press bands.

Fuller and Rao found that grind size made no significant difference to the caffeine and chlorogenic acid in their cold brews, though they note their grind sizes weren't tightly controlled. Fines matter more for clarity: they're what Easto lets settle before pouring. Our [grind size guide](/blog/coffee-grind-size-guide-what-grind-for-every-brew-method/) shows the texture.

## Filtering

In a French press, the mesh does the work, as long as you don't press it through the settled fines. In a jar, pour the concentrate through a paper or cloth filter, as Easto suggests. Our tutorials on the [Toddy cold brew system](/blog/whats-great-about-cold-brew-toddy-cold-brew-tutorial/) and the [CoffeeSock cloth filter](/blog/cold-brewing-coffee-with-the-coffeesock/) cover two dedicated setups.

## How long it keeps

Easto keeps the concentrate in an airtight container in the fridge for one to two weeks, and the Brew Guide uses the same window. Two studies add context:

- **Flavor fades before microbes grow, in sealed bottles.** Samuel Lopane and colleagues at Clemson University brewed ready-to-drink cold brew (1:14, steeped 4 hours at 39°F&nbsp;(4°C) or 77°F&nbsp;(25°C)), bottled it and kept it at 45°F&nbsp;(7°C) for 42 days. Their bacterial counts stayed below detection (under 25 colony-forming units per ml) throughout, but they didn't test for pathogens. A trained panel found the flavor changed over storage, with more sour, fermented and papery, stale notes, and the authors concluded that taste, not microbes, limited shelf life. That was bottled, ready-to-drink coffee, not homemade concentrate.
- **Cold brew doesn't kill germs quickly.** At the University of Georgia, Angela Parra found that *Salmonella*, *E. coli* and *Listeria* didn't grow in cold brew but survived for about 9 to 12 days, and *Bacillus cereus* for more than 30. Contamination usually comes from the ingredients or an unclean setup, such as a dirty pot or unwashed hands, the university's report explains, and cold brew is safest kept in the fridge.

So wash the jar or press and your hands before brewing, keep the concentrate covered and cold, use it within Easto's one to two weeks, and dilute it one glass at a time.

## Is cold brew less acidic?

Partly. In two studies, cold brew had lower titratable acidity, the total amount of acid, than hot coffee from the same beans. Its pH, which measures how acidic the liquid is rather than how much acid it holds, came out about the same in Rao and Fuller's 2018 study and slightly higher (less acidic) in Cordoba's. Our [Japanese iced coffee vs cold brew](/blog/japanese-iced-coffee-vs-cold-brew/) comparison goes through the numbers, and compares cold brew with iced coffee brewed hot, glass for glass.

## Adjusting the next batch

Change one thing at a time. The first change in each row follows our Fix My Coffee tool.

| If the glass is… | Try first | Then, as a separate change |
|---|---|---|
| Too weak, but tastes fine | Add less water or milk when you serve it | Brew the stronger 1:5.5 next time: 96 g of coffee to 528 g of water |
| Too strong, but tastes fine | Add more water or milk | Brew the lighter 1:7: 96 g to 672 g |
| Sour, thin | Grind one step finer | Steep a few hours longer |
| Bitter | Grind one step coarser | Steep a few hours less |
