You can't stop roasted coffee from going stale, but where you keep it decides how fast. Most of the damage comes from things in an ordinary kitchen: the warm spot by the stove, a sunny counter, a bag left half open. This guide covers the everyday choices. For why beans peak and fade, see our [coffee freshness timeline](/blog/coffee-freshness-roast-to-stale/).

## What storage protects against

In *The Physics of Filter Coffee*, Jonathan Gagné lists five enemies of freshness: time, heat, moisture, oxygen and UV light. Storage can't do anything about time, but it can limit the other four:

- **Heat** speeds up every staling process. Gagné cites a calculation by Labuza and colleagues that the shelf life of coffee in the same package drops from about 21 weeks at 72°F&nbsp;(22°C) to about 16 weeks at 95°F&nbsp;(35°C).
- **Moisture** drives damaging reactions and speeds the loss of aroma.
- **Oxygen** oxidizes the coffee's oils, which turn rancid.
- **Light** speeds staling too. James Hoffmann writes in *The World Atlas of Coffee* that light, "especially sunlight", rapidly accelerates staling.

## The everyday rules

1. **Keep it sealed.** If the bag reseals, keep it closed with the air pressed out. If it doesn't, move the beans into an airtight container. Gagné prefers bags to rigid containers for this: you can squeeze the air out of a bag as it empties, while a rigid container's headspace grows.
2. **Keep it dark.** Use an opaque bag or container. If you like a clear jar, keep it in a cupboard; Hoffmann suggests putting a clear container inside a cardboard box.
3. **Keep it cool.** The National Coffee Association warns against cabinets near the oven or dishwasher and spots that get strong afternoon sun, since all can get too warm. Gagné keeps his beans in a low, dark kitchen cabinet far from the oven.
4. **Keep it dry, and out of the fridge.** Hoffmann's advice is not to refrigerate coffee: it doesn't extend its life, and the beans can pick up the smells of strong foods. The National Coffee Association is less absolute: it says views differ, and that if you refrigerate or freeze coffee you should use a truly airtight container, because coffee absorbs moisture and odors and condensation can build up in a fridge. Our Freshness tool sides with Hoffmann: cool, dark, dry, and out of the fridge.
5. **Open one bag at a time.** If several bags arrive together, Gagné suggests drinking them in order rather than opening them all at once, so the unopened ones stay sealed.

### Plunger canisters

The <a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Airscape+coffee+canister&tag=jayarrcoffee-20" rel="sponsored noopener">Airscape</a> has an inner plunger lid that you press down onto the beans, pushing air out through a one-way valve; Planetary Design says the valve also lets freshly roasted beans release CO₂ without letting oxygen back in. The Classic comes in stainless steel or BPA-free plastic and holds 250 g or 500 g of beans, with glass and ceramic versions on the same valve system. Gagné writes that a movable lid like this achieves much the same as a bag with the air pressed out. It only works when the plunger sits on the beans, so press it right down each time you close it.

For anyone buying a 12-ounce or 1-pound bag every week or two, a plunger canister is a simple, sensible upgrade on a bag that won't reseal.

### Vacuum canisters

The <a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Fellow+Atmos+vacuum+canister&tag=jayarrcoffee-20" rel="sponsored noopener">Fellow Atmos</a> has a pump built into the lid: twisting the lid removes air, and an indicator shows when the vacuum is locked. Fellow says to twist it again every 4–5 days to maintain the seal. Sizes are 0.4 L (up to 6 oz of beans), 0.7 L (up to 11 oz) and 1.2 L (up to 16 oz of light or medium roasts). Fellow says stainless steel offers "protection from harmful UV rays"; the glass version is clear, so keep it out of the light. Fellow advertises "Fresh 2x Longer" without saying compared with what.

Gagné's view is that a vacuum canister like this will likely slow oxidation and moisture damage, but may speed up degassing, especially once the headspace becomes large.

## How much to buy, and how long to keep it

Hoffmann's rules are to buy within two weeks of roasting and buy only enough for a couple of weeks at a time; the National Coffee Association also suggests buying enough for one or two weeks. Many roasters suggest using coffee within a month of roasting, and Hoffmann follows that advice.

The arithmetic, if you brew one 15 g cup a day (our calculation):

- Two weeks' supply is 210 g, about 7½ oz.
- A 12-ounce (340 g) bag lasts about 23 days. Opened a week after roasting, it's finished around day 30, the end of the Freshness tool's "still good" stage.
- A 1-pound (454 g) bag lasts about 30 days, so it runs about a week into "likely fading". Freeze part of it if you'd rather not.

The Freshness tool's windows are typical, not a forecast for your bag: most coffees taste best 7 to 21 days after roasting, stay good to about day 30, and fade after that.

## When to freeze instead

If you won't finish a bag within about two weeks, freeze the rest. Gagné writes that freezing, done properly, can keep coffee fresh for up to a year or more, but only if the beans are sealed completely: every time the freezer door opens, moist air gets in and condenses on anything that isn't sealed. Freeze in portions and take out only what you'll use. The Freshness tool also says not to refreeze beans; Gagné notes he knows of no data showing that refreezing itself harms coffee, and that the risk is moisture condensing on beans exposed to air while they're still cold.

Our [freezing guide](/blog/how-to-freeze-coffee-beans/) covers portion sizes, sealing, and whether to grind from frozen or let the beans warm up first.

## Ground coffee and pre-ground

Grind just before you brew. Ground coffee releases much of its trapped gas within minutes of grinding, and its aromas degas the same way; the [freshness timeline](/blog/coffee-freshness-roast-to-stale/) explains the numbers. If you buy pre-ground coffee, the National Coffee Association says an airtight container helps it stay fresher especially, so keep it sealed and buy smaller amounts.

## Three storage myths

**"Oily beans are fresh."** The reverse is more often true. Gagné explains that oils migrate to the surface over days to months, faster in darker roasts, and fresh beans usually don't look oily. A glossy bean is usually a sign of age, a dark roast, or both.

**"The best-by date tells you how fresh it is."** Hoffmann notes that supermarket coffee often carries a best-before date 12 to 24 months after roasting. Look for the roast date instead.

**"Glass jars on the counter are fine."** Only if the counter is dark. Clear containers let light in, and both Hoffmann and the National Coffee Association advise opaque storage or a cupboard.
