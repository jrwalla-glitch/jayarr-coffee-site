In June 2020 we went to Walmart, bought every instant coffee it had, nine in all, and scored each one out of 10, tasted black apart from one sweetened mix. Before tasting, we lined them up in the order we expected to like them, going on brand and packaging alone: the fancier-looking jars and the two Starbucks boxes near the top, Walmart's store brand near the bottom. We were clearly wrong.

| Folgers Classic Roast instant (medium) | 5 | Rich yet smooth, no lingering aftertaste; tasted like a true medium |
| Taster's Choice House Blend (light medium) | 5 | Stronger and heavier than its label suggested, with a slightly bitter aftertaste |

Two 6s, three 5s, and nothing higher. Keep in mind these are instant coffees, and we didn't expect them to taste as good as brewed coffee. There were some drinkable ones here, though: they weren't terrible, but none made it over a 6.

## The coffees, one by one

### The two 6s

**Great Value Classic Roast.** We expected Walmart's store brand to be one of the worst, and it was one of the better coffees here: a really good flavor, not watery and very smooth. It tasted a little lighter than the medium on the label, which we happened to like.

**Juan Valdez.** We'd lined it up near the top because it looked fancy and was one of the higher-priced jars, and it lived up to that: a good flavor, a good smell, and not bitter. It was the first one we tried, and we'd drink it.

### The three 5s

**Folgers Classic Roast.** We put it in the middle of our guesses, and it was better than we expected, with no lingering aftertaste and a rich flavor that was still smooth. It's labeled a medium roast and actually tasted like one, unlike some of the others. We'd finish this cup.

**Taster's Choice House Blend.** Labeled light medium, but it had a very strong, heavy taste and a slightly bitter aftertaste. Not the greatest we tried, but we'd drink it.

**Maxwell House International Café Français.** We picked it up as an instant coffee before noticing it already contains creamer and sugar, so it isn't a like-for-like comparison. Made up as the package directs, it tasted light, with a very good flavor, not too sweet, but almost a little watery; next time we'd use a little more mix or less water. If you like milk and sugar, you may enjoy this one.

### The rest

**Taster's Choice Colombian.** A really good smell, and a little stronger, almost dark, when you first sip it, with a lingering bitterness. It has a rich flavor and was definitely drinkable, but darker than its medium label.

**Starbucks VIA Instant Colombia.** Bold, bitter and pretty strong, with a strange aftertaste almost like artificial flavoring. We weren't fans.

**Starbucks VIA Instant French Roast.** A nice flavor at first, smooth yet bitter, then a lingering aftertaste that was a little acidic. We didn't care for it at all.

**Nescafé Clásico.** Definitely dark and really, really strong. It smells nice, but it's a little acidic with an aftertaste. If you like your coffee very strong you might like it, but we didn't finish the cup.

## How much caffeine is in a cup?

None of these makers' pages lists caffeine. As a general figure, USDA's FoodData Central gives 26 mg of caffeine per 100 g of regular instant coffee made with water, which comes to about 47 mg in USDA's 6 fl oz serving (calculated). Your cup depends on how much powder you use: the makers above suggest anything from a teaspoon per 6 oz to a teaspoon per 4 oz, so check your own jar. Our [caffeine guide](/blog/caffeine-in-coffee-guide/) has the FDA's and EFSA's guidance on daily amounts.

## How instant coffee is made

Instant coffee is brewed coffee with the water taken out. A 2024 review of coffee manufacturing in *Food Engineering Reviews* describes the steps: the roasted, ground coffee is extracted with hot water or steam, the extract is concentrated, and then it's dried one of two ways.

- **Spray-drying,** the most common method, atomizes the concentrate into a chamber of hot air, typically about 250°C (480°F) going in, where the droplets dry into a fine powder. The fine powder is often agglomerated into larger granules so it dissolves more easily.
- **Freeze-drying** freezes the extract solid, then removes the ice under vacuum by sublimation, turning it straight to vapor. It costs more to run, because of the freezing and the vacuum.

The review's authors write that the main disadvantage of spray-drying is believed to be a loss of aroma and flavor, and that studies have found freeze-drying preserves coffee's sensory qualities better. Freeze-dried instant is decades old: Mark Pendergrast's history of coffee dates the first, General Foods' Maxim, to 1964, with Nestlé's Taster's Choice following. The historian Jonathan Morris describes freeze-drying replacing the original spray-dried method in Nestlé's Gold Blend, launched in the late 1960s and sold in the US as Taster's Choice.

Our scores didn't line up neatly with the method, though. Juan Valdez, which calls its Classic freeze-dried, tied for first with the Great Value jar, whose listing doesn't say how it's made. Nescafé describes Taster's Choice House Blend as "brewed and then flash frozen", and its two Taster's Choice jars scored 5 and 4.
