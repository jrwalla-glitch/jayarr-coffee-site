Coffee water recipes differ mainly in two numbers: **hardness** (GH, mostly calcium and magnesium) and **alkalinity** (KH, mostly bicarbonate, which buffers the coffee's acids). This page compares the eight recipes in our Water Recipe Builder, says where each one comes from and what it is really made of, and shows how to mix them. For why water matters at all, start with [why water matters for coffee](/blog/why-water-matters-more-than-your-coffee-beans/).

## The eight recipes at a glance

Hardness and alkalinity are both given in ppm (mg/L) as calcium carbonate, the usual unit. Barista Hustle explains why: simple drop-test kits can't tell calcium from magnesium, so hardness is counted as if it were all calcium carbonate.

## Where each recipe comes from

**SCA standard.** The Specialty Coffee Association's water standard targets a calcium hardness of 68 mg/L (acceptable from 17 to 85), alkalinity at or near 40 mg/L, total dissolved solids of 150 mg/L (75 to 250), a pH of 7.0 and sodium near 10 mg/L. Its 68 is a *calcium* hardness, so an Epsom salt mix, whose hardness is all magnesium, matches the number but not the mineral.

**Melbourne, Barista Hustle #4 and Hendon.** These three come from Barista Hustle's "DIY Water Recipes Redux" (2019), which numbers its recipes; #4 is the original Barista Hustle recipe. Barista Hustle calls Melbourne "a close approximation to Melbourne water": very soft, and useful, it suggests, for long filter brews and cuppings. Its Hendon recipe sits close to the center of the "Ideal Brew Zone" that Christopher Hendon and Maxwell Colonna-Dashwood proposed, and it calls that a good starting point for tuning water to a roast.

**Rao/Perger: the original and the simplified version.** These are two different waters:

- **The original** uses five salts: Epsom salt, magnesium chloride, calcium chloride, baking soda and potassium bicarbonate, as Jonathan Gagné publishes it on *Coffee ad Astra*, adapted to his standard concentrate. From his weights and dilution, it works out at about 87 GH and 40 KH (calculated). Scott Rao's own drop recipe for "Rao/Perger", which he credits to Dan Eils, targets 90 GH and 42 KH.
- **The simplified version** is Barista Hustle's two-salt "Rao Water", at 76 GH and 50 KH, which Barista Hustle describes as close to Scott Rao's recommended water chemistry; Gagné lists it as "Simplified Rao 2008". It has less hardness and more alkalinity than the original, and all its hardness is magnesium, with no calcium or chloride.

The Builder makes the simplified version, which is why it's labeled that way.

**Holy Water.** The Builder targets 62 GH and 23 KH. We couldn't trace this recipe to a published original, so treat these numbers as the Builder's target rather than a credited recipe.

**Third Wave Water.** These are ready-made mineral sticks for distilled water. As published by the maker, the Classic profile contains magnesium sulfate, calcium citrate and sodium chloride, with about 40 mg/L alkalinity and about 150 mg/L dissolved solids; the Espresso Machine profile swaps the sodium chloride for potassium bicarbonate and has about 80 mg/L alkalinity. Gagné measured the Classic's alkalinity at 43 ± 5 ppm and found its hardness much higher than the other recipes he compared. In his tasting its acidity was "a bit overwhelming", and he preferred Rao/Perger. The maker also sells a Dark Roast profile at about 135 to 140 mg/L alkalinity, which the Builder doesn't include.

**RPavlis, a ninth recipe.** This one isn't in the Builder, because it uses neither of the Builder's salts: it's 100 mg/L of potassium bicarbonate in distilled water, named after the Home-Barista member Robert Pavlis. That gives about 50 KH and no hardness at all (calculated).

## What hardness and alkalinity change

**Alkalinity buffers the coffee's acids.** As the Builder puts it, too little and the cup can taste sharp; too much and it can taste flat. Rao found the effect isn't linear: in one test of four recipes with the same coffee, the cups at 10 and 70 KH tasted better to him than those at 30 and 50.

**Hardness minerals are treated as the extracting agents, but the details are less settled than they sound.** Hendon and the Colonna-Dashwoods' 2014 paper used computer modeling to estimate how strongly sodium, magnesium and calcium bind to coffee acids, caffeine and eugenol; it explains why minerals could matter, but it isn't a tasting study. Gagné writes that magnesium and calcium are believed to extract slightly different flavors, but that lab tests didn't yet back it up. A 2024 laboratory study found that at the concentrations usually found in drinking water, these salts changed the coffee's extracted acids only a little; ten times more made a clearer difference.

So use the recipes as starting points and taste. The Builder's map draws guide lines where hardness is two and three times the alkalinity; the eight recipes sit on both sides of them.

## How to mix them: the two-bottle method

Barista Hustle's method uses two stock bottles made with distilled or deionised water and a scale that reads to 0.01 g:

- **Bottle A (hardness):** 2.45 g of Epsom salt (magnesium sulfate heptahydrate) dissolved in 1 liter of water. This gives 1,000 ppm of hardness.
- **Bottle B (alkalinity):** 1.68 g of baking soda (sodium bicarbonate, not baking powder) dissolved in 1 liter of water. This gives as close to 1,000 ppm of alkalinity as the weights allow.

Then, for each liter of brewing water, use as many milliliters of each bottle as the ppm you want, and fill to the liter line with distilled water. The SCA standard is about 68 mL of A and 40 mL of B; Barista Hustle #4 is about 80 mL and 40 mL. Use plain, unscented Epsom salt; the "heptahydrate" matters, because the water in the crystal is part of the weight.

For a US gallon, the Builder offers a shortcut that skips the bottles: for the SCA recipe, weigh 0.63 g of Epsom salt and 0.25 g of baking soda straight into the jug and fill with distilled water. For some recipes, such as Melbourne, one of the amounts is too small to weigh accurately at that size, and the Builder says so.

If you'd rather add potassium than sodium, 2.00 g of potassium bicarbonate gives the same alkalinity as 1.68 g of baking soda (calculated from their molar masses). The Builder's amounts assume baking soda.

## Bottled and tap water

If a bottled water lists its minerals in mg/L, you can compare it with these recipes by converting them: GH as calcium carbonate is about 2.5 × calcium + 4.1 × magnesium, and KH is about 0.82 × bicarbonate (calculated from the molar masses). For tap water, your water utility's annual report or an aquarium GH/KH test kit gives the numbers; Barista Hustle prefers measuring it yourself, because a city's water can vary from one area to another. Enter them in the Builder's "I have test results" to see which recipe your water is closest to and how to adjust it.

## Espresso machines

Water in an espresso machine is a question for the machine's maker first: check its water specification. Barista Hustle's own warning is plain: don't put remineralised water in your espresso machine unless you're very sure what you're doing, because descaling one is much harder than descaling a kettle.

Chloride is another consideration. The original Rao/Perger contains calcium and magnesium chloride, and Third Wave Water Classic contains sodium chloride; Third Wave Water says its Espresso Machine profile avoids chlorides to protect the boiler. Epsom salt and baking soda add none. None of this makes a recipe right for a particular machine; its maker's specification does.
