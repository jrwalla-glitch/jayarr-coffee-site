The flat white is an espresso-and-milk drink from Australia and New Zealand, though which of the two invented it is still argued over. What sets it apart from a latte is less milk for the same coffee, or more coffee for the same milk, and milk with very little foam. This page sets published recipes side by side rather than giving one rule.

Our 2019 video predates this revision; the recipes below supersede its details.

## What makes a flat white

- **The coffee.** Hoffmann writes that a flat white should taste strongly of coffee and is usually made with a double ristretto or a double espresso. Starbucks uses ristretto shots: two in a Tall and three in a Grande. A ristretto is a shorter, more concentrated shot from a similar dose; our [ristretto and lungo guide](/blog/whats-different-about-ristretto-or-lungo-espresso-shots/) has the numbers.
- **The milk.** Scott Rao, in *The Professional Barista’s Handbook*, makes a flat white with minimally aerated milk, with “only a skin of froth on top.” Hoffmann says the milk has a little foam added, but not very much, which makes it relatively easy to pour latte art. Cimbali puts the foam at only 0.5&nbsp;cm, about 3⁄16&nbsp;inch.
- **The size.** Hoffmann gives 150–175&nbsp;ml in all, about 5 to 6&nbsp;US&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz, and Cimbali serves it in a 150–160&nbsp;ml cup (both calculated to ounces). Chains go bigger: Starbucks sells its Flat White from a Short, 8&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz, up to a Venti, 20&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz.

## Flat white vs latte vs cappuccino

Each row is one published recipe, not a universal definition.

| Drink (recipe) | Shot | Milk and foam | Cup |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flat white (La Cimbali) | Double | 125–130 ml frothed; 0.5&nbsp;cm foam | 150–160&nbsp;ml |
| Cappuccino (La Cimbali) | Single | 125&nbsp;ml frothed; 1&nbsp;cm foam | 150–160&nbsp;ml |
| Cappuccino (Hoffmann) | Single | Milk and foam, 2 parts each to 1 of espresso | 150–175&nbsp;ml in all |
| Latte (illy) | Single, 20–25&nbsp;ml | 120&nbsp;ml, cold | Tall glass |
| Latte (Ineffable) | Double (40&nbsp;g) | 180–240&nbsp;g | Not stated |

Two patterns stand out. The flat white and the cappuccino can hold the same amount of milk, as Cimbali’s two recipes do; the difference is the cappuccino’s deeper foam and the flat white’s double shot. And a latte can be built on about the same milk as a flat white, as illy’s is (120&nbsp;ml measured cold, against Cimbali’s 125–130&nbsp;ml frothed), but on a single shot; or it can simply be bigger, as Ineffable’s is.

Rao draws the same line by foam depth. In his 6 to 8&nbsp;oz cups, a cappuccino’s froth is about ½&nbsp;inch deep once the drink has separated, a latte’s about ¼&nbsp;inch, and a flat white has only a skin. Hoffmann adds that a latte traditionally has more liquid milk and less foam than a cappuccino, and that the cappuccino’s “rule of thirds” has no root in tradition.

At Starbucks the difference is mostly the coffee. A Tall Flat White has two ristretto shots and a Tall Caffè Latte one shot, both in a 12&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz cup; the Flat White comes with whole milk and the latte with 2% (menus checked October 18, 2026).

## How to make a flat white at home

You need an espresso machine with a steam wand. This follows La Cimbali’s proportions, with the shot from our Brew Guide and the milk temperature from Breville’s Bambino manual.

- **Espresso:** a double, 18&nbsp;g of coffee to 36&nbsp;g in the cup. For a shorter, ristretto-style shot, the Brew Guide’s Shorter setting (1:1.5) stops at 27&nbsp;g from the same 18&nbsp;g.
- **Milk:** cold whole milk, with the pitcher no more than 60% full, as Hoffmann advises. About 125–130&nbsp;ml of it goes into the drink.
- **Cup:** 150–160&nbsp;ml, about 5 to 5½&nbsp;US&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz.
- **Makes:** one flat white that fills that cup.

1. Pull a double espresso, 18&nbsp;g of coffee to 36&nbsp;g in the cup, into a 150 to 160&nbsp;ml cup.
2. Pour cold whole milk into the pitcher, no more than 60% full. Add only a little air at the start, then spin the milk until the jug is hot to touch, about 140–149°F&nbsp;(60–65°C).
3. Tap the pitcher on the counter to pop any large bubbles, then swirl until the milk looks glossy.
4. Pour the milk into the espresso until the cup is full, about 125 to 130&nbsp;ml of it, leaving a thin layer of foam on top, about 0.5&nbsp;cm.
5. Serve it straight away.

**Less air than a latte.** Rao and Hoffmann both stretch the milk first, adding air while it’s still cool, and then heat it. Rao finishes the stretch before the milk reaches 100°F, and Hoffmann before it feels warm to the touch. For a flat white, keep that stretch short: Rao’s flat white has only a skin of froth.

**On the temperature:** 140–149°F&nbsp;(60–65°C) is the range Breville’s manual gives. Hoffmann says milk begins to degrade in flavor and texture above 154°F&nbsp;(68°C), and that milk heated to 140°F&nbsp;(60°C) gives a sweeter drink than a very hot one. Rao’s café target is 150–160°F&nbsp;(66–71°C), and the top of that is past Hoffmann’s limit. Our [milk steaming guide](/blog/how-to-steam-milk-for-lattes/) explains the stretch and the spin step by step, and our [latte art guide](/blog/latte-art-beginners-guide/) covers the pour.

## Plant milks

Neither Rao nor Hoffmann covers plant milks. In a 2024 lab comparison of six plant milks, Lena Hassan and colleagues found that, frothed hot, oat, pea and soy foamed best of the plant milks, though cow’s and goat’s milk made more foam than any of them. For which cartons are made for steaming, see our guides to [plant milks](/blog/best-plant-milks-for-coffee/) and [oat milk vs dairy](/blog/oat-milk-vs-dairy-in-coffee/).

## Where the flat white comes from

Hoffmann says the argument over whether Australia or New Zealand invented it continues, but that it is undeniably from Australasia. He also suspects humble beginnings: by the 1990s, cappuccinos outside Italy often came with a mountain of dry foam, and customers frustrated at paying for air began asking for a flat, white coffee, just coffee and milk. When cafés later turned to milk texture and latte art, the flat white was remade as the drink it is now.

These are the best-known claims. Each rests largely on the claimant’s own account.

- **Alan Preston, Sydney, 1985.** Preston says he was the first to name it, at Moors Espresso Bar on Sussex Street, which he opened in 1985, and he has a photograph of the menu board said to date from that year (*Sydney Morning Herald* Good Food, 2015). He says he brought the drink from Far North Queensland, where cafés offered a “flat” among their white coffees. Historian Garritt Van Dyk, writing in *The Conversation* in 2024, judges Preston’s case to have the most solid historical evidence.
- **Fraser McInnes, Wellington, 1989.** McInnes says the drink was a failed cappuccino at Cafe Bodega on Willis Street, when the milk wouldn’t froth and he told the customer, “sorry, it’s a flat white” (*The Dominion Post*, January 2015). That report places it in the summer of late 1989; the *Sydney Morning Herald*’s report says a Wellington winter’s day. McInnes himself accepts that other flat whites may have come first.
- **Derek Townsend, Auckland, 1980s.** Townsend says he opened the DKD Cafe in Auckland in 1981, that a friend who had worked as a barista in Melbourne brought back the name for a long black topped up with cold milk, and that he began making it with hot, stretched milk instead (Newshub, 2019).
- **Others.** Craig Miller, author of a book on Wellington’s early coffee houses, told *The Dominion Post* he made a drink called a flat white in Auckland in the mid-1980s from an Australian recipe: a double black coffee with a jug of milk on the side. Ian Bersten, founder of the roaster Belaroma and author of a history of coffee brewing technology, told the *Herald* that nobody knows who invented it, and that it probably took off in England in the 1950s.

Hoffmann credits its spread to people from Australasia who opened cafés in Europe and North America; in the UK, he writes, the name became linked with quality-focused cafés before the big chains adopted it. Starbucks put the Flat White on its United States and Canadian menus in January 2015, according to *The Dominion Post*’s report that month.

## Which one to order

Going by the recipes above:

- **A flat white** if you want milk coffee that tastes clearly of the coffee. Cimbali’s has a double shot where illy’s latte has a single, and a Starbucks Tall Flat White has two ristretto shots where its Tall latte has one.
- **A cappuccino** if you want a deeper cap of foam; Cimbali’s has about twice the flat white’s.
- **A latte** if you want a milder or bigger drink.
- **A [cortado](/blog/what-is-a-cortado/)** if you want something smaller still, with only a little milk.

## Without an espresso machine

A flat white starts with espresso, so without a machine you’re making a similar milk coffee rather than the drink itself. Hoffmann describes moka pot coffee as very strong, but it isn’t espresso; our [moka pot guide](/blog/moka-pot-brewing-guide/) explains the difference and the method. For the milk, our [milk frother guide](/blog/best-milk-frothers-home-baristas/) compares tools that make pourable foam without a steam wand. Keep the foam thin, as for a flat white made on a machine.
