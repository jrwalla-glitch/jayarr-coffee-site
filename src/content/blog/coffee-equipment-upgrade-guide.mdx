Every piece of coffee gear fixes something different, and the upgrade that helps is the one that fixes your problem. This page is organized by those problems: what each upgrade changes in the cup, what the sources we rely on say about it, and how to tell whether you need it. Sometimes the answer is to buy nothing.

## Before you buy anything

Three free checks come first:

- **The beans.** Whole beans usually taste best about 7 to 21 days after roasting and stay good to about day 30, though those are typical windows rather than a forecast for your bag. Ground coffee fades much faster: Jessica Easto writes in *Craft Coffee* that a trained palate can notice the loss about 30 minutes after grinding, and most people after an hour or more. Our [freshness timeline](/blog/coffee-freshness-roast-to-stale/) explains the windows.
- **The recipe.** If the cup is weak but otherwise balanced, try more coffee for the same water before buying anything; if it's also sour or runs through unusually fast, check extraction and grind size first. Weigh both: our Brew Guide starts a V60 at 1:16, so a 12 fl oz mug takes 25.4 g of coffee and 406 g of water.
- **One change at a time.** Sour usually means under-extracted and bitter over-extracted, but the fix depends on how the brew went. Change one thing, taste, then change the next.

## Sour and bitter at once, or it won't dial in: the grinder

**What it fixes:** uneven extraction. James Hoffmann explains that a blade grinder smashes coffee into some very fine powder and some very large pieces; in the brew, the fine powder quickly adds bitterness while the large pieces add sourness. A burr grinder cuts the coffee between two burrs set a chosen distance apart, so the grounds come out more even and you can adjust their size. No grinder makes a single size, but in Jonathan Gagné's measurements lower-quality grinders make wider spreads of particle sizes than better ones.

**Do you need it?** Probably, if you use a blade grinder or pre-ground coffee. Easto's advice is that if you'll buy only one piece of coffee equipment, it should be a decent grinder: grinding fresh protects the flavor, and pre-ground coffee is unlikely to be the right size for your brewer, so you can't adjust the grind when a cup goes wrong.

**Maybe not,** if you already own a burr grinder. Before replacing it, check that you're starting from the setting its maker suggests, and that the burrs aren't worn; our guide to [whether your grinder is the bottleneck](/blog/why-grinder-matters-more-than-machine/#is-your-grinder-the-bottleneck) lists the signs.

## Good cups you can't repeat: a scale

**What it fixes:** inconsistency. A scoop measures volume, and Easto explains that beans vary in size and density, so a tablespoon of one coffee can weigh a gram or more different from another, while the gap between a great cup and a lesser one can be half a gram or less. Weighing makes the ratio a constant, so when something changes you know it wasn't the dose. Hoffmann calls digital scales a worthwhile investment for anyone who wants consistently good coffee, and not an expensive one.

**Do you need it?** If you measure with scoops, yes. If you already own a 1 g kitchen scale, it's a useful step up from scoops. For a new purchase, Easto recommends 0.1 g readability, and Hoffmann's espresso instructions call for weighing the dose to within 0.1 g of the recipe. Our [coffee scale guide](/blog/5-coffee-scales-reviewed/) covers what to look for.

## Every coffee tastes flat, chalky or off: the water

**What it fixes:** the minerals in the cup. Brewed coffee is almost all water, and its hardness and alkalinity change what the coffee tastes like even when the water tastes fine on its own. Our guide to [why water matters](/blog/why-water-matters-more-than-your-coffee-beans/) explains the numbers and the Specialty Coffee Association's targets, and our [water recipes comparison](/blog/coffee-water-recipes-compared/) covers mixing your own.

**Do you need it?** Test before you buy. For a flat cup from fresh beans and tap water, Fix My Coffee suggests brewing the same recipe with bottled spring water: if that tastes better, the water is worth fixing. For a chlorine or medicinal note from tap water, it suggests a carbon filter or spring water. If you brew with distilled or reverse-osmosis water, the problem may be the opposite: too few minerals.

## Pour-over that's hard to control: a gooseneck kettle

**What it fixes:** control over the pour. Gagné writes that a kettle doesn't affect brewing anywhere near as much as a grinder, but that a thin gooseneck lets you pour slowly and steadily where you aim, which helps you repeat a brew and control its timing and agitation.

**Do you need it?** For a V60 or a Chemex, Easto suggests considering one. For a French press, an AeroPress or a Clever, she doesn't think you gain much from a special kettle; an ordinary kettle works well for immersion brewing. A temperature-controlled kettle adds a set temperature and a hold; Gagné's advice is to check its display with a thermometer once. Our [gooseneck kettle guide](/blog/best-gooseneck-kettles-pour-over/) compares four.

## Espresso: the grinder first, then the machine

For espresso, Hoffmann's advice is to upgrade the grinder before the machine. In his words, a good grinder and a basic thermoblock machine "will make better coffee than a cheap grinder and a top-of-the-line dual-boiler espresso machine." An espresso grinder has to grind fine enough and adjust in very small steps, and no machine can make up for one that can't.

The machine becomes the limit later. Hoffmann notes that thermoblock machines, the cheapest kind, don't hold a very consistent brewing temperature and can't brew and steam at once, and that many owners soon feel constrained; dual-boiler machines give stable, finely tuned temperatures. Don't shop by a pump's bar figure: he puts the pressure espresso wants at around 9 bar, and calls makers' boasts of 15 bar a case of treating more as better. Our [grinder vs machine guide](/blog/why-grinder-matters-more-than-machine/#is-your-grinder-the-bottleneck) sets out how to tell which one is holding you back.

## The brewer: change it for a different cup, not to fix one

Choose a brewer for the kind of coffee and the routine you want. Easto's first question when choosing one is whether you want to brew by immersion, where the coffee steeps, or by pour-over, where the water runs through, and she weighs devices by ease of use, availability and price. If your current brewer makes sour-and-bitter cups, the grind or the pour is the likelier cause; Fix My Coffee asks about both.

When you do want a change, our guides compare [pour-over drippers](/blog/best-pour-over-coffee-makers/), [flat-bottom and cone drippers](/blog/flat-bottom-vs-cone-pour-over/) and the [AeroPress and French press](/blog/aeropress-vs-french-press/).

## Measuring instead of guessing: a refractometer

Taste tells you something is off; a refractometer gives you a number. It measures how strong the coffee is as total dissolved solids (TDS), and with your dose and the drink's weight that gives the extraction yield. It doesn't fix a cup by itself, but it shows whether a change moved extraction or only strength. Our [refractometer guide](/blog/best-coffee-refractometer-home-barista/) covers taking a good reading.

## Upgrades that rarely fix the problem

- **An espresso machine before a good grinder.** Hoffmann's advice runs the other way.
- **A gooseneck for a French press or AeroPress.** Easto sees little gain for immersion brewers.
- **A higher pump pressure.** Around 9 bar is what espresso wants, by Hoffmann's account.
- **A new brewer for a sour-and-bitter cup.** Look at the grind and the pour first.

## If you're starting from scratch

Easto builds her equipment advice in a set order, by how many pieces you're willing to buy: a burr grinder first, then a scale, then, for pour-over, a gooseneck kettle. Her recipes in *Craft Coffee* are marked by which of the three each one needs. For espresso, Hoffmann's order is the grinder, then the machine.
