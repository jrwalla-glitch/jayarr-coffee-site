Arabica is a narrow crop genetically. WCR's history of the species notes that in Brazil, the largest producer, 97.55 percent of coffee cultivars derive from just two old lines, Typica and Bourbon, and its catalog calls the traditional American varieties "severely genetically constricted." F1 hybrids are one way breeders are trying to widen that base. This article covers what they are, what WCR's catalog says about the ones farmers can plant, and what holds them back. For how they fit into the wider family of varieties, see our [coffee varietal guide](/blog/coffee-varietal-guide/).

## What an F1 hybrid is

WCR defines F1 hybrids as varieties "created by crossing genetically distinct Arabica parents and using the first-generation offspring." Many were made, it writes, "to combine the best characteristics of the two parents, including high cup quality, high yield, and disease resistance," and F1s "tend to have significantly higher production than non-hybrids."

The reason is hybrid vigor, or heterosis. In plant breeding generally, WCR explains, the greater the genetic distance between two parents, the more vigorous the offspring, and in coffee that vigor tends to show up as productivity, uniformity and better growth. Typically, WCR writes, the parents are chosen to be genetically distant from one another, which is why so many F1s pair a compact variety bred in the Americas with an Ethiopian landrace from a gene bank.

F1 hybrids aren't genetically modified. WCR says its crosses are made by classic plant breeding, moving pollen from one flower to another with a paintbrush; genetics only helps choose the parents.

## Where their rust resistance comes from

Seven of the nine F1s in WCR's catalog, including all six it lists as commercially available, get their resistance to coffee leaf rust from the same place older resistant varieties do: the Timor Hybrid, a natural arabica and robusta cross from the 1920s. WCR classes them as "F1 hybrid (introgressed)," meaning they carry genes brought over from another species, and their resistant parent is a Timor Hybrid descendant:

- **Centroamericano, Milenio, Mundo Maya and Esperanza** all have T5296 as a parent, a Sarchimor that WCR describes as a rust-resistant descendant of the Timor Hybrid.
- **Starmaya's** fertile parent is Marsellesa, another Sarchimor (Timor Hybrid 832/2 × Villa Sarchi).
- **Evaluna's** is Naryelis, a Catimor-type variety, and **Ruiru 11's** mother plants are Catimors.

The two F1s without that parentage show what it's worth. H3 and Casiopea are crosses of Caturra and Ethiopian landraces, classed "F1 hybrid (not introgressed)," and WCR rates both susceptible to leaf rust.

So F1s don't escape the problem our [leaf rust history](/blog/coffee-leaf-rust-hemileia-vastatrix-history/) describes. WCR writes that because most resistant varieties got their resistance from the same parent, most experts believe most existing rust-resistant varieties will lose it in the near to medium term. The Ethiopian parent widens the genetic base; on WCR's ratings, it doesn't make an F1 rust-resistant on its own.

## The F1 hybrids farmers can plant

WCR's program page lists Centroamericano, Mundo Maya, Starmaya, Milenio and Evaluna as commercially available in a few Latin American countries, and Ruiru 11 in Kenya. Here are its catalog ratings for those and three more F1s, checked October 18, 2026. Cup quality is WCR's rating of potential at high altitude, not a score for any one lot.

### Centroamericano

A consortium of the French research institute CIRAD, PROMECAFE (the network of Central America's national coffee institutes) and the CATIE gene bank in Costa Rica created Centroamericano, and it was released in 2010. In breeding evaluations, WCR writes, it produced 22 to 47 percent more than the standard varieties in Central America. Its rating table puts cup quality at Very Good, while the history on the same entry says it "has also proven to have exceptional cup quality potential when well-managed at high altitudes."

That potential has shown up in competition. In May 2017 WCR reported that a Centroamericano lot from Nicaragua had scored 90 points in the country's Cup of Excellence, and that WCR had confirmed the variety with DNA tests of the green coffee. The same report put Centroamericano on roughly 1,000 hectares in the region at the time.

WCR's agronomic notes are a reminder that it isn't an easy plant: it can struggle to establish roots in its first two years and has a very high nutrition requirement.

### Starmaya

Starmaya is the F1 that can come from seed. Its mother plant produces no pollen, so when it's planted beside its fertile parent, Marsellesa, the seeds it sets must be hybrids of the two, as WCR explains.

It was bred by CIRAD and the agricultural trader ECOM, and the breeders tell the story in a 2019 paper. CIRAD researchers found the male-sterile plant, which they named CIR-SM01, in 2001 while screening Ethiopian plants in CATIE's gene bank in Costa Rica. A laboratory in Sébaco, Nicaragua, multiplied it by cloning, and the first experimental seed garden was planted in 2009 on the La Cumplida farm in Matagalpa, Nicaragua, with four male-sterile mothers to every Marsellesa. WCR's catalog tells it slightly differently, placing the find among wild Ethiopian and Sudanese coffees on La Cumplida itself. CIRAD announced in December 2019 that Starmaya was available as seed for the first time.

WCR calls Starmaya "the only F1 hybrid in the world that professional seed producers can propagate by seed." It isn't perfectly uniform: WCR notes that about 15 percent of planted Starmaya segregate, looking or performing differently from the rest. And as with every F1, seed saved from Starmaya trees on a farm won't grow true.

### Mundo Maya, Esperanza and Evaluna

- **Mundo Maya** was selected by ECOM and CIRAD in Nicaragua from a cross of T5296 with an Ethiopian landrace from CATIE's collection, originally collected in Ethiopia by a French ORSTOM mission.
- **Esperanza** was tested in the 1990s and early 2000s alongside Centroamericano and Milenio but wasn't chosen for release. Some plants stayed at the Aquiares farm in Turrialba, Costa Rica, where they did well in heavy rain and humidity; local farmers asked for it, and WCR writes it's now one of the most-demanded F1s in Costa Rica.
- **Evaluna** was selected and released in 2010, WCR writes.

### H3, Casiopea and Ruiru 11

- **H3** was dropped from the final round of selection after 2000 because it showed some susceptibility to rust, but its cup quality was exceptional, WCR writes, so some farmers in the early trials kept growing it.
- **Casiopea** is rated for exceptional quality above 1,300 m.
- **Ruiru 11** is older and different in kind. Kenya bred it after a 1968 coffee berry disease epidemic cost half the country's production, and released it in 1985. Its seed is made by hand-pollinating Catimor mothers with pollen from a complex male parent, and that hand work has made it hard to supply enough seed, WCR notes.

## Clones, seed and cost

Seed taken from an F1 plant gives a second generation that "segregates," WCR explains: the plants come out taller and shorter, stronger and weaker, and few behave like the F1 they came from, with possible losses of yield, disease resistance and quality. So WCR's advice for F1s is clonal propagation and seedlings bought from trusted nurseries. There are three ways to make F1 plants in quantity:

- **Cloning in the lab,** by tissue culture or somatic embryogenesis, for Centroamericano and most others.
- **Hand pollination** of the mother plants, as for Ruiru 11.
- **Male sterility,** as for Starmaya.

WCR calls the first two "costly" and limited in capacity. Its program page says F1s are more expensive and harder to mass-produce, and need more fertilizer. Its 2019 explainer gave an example of an F1 seedling costing three times as much as a traditional one. The case it makes for the extra cost: a first harvest two years after planting instead of three or four, and yields up to 50 percent higher, with the choice left to the farmer.

The risks are real too. Because the result of planting F2 seed may not show for three years, WCR warns that farmers who save seed, as most do, could lose six years of production. It says F1 releases should come with farmer education.

## What's next

WCR is breeding its own F1s. Its program page, checked October 18, 2026, gives the timeline:

- **2015:** 43 crosses made.
- **2017 onward:** the plants went to field trials in El Salvador, Costa Rica and Rwanda and were watched for six years.
- **2022:** four finalists chosen. On average they yielded 28 percent more than the commercial varieties they were compared with, and three of them averaged a cup score above 84.
- **2024 to 2031:** pre-commercial testing with industry partners.

WCR stresses that F1s "are not a silver bullet": they need different management, more nutrition and farmer education to pay off.

## F1 hybrids and climate change

WCR frames its F1 program as a response to climate change, and its program page notes recent results suggesting F1s may also tolerate frost better. Our Deep Brew research note on [coffee and climate change](/deep-brew/research/coffee-climate-change-data/) gathers the heat, harvest and suitable-land data, and our [arabica vs robusta guide](/blog/arabica-vs-robusta-differences/) explains why robusta isn't the heat-proof backup it was once thought to be.

## What it means for coffee drinkers

You may see these names on bags, such as Centroamericano, Starmaya or Mundo Maya. A few things help in reading them:

- **The rating is a potential.** WCR's "Very Good" for Centroamericano assumes high altitude and good management; any variety's cup still depends on the farm, the processing and the roast. Our guide to [reading a coffee bag](/blog/how-to-read-coffee-bag-label/) covers the rest of the label.
- **Rust resistance isn't permanent.** Most F1s share the Timor Hybrid's resistance genes, so they face the same erosion as older resistant varieties.
- **The parents are visible.** An F1's Ethiopian half comes from landraces like those behind Ethiopia's own coffees; our guide to [Ethiopian heirloom varieties](/blog/ethiopian-heirloom-vs-named-varietals/) explains them.
