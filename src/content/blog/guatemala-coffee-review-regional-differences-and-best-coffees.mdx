Guatemala is one of the easiest origins to explore by region. Hoffmann notes that it has been more successful than most countries at defining its regions and marketing them as different, and that its coffee is traceable because many farmers have their own wet mills. In 2020 we tasted a selection of widely available Guatemalan coffees and scored each out of five; the scores and tasting notes below come from that video, where each coffee got an on-screen card with its region, roast and notes.

## The coffees, one by one

What each maker sells now was checked on its own website on October 18, 2026. Coffee is a crop, so a coffee sold under the same name today may come from a different harvest, or a different farm, than the bag we tasted.

### Cat & Cloud Guatemala Los Cedros: 4.7

The Guatemalan that got our best rating. A washed coffee that kept the chocolate and nut flavors typical of Guatemala and married them to brighter notes like tangerine; we thought it would pair well with a morning pastry or an evening dessert. The video introduced Cat & Cloud, a Santa Cruz, California roaster, through its founders' competition record; Cat & Cloud's own page says co-founder Chris Baca is a former regional barista champion.

**Now:** Cat & Cloud no longer lists Los Cedros. Its current Guatemala is Rosalío Martínez, from a farmer with about 7 hectares across nine plots in Antigua, bought through the Bella Vista mill, with notes of peach, kiwi, toffee and nuts. A 10 oz bag was listed at $28.58 when checked October 18, 2026. It's a different coffee from a different region.

### Starbucks Guatemala Antigua: 4.2

A crowd pleaser. The chocolate and nuts weren't especially interesting, but it was a low-risk introduction to Guatemalan coffee, with a relatively buttery texture and a clean, almost crisp finish. It started with high acidity that the bolder, roastier notes muted mid-palate.

**Now:** Starbucks at Home's US site map doesn't list it, but Amazon still sells a 16 oz whole-bean bag. Starbucks' product page on its China site describes a washed, medium-roast coffee from the Antigua Valley with notes of cocoa and soft spice.

### Belux Guatemala Red Pacamara Natural: 4.1

An auction-lot coffee from a small Georgia roaster: bright acidity, chocolate and fruit, and a subtle but interesting wine-like quality. We recommended it to anyone new to Pacamara.

**Now:** Belux Coffee Roasters, in Alpharetta, Georgia, still sells a Guatemala Red Pacamara Natural from Santa Felisa in Acatenango, grown at 1,650 m by its detailed profile (the listing title says 1600) and roasted medium. It describes a 2025 lot with notes of plum, caramel-like sweetness, mandarin orange and sugarcane, listed at $38 (no bag size shown) when checked October 18, 2026.

### Volcanica Guatemala Antigua: 4.0

Rich, sophisticated and smooth-bodied, with a pleasant, robust aroma and a balanced mix of chocolate and nuts, not overly sweet. We called it an easy baseline for what to expect from Guatemalan coffee.

**Now:** Volcanica sells "Guatemala Antigua Coffee - USDA Organic," a washed, sun-dried medium roast, now listing berry, green apple, jasmine and sweet lemon. A 16 oz bag was listed at $22.99 when checked October 18, 2026. The page names two different farms as its source, La Hermosa Estate and Finca Nueva Granada.

### Pablo's Pride: 3.8

A clean finish and a smooth texture, with the caramel and cocoa notes typical of Guatemala. The video called it one of the only blends in the lineup, and we liked it enough to recommend trying it.

**Now:** Burke Brands sells it on its Don Pablo website as Pablo's Pride Guatemala Coffee, a medium-dark roast of 100% arabica grown in Guatemala, with notes of caramel and cocoa. A 2 lb bag was listed at $26.99 when checked October 18, 2026.

### Two Volcanoes: 3.8

A San Marcos single origin. Like many Guatemalans, nuts led, but with reduced acidity and floral hints that set it apart: a low-toned, pleasant coffee that's easy to drink.

**Now:** Two Volcanoes Coffee's website gives a Guatemala City address and says the company grows, roasts, packs and ships its own coffee. Its own store lists no products and points buyers to Amazon, which sells a 1 lb Guatemala whole-bean medium roast.

### Out of the Grey ASOBAGRI Huehuetenango: 3.5

From ASOBAGRI, a cooperative in Huehuetenango. Its strength was a range of rich, unusual flavors (blood orange, coconut and jasmine), and opinions will vary on whether they come together. A love-it-or-hate-it coffee for adventurous palates.

**Now:** Out of the Grey still sells it as "Guatemalan ASOBAGRI Hue Hue Tenango SHB - Fair Trade Coffee," a medium roast grown at 1,200–1,600 m, listing jasmine, date, pistachio, blood orange zest and coconut. A 12 oz bag was listed at $22.99 when checked October 18, 2026. The roaster writes that ASOBAGRI was founded in 1989 by 20 K'anjob'al Maya coffee and cardamom farmers and became Fair Trade certified in 1999.

### Java Planet Guatemala Organic: 2.8

We weren't thrilled with it. It didn't taste bad, but it was a bit flat and stale-tasting, and its chocolate, caramel and fruit were typical of Guatemala without being interesting. One to pass on.

**Now:** Java Planet, an organic roaster in Tampa, Florida, still sells a Guatemala Organic medium roast, listed at $26.99 for 1 lb when checked October 18, 2026.

## Guatemala's coffee regions

Our video said Guatemala has eight distinct coffee regions and described seven of them. Hoffmann's *World Atlas of Coffee* lists the eight, with these altitudes and harvest months:

| Region | Altitude (Hoffmann) | Harvest (Hoffmann) |
|---|---|---|
| Antigua | 1,500–1,700 m (4,900–5,600 ft) | January–March |
| Acatenango | 1,300–2,000 m (4,300–6,600 ft) | December–March |
| Atitlán | 1,500–1,700 m (4,900–5,600 ft) | December–March |
| Cobán | 1,300–1,500 m (4,300–4,900 ft) | December–March |
| Fraijanes | 1,400–1,800 m (4,600–5,900 ft) | December–February |
| Huehuetenango | 1,500–2,000 m (4,900–6,600 ft) | January–April |
| Nuevo Oriente | 1,300–1,700 m (4,300–5,600 ft) | December–March |
| San Marcos | 1,300–1,800 m (4,300–5,900 ft) | December–March |

These are the windows in our [coffee harvest calendar](/blog/coffee-harvest-calendar/), which adds Café Imports' typical US arrival for Guatemalan coffee: June. Hoffmann adds that while some flavors are more common in certain regions, "there are no hard and fast rules." Here's how our video, Hoffmann and Café Imports describe each:

- **Antigua.** Café Imports notes that many of its farms sit on the volcanoes Agua, Fuego and Acatenango. Our video found a mild sweetness, chocolate and an apple-like acidity in genuine Antigua; Café Imports calls the coffees sweet and smooth, mild and lower in acidity. Antigua's popularity led to fraud, and Hoffmann writes that the region gained a denomination of origin in 2000 as "Genuine Antigua Coffee," which stopped coffee from other origins being sold as Antigua, though not the practice of bringing in cherries from other regions to process there. Look for a named farm.
- **Huehuetenango.** In the northwest, on the Mexican border. Our video called it Guatemala's highest, driest and most remote region, and non-volcanic; Hoffmann says it has the highest non-volcanic mountains in Central America and is probably the region most dependent on coffee. Our video found a mild, wine-like acidity; Café Imports describes "crisp malic and citrus acidity, full body, and toffee sweetness."
- **Acatenango.** Named after its volcano. Hoffmann writes that its growers once sold cherries to "coyotes" who processed them in Antigua for a better price, a practice now less common as Acatenango's own coffees become better known. Our Belux Pacamara came from here.
- **Atitlán.** Around Lake Atitlán, which sits at about 1,500 m. Our video described full-bodied coffee with a floral, somewhat spicy taste; Café Imports describes nutty, chocolate notes balanced by a lemony acidity and some florals. Hoffmann notes the strong afternoon winds, the *xocomil*, and pressure on coffee land from urban sprawl and rising labor costs.
- **Cobán.** A rainforest region in central Guatemala. Hoffmann notes its very wet climate makes drying difficult and that it's remote, "but there are, nonetheless, stunning coffees." Our video described Cobán coffee as medium-bodied and bright but less acidic than other Guatemalan coffees.
- **Fraijanes.** A plateau around Guatemala City. Hoffmann notes fairly regular volcanic activity, which benefits the soil, and that land under coffee is shrinking as the city grows. Our video called it rare in the United States, with sweet aromas and complex flavors.
- **Nuevo Oriente.** In the east, on the border with Honduras. Our video called it one of the oldest regions; Hoffmann says the opposite, that coffee arrived there quite late, in the 1950s, mostly on smallholdings. Our video described a strong aroma, high acidity and a full body, and Café Imports "a full-bodied coffee with loads of balance."
- **San Marcos.** Hoffmann calls it the warmest and rainiest region; rains reach its Pacific-facing slopes early, so it flowers earlier, and some farms combine sun and mechanical drying. Our video found a lime-like acidity, with floral aromas and cocoa.

## How much Guatemala grows

USDA forecasts 3.26 million 60 kg bags for 2026/27 (October to September), 3.13 million of them arabica, about 1.7 percent of world production (calculated). That's up about 3 percent on the year, mostly from more harvested area. Volumes are lower than they were: USDA's series had 4.41 million bags in 2011/12, and Hoffmann writes that production peaked around the turn of the millennium, before many producers moved to macadamia nuts and avocados after the 2001 coffee crisis.

Guatemala's coffee comes mostly from smallholders. Anacafé's president told the news agency EFE in February 2025 that about 125,000 producers grow coffee in Guatemala, 97 percent of them small farmers. USDA reports that coffee is grown in 261 of the country's 340 municipalities, that 98 percent of it grows under shade, and that the United States takes 42 percent of exports.

A short history: Hoffmann writes that coffee was probably introduced around 1750 but became important only after 1856, when chemical dyes cut demand for indigo; under Justo Rufino Barrios, from 1871, coffee became the backbone of the economy, at the cost of indigenous people's land, and by 1880 it made up about 90 percent of exports.

## Altitude grades

Guatemala grades coffee by altitude. Hoffmann gives Strictly Hard Bean (SHB) as grown above 1,300 m (4,300 ft), Hard Bean as 1,220–1,300 m, then Semi Hard Bean, Extra Prime and Prime below. In Anacafé's breakdown of the 2024/25 harvest, reproduced by USDA, 81 percent was SHB arabica. So SHB on a bag tells you the coffee was grown high, not which region or farm it came from. Our [altitude guide](/blog/coffee-altitude-guide/) compares Guatemala's grades with its neighbors'.

## Varieties and leaf rust

Hoffmann's regional entries list Bourbon, Caturra and Catuai almost everywhere, with Typica, Pache and Maragogype in some regions. Pache is a Guatemalan variety: World Coffee Research (WCR) dates its discovery to 1949, as a dwarf mutation of Typica on a farm in Santa Rosa.

The mix has shifted. USDA's April 2026 report says Catimor and Caturra now make up nearly half of plantings, followed by Catuai, Sarchimors and Bourbon, each on fewer than 10 percent of farms, and that about a third of arabica plantations have been renovated with rust-tolerant hybrids. (WCR's Catuai entry puts that variety at about 20 percent of Guatemala's production; the two figures count different things.) Leaf rust remains a pressure: incidence reached up to 20 percent in January to March 2025, in unusually wet, warm months.

WCR rates Bourbon "Very Good" for quality potential at high altitude, and Caturra, Catuai and Pache "Good," and it rates all four susceptible to leaf rust. Pacamara, the large-beaned variety in our Belux coffee, was bred in El Salvador, and WCR rates its quality potential "Exceptional." Our [coffee varietal guide](/blog/coffee-varietal-guide/) explains how these varieties are related.

## Processing

Most Guatemalan coffee is washed: Café Imports lists it as washed or mechanically demucilaged, and Hoffmann notes that much of it is still dried in the sun. Naturals are the exception, like our Belux Pacamara. Hoffmann writes that washed coffees tend to taste cleaner, while naturals can be fruitier. Our [processing guide](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/) explains washed, honey and natural coffees.
