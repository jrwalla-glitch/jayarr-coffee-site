A mocha is a latte with chocolate in it. That makes it easy to get roughly right and easy to tip into hot chocolate, so this page starts from published recipes and their measured amounts.

Our 2019 video predates this revision; the recipes below supersede it.

## Where the name comes from

Mocha, or Mokha, is the port in Yemen that coffee was once exported from. James Hoffmann, in *The World Atlas of Coffee*, calls “mocha” probably the most confusing word in coffee: it has meant the port, the potent coffees of Yemen, the Mocha-Java blend, and now a mix of espresso and hot chocolate, a use he says only bewilders people further. Our [Yemen coffee guide](/blog/yemen-coffee-guide-mocha-origin/) tells the port’s story. This page is about the drink.

## Three published recipes

Each is one maker’s or chain’s recipe, not a universal standard.

- **Breville’s Mocha:** one shot of espresso, 2&nbsp;teaspoons of drinking chocolate and milk steamed with the wand, with more drinking chocolate dusted on top.
- **Starbucks’ Caffè Mocha, Grande (16&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz):** two shots, four pumps of mocha sauce and steamed 2% milk, topped with whipped cream.
- **Starbucks at Home’s Duo Cocoa Mocha, per mug:** 1&nbsp;cup of brewed coffee, 1&nbsp;tablespoon each of cocoa powder and sugar, and ½&nbsp;cup of 2% milk heated and frothed in a pan, topped with whipped cream, melted dark chocolate and cocoa nibs.

Starbucks scales its café mocha by size: a Short (8&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz) has one shot and two pumps of sauce, a Tall (12&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz) one shot and three pumps, a Grande two shots and four pumps, and a Venti (20&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz) two shots and five pumps (US menu, checked October 18, 2026). Starbucks at Home’s recipe is written for two mugs; the amounts above are halved (calculated).

## Choosing the chocolate

The three recipes use three kinds of chocolate, and they differ in sweetness and in how they mix:

- **Cocoa powder:** Starbucks at Home adds a tablespoon of sugar for each tablespoon of cocoa, so you control the sweetness. The powder has to be stirred into the hot coffee until no dry powder is left.
- **Drinking chocolate** is a powder sold for making hot chocolate, and it’s what Breville stirs into the shot. Check its label for how much sugar it already has.
- **Chocolate sauce** is already liquid and already sweet. Starbucks lists its mocha sauce as water, sugar, cocoa processed with alkali and natural flavor.

Whatever you use, mix it with the hot coffee before the milk goes in, as both home recipes do. Breville adds a tip: dusting the chocolate powder into the espresso before pouring the milk helps make the latte art more defined.

## With an espresso machine

This is Breville’s mocha. Breville doesn’t give the shot’s size or the cup’s, so it scales with whatever you pull.

1. Pull one shot of espresso into your cup.
2. Pour cold milk into the jug, up to the bottom of the spout. Put the steam wand about ½&nbsp;inch (1.5&nbsp;cm) into the milk, open the steam, and lower the jug to bring air into the surface.
3. Steam the milk to around 140–150°F&nbsp;(60–65°C), Breville’s figures, about when the jug becomes too hot to touch. For non-dairy milk, Breville says not to go over 130°F&nbsp;(55°C), as it can start to separate or curdle. Swirl the jug until the milk looks velvety.
4. Stir 2 teaspoons of drinking chocolate into the espresso.
5. Pour in the milk and dust the top with a little more drinking chocolate.

For a double shot, as in a Starbucks Grande, double the chocolate to 4 teaspoons (scaled from Breville’s recipe). Our Brew Guide’s double is 18&nbsp;g of coffee to 36&nbsp;g in the cup. James Hoffmann says milk begins to degrade in flavor and texture above 154°F&nbsp;(68°C); our [milk steaming guide](/blog/how-to-steam-milk-for-lattes/) explains the stretch and the spin, and compares the temperature advice.

## Without an espresso machine

### A cocoa mocha with brewed coffee

This is Starbucks at Home’s Duo Cocoa Mocha, halved to one mug. Starbucks makes it with its Espresso Roast, brewed; any strong brewed coffee works.

- **Coffee:** 1 cup (about 240&nbsp;ml) of hot brewed coffee
- **Chocolate:** 1 tablespoon of cocoa powder and 1 tablespoon of granulated sugar
- **Milk:** ½ cup (about 120&nbsp;ml) of 2% milk
- **Toppings, if you like:** ¼ cup of whipped cream, ½&nbsp;oz (about 14&nbsp;g) of dark chocolate, melted, and 1 tablespoon of cocoa nibs
- **Makes:** one mug of about 1½ cups (12&nbsp;US&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz, about 355&nbsp;ml) before toppings (calculated)

1. Heat the milk in a saucepan over medium-low heat, stirring often, for 3 to 5 minutes, until it’s steaming.
2. Whisk the milk with a frother or hand blender until it’s frothy.
3. Put the cocoa powder and sugar in a large mug, pour in the hot coffee, and stir until no dry powder is left.
4. Pour the frothed milk over the coffee.
5. If you like, top it with the whipped cream, a drizzle of the melted chocolate and the cocoa nibs.

Starbucks’ own steps divide the coffee, cocoa and sugar between the mugs without saying to stir; step 3 adds the stir, as Breville does with its chocolate. Our [milk frother guide](/blog/best-milk-frothers-home-baristas/) compares handheld and jug frothers if you’d rather not use a pan.

### With a moka pot

A moka pot gives you stronger coffee than a filter brewer: our Brew Guide brews a moka pot at about 1:10, coffee to water, against 1:17 for a drip machine at its classic strength. It isn’t espresso. Use it in place of the shot or the brewed coffee above, and stir the chocolate into it while it’s hot. This is James Hoffmann’s method, from our [moka pot guide](/blog/moka-pot-brewing-guide/):

1. Grind the coffee a little coarser than for espresso and fill the basket level with the rim. Do not press it down.
2. Boil the kettle and pour the hot water into the base, to just below the safety valve and never over it.
3. Drop in the basket, wipe any grounds off the rim, and screw the top on firmly, holding the hot base with a towel.
4. Put the pot on low to medium heat, with any flame no wider than the base.
5. Take the pot off the heat as soon as it starts to gurgle or sputter.
6. Run the base under cold water to stop the brew, stir the coffee in the top, and serve straight away.

A basket that holds 15&nbsp;g makes about 120&nbsp;g, or about 4&nbsp;US&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz, of coffee (the Brew Guide’s estimate): about half the coffee in Starbucks at Home’s mug, but stronger, so adjust the chocolate and milk to taste.

### With an AeroPress

AeroPress says its Original can brew up to three shots of what it calls “espresso style” coffee for lattes and cappuccinos; it isn’t espresso either. Our [AeroPress guide](/blog/why-and-how-you-should-use-an-aeropress/) has the maker’s method. Use the coffee as you would the shot in Breville’s recipe.

## Adjusting it

- **Tastes like hot chocolate:** use less chocolate or more coffee, such as a second shot.
- **Too sweet:** switch from sauce or drinking chocolate to cocoa powder, and add sugar to taste.
- **Coffee too weak:** use espresso or moka pot coffee rather than filter coffee.
- **Powder left in the bottom of the cup:** stir it into the hot coffee until none is left dry, before the milk goes in.

If you like coffee with a little milk and no chocolate, the [cortado](/blog/what-is-a-cortado/) is worth trying next.
