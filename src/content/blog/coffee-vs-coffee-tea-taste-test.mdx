While browsing teas, we found something unusual: **coffee fruit tea**, a tea brewed from the dried fruit of the coffee plant. Its package said it tastes just like coffee. We put that claim to a side-by-side test, and what we learned sent us down a much deeper rabbit hole.

The tasting below is from that August 2020 video. The video calls the dried pieces "tea leaves" in places; they're dried coffee fruit, as the video itself says once the pieces are out of the bag. The background, caffeine figures and food rules were checked on October 18, 2026.

## The taste test

**The tea.** The bag was clear, so you could see the dried fruit inside. The pieces looked a little like coffee beans and smelled a little like dried dates. The back of the bag gave two ways to brew it: about two tablespoons to 8 oz of cold water, left for 24 hours, or the same two tablespoons to 8 oz of hot water for 5 to 10 minutes.

We went hot: three heaping teaspoon scoops, aiming for the two tablespoons, in a disposable tea bag, 8 oz of just-boiled water, and a 7-minute steep, in the middle of the bag's range. A few ice cubes went in so it could be tasted right away, beside a cup of regular brewed coffee.

**Coffee fruit tea.** "That is really good." The strongest flavor was date, which gave it a little natural sweetness, without a strongly fruity taste. It was very smooth and went down easily: not bitter, not weak, not strong, so the bag's directions were spot on. It brewed darker than most teas, which made it look more like coffee than it tasted. It would make a really good cold brew too.

**Regular coffee, black.** It definitely tasted like coffee, and next to the fruit tea it was nothing exciting. It needed cream and sugar.

**Does it taste like coffee?** No. If you love coffee and switched to this, you'd know it wasn't coffee, but you probably wouldn't be disappointed.

**The verdict.** Given the choice between plain black coffee and this coffee fruit tea, **we'd pick the tea.** That's surprising coming from a coffee company. The tea was enjoyable without anything added, and the coffee wasn't: when you have to alter a drink to make it good, that tells you something. It was also reasonably priced.

## What cascara is

James Hoffmann's *World Atlas of Coffee* describes the Yemeni original, qesher (also written q'shr or qishr): the dried, but not roasted, husks removed from coffee cherries, which in Yemen are often brewed like tea. Producers in Central America have been experimenting with the same product, there called cascara, which, Hoffmann notes, is usually just the dried fruit, rather than the fruit plus the dried parchment found in qesher. Our [coffee cherry anatomy guide](/blog/coffee-cherry-anatomy-explained/) shows where the skin, pulp and parchment sit.

The drink is old. In Thurston's *Coffee: A Comprehensive Guide*, Jonathan Morris writes that the Oromo people of Ethiopia simmered dried coffee husks in boiling water for about fifteen minutes, making an infusion called buno locally and qishr in Arabic, and that Sufis in Yemen took up qishr in the fifteenth century. Mark Pendergrast's history describes the early Ethiopian drink as made from lightly roasted husks, so not every version was unroasted. Jeff Koehler, writing about the market in Harar, Ethiopia, lists four coffee products on sale there in the mid-nineteenth century, still sold today: ordinary beans, the best beans, dried husks and the leaves. The European Union's 2022 authorization, below, rests on cascara's history of safe food use in Yemen, Ethiopia and Bolivia.

**Why it doesn't taste like coffee.** Coffee's familiar flavor develops when the seed is roasted; our guide to [how coffee is roasted](/blog/how-is-coffee-roasted/) explains what happens. Cascara is the fruit around the seed, dried rather than roasted, so it keeps a dried-fruit character instead.

"Coffee tea" can also mean coffee-leaf tea, made from the plant's dried leaves, which is a different product with its own EU authorization. The video's tea was cascara.

## How much caffeine is in it

Less than in coffee, but not none. Two lab analyses measured it in cascara drinks. The per-cup figures below are calculated from their measurements per liter, and the tea figure from the USDA's per-100 g value:

| Drink, per 8 fl oz (237 ml) | Caffeine | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Cascara drink made in Switzerland | about 54 mg | Heeger 2017 |
| Cascara cold-brewed 24 h | about 29–32 mg | Muriqi 2025 |
| Black tea, brewed | about 47 mg | USDA |
| Drip coffee | 105–195 mg | Caffeine Calculator |

- **Heeger and colleagues, 2017,** analyzed six dried coffee pulps and a cascara drink produced in Switzerland from one of them. The drink held 226 mg of caffeine per liter. The two richest pulps, a Bourbon from Congo and a Maragogype, held 6.5 and 6.8 mg per gram of dry matter.
- **Muriqi and colleagues, 2025,** brewed one Costa Rican cascara, 9 g to 300 ml of water (3 g per 100 ml), cold for 24 hours at 41 to 68 °F (5 to 20 °C), and found 123–136 mg per liter, most at the warmest temperature. Their hot-brewed sample, made with just-boiled water and steeped 8 minutes, had significantly less caffeine than the cold brews.
- **Black tea:** the USDA's FoodData Central lists brewed black tea at 20 mg per 100 g.
- **Drip coffee:** our Caffeine Calculator's estimate, at the Brew Guide's dose.

Your cup depends on how much cascara you use and how long you steep it, so treat these as examples rather than a label. The EU requires cascara products with more than 150 mg of caffeine per liter to carry a "High caffeine content" warning; Heeger's drink was above that line.

The European rules for coffee-leaf tea cap its caffeine below 80 mg per liter, about 19 mg in 8 fl oz (calculated). That's a limit, not a measurement: a 2022 review by Tritsch and colleagues notes that the caffeine in coffee leaves varies strongly with the leaves' age, the climate, the species and the variety.

## How to brew it

- **The bag's method, hot:** about two tablespoons to 8 oz of hot water, steeped 5 to 10 minutes. We used just-boiled water and 7 minutes.
- **The bag's method, cold:** the same two tablespoons to 8 oz of cold water, left for 24 hours.
- **The EU's description:** the 2022 regulation says typical infusions use up to 6 g of cascara per 100 ml of hot water above 167 °F (75 °C).

Our advice: a tablespoon is a loose measure for pieces of dried fruit, so if you want the same cup twice, weigh what you use. Strain it through a tea bag, an infuser or a fine strainer.

## Where it stands as a food

**In the EU,** Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2022/47 of January 13, 2022, authorized dried cherry pulp of *Coffea arabica* and *Coffea canephora*, and its infusion, as a "traditional food from a third country" under the EU's novel food rules. Nestlé and Lavazza had notified it in 2020. It may be sold for coffee, tea, herbal and fruit infusions, coffee substitutes and mixes, and ready-to-drink beverages, under names such as "coffee cherry pulp" or "cascara (coffee cherry pulp)." Products with more than 150 mg of caffeine per liter must say: "High caffeine content. Not recommended for children or pregnant or breast-feeding women."

Coffee-leaf tea came first. Regulation (EU) 2020/917 of July 1, 2020, authorized "infusion from coffee leaves" as a traditional food, for use as a herbal infusion, with caffeine below 80 mg per liter.

**In the US,** we found no FDA page about cascara tea. The FDA's inventory of GRAS notices, searched October 18, 2026, lists none for cascara or dried coffee cherry pulp. Its one coffee-fruit entry, GRN 868, is a coffee fruit *extract* to be used as an ingredient at 20 to 300 mg a serving, which the FDA closed in March 2020 with a "no questions" letter. That's a different product from the dried fruit you steep, so this page makes no claim about cascara's US status.

This page makes no health claims for cascara either: the studies above measured what's in the drink, not what it does in people. If you're pregnant or breastfeeding, the EU warning above is the agency's line; ask your clinician about caffeine.

## Who might like it

If you go in expecting coffee, you'll be let down. If you go in expecting a smooth, date-like fruit tea, it's worth a try, especially in the afternoon, when you want something with a little caffeine but much less than a cup of coffee.
