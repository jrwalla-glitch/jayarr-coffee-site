Taste tells you a cup is sour or bitter. A refractometer tells you how much coffee is dissolved in it, which, with two weights you already know, tells you how much of your coffee you extracted. It doesn't tell you whether the cup is good; it gives you a number to compare against how the cup tasted.

## What a refractometer measures

A refractometer measures how much a drop of coffee bends light, its refractive index, and converts that into TDS: the percentage of the drink that is dissolved coffee. DiFluid's guide to its R2 Extract explains that the conversion depends on temperature, which is why a meter reads the sample's temperature too.

From TDS, extraction yield is arithmetic:

**Extraction yield (%) = drink weight (g) × TDS (%) ÷ coffee dose (g)**

Two worked examples, calculated:

- **Pour-over:** 20 g of coffee gives 300 g in the cup at 1.3% TDS. That's 3.9 g dissolved, and 3.9 ÷ 20 = **19.5%**.
- **Espresso:** an 18 g dose gives a 36 g shot at 10% TDS. That's 3.6 g dissolved, and 3.6 ÷ 18 = **20%**.

For immersion brews such as a French press, use all the water you added rather than the drink weight, and take the reading before you dilute. Our [extraction yield guide](/blog/coffee-extraction-yield-explained/) explains why, and what the SCA's 18 to 22% reference range means.

## Taking a reading you can trust

Most bad readings come from the sample, not the meter. These steps combine Scott Rao's procedure in *Espresso Extraction* (2013), Jonathan Gagné's measurements on his *Coffee ad Astra* blog, and the makers' own instructions.

1. **Zero the meter first.** Rao zeroes with distilled water every day, keeps the coffee sample within 1&nbsp;°C of the zeroing water, and re-zeros at least every 30 minutes. DiFluid also allows zeroing with your brewing water, which takes the water's own dissolved solids out of the reading; if you zero with distilled water instead, DiFluid says to measure your water and subtract it.
2. **Mix before you sample.** Gagné stirs the cup thoroughly as he draws his sample.
3. **Filter espresso.** Rao waits one minute after the shot for gas to leave it, draws 4 to 5 ml from just below the crema, and pushes it through a syringe filter. In Mitch Hale's tests on a VST meter (20 shots, summarized by Gagné), unfiltered espresso read about 0.38% TDS too high, and the gap itself varied by about 0.11%, so you can't simply subtract it. On an 18 g to 36 g shot, 0.38% is 0.76 percentage points of extraction (calculated). VST says its syringe filters are for espresso and for any coffee brewed through metal mesh or perforated filters; Gagné's method for paper-filtered coffee uses none.
4. **Cool the sample.** A warm drop evaporates on the meter, which concentrates it and pushes the reading up, DiFluid warns, and a sample warmer or cooler than the zeroing water reads wrong. In Gagné's tests, a sample only 5°F&nbsp;(about 3&nbsp;°C) off read up to 0.08% TDS too low. On a 20 g to 300 g pour-over, that's 1.2 percentage points of extraction (calculated). He cools a small sample quickly in a glass pipette under the tap, then lets it rest.
5. **Read more than once.** Rao presses the read button several times and expects the readings to agree within the meter's stated precision; if they don't, the sample probably wasn't filtered well or the glass wasn't clean. DiFluid suggests a fresh drop for each new reading rather than re-reading the one on the prism.

Our guide to [strength and extraction](/blog/coffee-strength-vs-extraction/) covers what to change once you have the numbers.

## What the number tells you that taste can't

Rao's case for the refractometer is that taste can mislead you while you dial in. In his example, a shot at 17.0% extraction tastes sour; grinding finer gives 17.7%, which may taste marginally worse; and a barista guided by taste alone would go back, when one more step finer, to 18.5%, would have brought out "ripeness and caramels." He recommends tasting every shot and measuring often while you adjust, so you learn how the numbers match the flavors.

Rao describes three more uses:

- **Burr wear.** He recommends that every café record weekly extraction data. As burrs dull, he expects extraction and TDS to decline slowly over several months while bitterness and astringency rise, and he gives a hypothetical in which the best extraction for one coffee falls from 20.5% with sharp burrs to 19.0% after 30,000 shots.
- **Roast development.** He has frequently seen underdeveloped roasts extract 1 to 4 percentage points less than another batch of the same coffee.
- **Testing your own changes.** A friend of his found that each of five changes could raise his average extraction by up to 0.5 percentage points: a 58.4 mm tamper instead of 58.0 mm, sharp new burrs, E61-style preinfusion, a different water source, or a darker or better-developed roast.

Taste is still a hypothesis and the refractometer gives the number: neither replaces the other.

## Three refractometers, as their makers publish them

This is a research comparison from the makers' own pages, checked October 15, 2026, not a test. The specs are as published by each maker, all in % TDS; a dash means the maker's page doesn't say.

| Meter | Range | Accuracy | Precision |
|---|---|---|---|
| [DiFluid R2 Extract](https://digitizefluid.com/products/r2-extract) | 0–30% | ±0.03% | ±0.02% |
| [VST LAB Coffee III](https://store.vstapps.com/products/vst-lab-coffee-iii-refractometer) | 0–20% | ±0.03% below 5% TDS; ±0.05% from 5% | 0.01% |
| [Atago PAL-COFFEE (TDS)](https://www.atago.net/product/index.php?l=en&k=CGD47132), Cat. No. 4532 | 0–22% | ±0.15% | – |

What those accuracy figures mean in extraction, calculated for the two recipes above:

| Stated accuracy | Pour-over, 20 g to 300 g | Espresso, 18 g to 36 g |
|---|---|---|
| ±0.03% TDS | ±0.45 points | ±0.06 points |
| ±0.05% TDS (VST at espresso strength) | Not applicable | ±0.10 points |
| ±0.15% TDS | ±2.25 points | ±0.30 points |

The difference comes from the ratio. A pour-over has 15 g of drink for every gram of coffee, so any TDS error is multiplied 15 times; a 1:2 espresso multiplies it twice. All three meters' stated accuracies are smaller than the half-point changes Rao's friend measured on espresso. For filter coffee, the Atago's stated ±0.15% is about 2¼ points of extraction either way: a band 4½ points wide, wider than the SCA's whole 18 to 22% reference range.

Accuracy matters most when you compare your numbers with someone else's or with a published range. When you compare your own brews on one meter, repeatability matters more; DiFluid and VST publish a precision figure, and Atago's product page lists only its 0.01% resolution.

**DiFluid R2 Extract.** DiFluid gives a reading time of about 4 seconds. It connects to DiFluid's CoffeeOS app, which calculates extraction and plots each brew on a brewing control chart. DiFluid's guide says to filter espresso through paper for the most accurate readings, and gives an operating range of 5 to 40&nbsp;°C. DiFluid's US store listed it at about $230 when checked October 15, 2026. <a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=DiFluid+R2+Extract+Coffee+Refractometer&tag=jayarrcoffee-20" rel="sponsored noopener">Search for the DiFluid R2 Extract on Amazon</a>.

**VST LAB Coffee III.** VST includes its CoffeeTools software for calculating extraction, uses a sapphire prism, and lists a 15 to 40&nbsp;°C temperature range. VST gives a reading time of 2 to 3 seconds in its feature list, and under 2 seconds, typical, in its spec table. VST's store listed it at $759 on sale ($902 regular) when checked October 15, 2026, with syringe filter kits as optional bundles. Gagné used a VST for his concentration tests. VST sells it through <a href="https://store.vstapps.com/products/vst-lab-coffee-iii-refractometer">its own store</a>.

**Atago PAL-COFFEE (TDS).** Atago describes continuous measurement for stable readings of hot, fresh brews, and also sells a Brix model and a dual Brix/TDS model; make sure you're buying the TDS scale. ATAGO Direct listed the 4532 at $326 when checked October 15, 2026.
