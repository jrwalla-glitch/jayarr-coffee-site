Oat milk and dairy both work in coffee, but they get there differently. Dairy brings its own protein, fat and sugar. A barista oat milk is formulated for the job, with added oil and an acidity regulator. Here's what's in each, per named product, and what the foam research shows.

A few things stand out:

- **Protein.** Whole milk has about 2.7 times the protein of either Oatly carton (our calculation: 8.1 g against 3 g).
- **Fat.** The Barista Edition's 7 g is close to whole milk's 8 g, while the plain 4 Ingredient carton has 1 g. The difference is the added oil.
- **Sugar.** Milk's sugar is its own lactose. Oatly labels its 7 g as added sugar and says it's "created when we liquefy our oats": its enzymes break oat starch down into smaller sugars such as maltose.
- **Calcium.** The Barista Edition is fortified with calcium salts; the 4 Ingredient carton isn't, and lists 0 mg.

## What makes an oat milk a barista edition

The ingredient lists show the difference. Oatly's 4 Ingredient oatmilk is an oat base (water and oats), sea salt and citrus fiber. Its Barista Edition is an oat base plus low erucic acid rapeseed oil, and 2% or less of dipotassium phosphate, calcium carbonate, tricalcium phosphate, sea salt, dicalcium phosphate and vitamins.

Two of those additions do the coffee work:

- **The oil** brings the fat up from 1 g to 7 g a cup.
- **Dipotassium phosphate** is there, in Oatly's words, "as an acidity regulator", and Oatly says it "keeps our Barista Edition Oatmilk from separating when it hits your favorite hot drinks."

Oatly describes the Barista Edition as "formulated to work particularly well in hot beverages, like coffee." Other brands use different oils, buffers and thickeners, so read the label rather than the word "barista". Our [oat milk label test](/blog/your-6-dollar-oat-milk-is-wrecking-your-latte/) shows what to look for.

## Foam and texture

Two recent studies compared dairy and plant milks directly.

**Hassan and colleagues (2024)** compared cow's and goat's milk with six popular plant milks (almond, oat, soy, pea, coconut and rice), using an electric frother at 50°F&nbsp;(10°C) and 149°F&nbsp;(65°C) and two lab instruments:

- Of the eight milks, oat produced the longest-lasting foams and rice the least foam.
- Frothed hot, the animal milks made more foam than the three plant milks that foamed best (oat, pea and soy).
- Frothed cold, oat, soy and almond beat cow's milk on both foam volume and lifetime.
- Most of the plant milks were more viscous than milk, which the authors put down to added polysaccharide thickeners.

**Tarasov and colleagues (2025)** tested plant drinks labeled "Barista" against cow's milk for cappuccino:

- Steamed at 149°F&nbsp;(65°C), the plant drinks' foaming capacity ranged from 93% to 202%, against 197% for milk. The authors judged all of them viable alternatives to milk when steamed, but not when foamed by mechanical stirring.
- Foaming didn't correlate with the drinks' protein content.
- Among the plant drinks, more fat went with less foam when they were stirred, with a similar trend for steaming.

So whole milk's extra protein doesn't make a barista oat milk unworkable. How a drink is formulated, and how you foam it, matters more than its protein figure alone suggests. For pitcher size, stretching and temperatures, see our guide to [steaming milk for lattes](/blog/how-to-steam-milk-for-lattes/).

In a latte the milk is most of the drink, so whichever you choose shapes much of the cup's flavor and texture.

## Choosing between them

- **For the most protein per cup:** whole milk, at about 8 g against 3 g.
- **Dairy-free and hot, with foam:** a barista oat milk, which is formulated with oil and an acidity regulator for hot coffee. Oatly's plain 4 Ingredient carton has neither.
- **Iced drinks:** foam matters less, so the choice comes down to taste and nutrition. In Hassan's cold tests, oat foamed better than cow's milk.
- **Watching added sugar:** Oatly's 7 g per cup is labeled as added sugar; whole milk's 12 g of lactose isn't.

If you avoid dairy or oats for a medical reason, follow your clinician's advice on what to use instead.

## Beyond oat

Hassan's results put pea and soy alongside oat among the plant milks that foamed best when hot, and rice at the bottom.
