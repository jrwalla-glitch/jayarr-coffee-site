A cappuccino is espresso with steamed milk and a deep cap of foam. Asking for it “wet” or “dry” changes the balance between the liquid milk and the foam, and “bone dry” takes the liquid milk out altogether.

Our 2020 video predates this revision; the text below supersedes it.

## Wet, dry and bone dry

Starbucks explained the terms in a 2016 post, “Wet vs. Dry Cappuccino.” All cappuccinos, it said, have espresso and a smooth layer of foamed milk; the orders differ in how the milk is split between liquid and foam.

| Order | Steamed milk | Foam | Starbucks’ wording, 2016 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Super wet | Most | Least | “A super wet cappuccino is a latte” |
| Wet | More | Less | “more steamed milk and less foam” |
| Dry | Less | More | “less steamed milk and more foam” |
| Bone dry | None | All of it | “no steamed milk at all–just a foam pillow and espresso” |

These aren’t on every menu. Starbucks’ US web menu, checked October 18, 2026, offers its Cappuccino with “Extra Foam,” “Foam,” “Light Foam” or “No Foam,” not wet or dry. If a café doesn’t recognize the term, ask for espresso with foam only and no steamed milk.

## What a regular cappuccino holds

There’s no single standard, but published recipes give a sense of the amounts:

- **James Hoffmann** writes that the “rule of thirds,” equal parts espresso, milk and foam, has no root in tradition. The traditional Italian cappuccino he describes is 150–175&nbsp;ml made on a single shot, with espresso, milk and foam at 1:2:2. That works out to about 30–35&nbsp;ml of espresso, 60–70&nbsp;ml of milk and 60–70&nbsp;ml of foam (calculated from his ratio).
- **La Cimbali** pours a single espresso with 125&nbsp;ml of hot frothed milk into a 150–160&nbsp;ml cup, with about 1&nbsp;cm of foam on top.
- **Scott Rao** makes his cappuccino with “very aerated milk,” deep and plush, so that once the drink has separated, the froth is about ½&nbsp;inch deep, in a 6 to 8&nbsp;oz cup.
- **Starbucks’** Grande Cappuccino is two shots in a 16&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz cup, with 2% milk by default.

Going by Hoffmann’s ratio, a bone dry version drops the 60–70&nbsp;ml of liquid milk, and foam fills the space instead.

## How milk foam forms and drains

A bone dry cappuccino is all foam, so it helps to know what makes good foam.

- **Protein holds the bubbles.** Hoffmann writes that it’s the protein in milk that supports the bubbles, so milk foams well whether it’s skimmed or whole. Fat changes the drink instead: in his description, a skim-milk cappuccino tastes of coffee immediately and intensely but briefly, while whole milk gives less intense flavors that last longer.
- **Use fresh milk.** Near its use-by date, Hoffmann says, milk still foams as normal, but the bubbles soon break down.
- **Add the air early.** Rao finishes stretching, or aerating, the milk before it reaches 100°F, because hotter milk is harder to froth well; Hoffmann adds air until he has the foam he wants, ideally before the milk feels warm. A cappuccino takes more air than a latte, and a bone dry one more still.
- **Foam and liquid separate.** Rao writes that a cappuccino or latte loses its mouthfeel within a minute or two as the milk separates, and shows a latte fully separated two minutes after it was made. Hoffmann notes that if foamed milk sits for a few seconds, the milk drains off the surface of the larger bubbles and they turn brittle, so a couple of taps pops them. For most drinks you swirl the milk and foam back together. For a bone dry cappuccino, the separation is what you want: the foam rises and the liquid milk sinks, so you can spoon off the foam.

Neither Rao nor Hoffmann covers plant milks. In a 2024 lab comparison of six of them, Lena Hassan and colleagues found that, frothed hot, oat, pea and soy foamed best, though cow’s and goat’s milk made more foam than any plant milk. Our [plant milk guide](/blog/best-plant-milks-for-coffee/) has more.

## How to make a bone dry cappuccino at home

This uses an espresso machine with a steam wand. Hoffmann’s and Cimbali’s cappuccinos are built on a single shot and Starbucks’ Grande on two; this recipe uses the Brew Guide’s double.

- **Espresso:** a double, 18&nbsp;g of coffee to 36&nbsp;g in the cup.
- **Milk:** cold, fresh whole milk, with the pitcher a third to half full, as Rao suggests. Only its foam goes into the drink, so you’ll have liquid milk left over.
- **Cup:** a cappuccino cup of 150–160&nbsp;ml, about 5 to 5½&nbsp;US&nbsp;fl&nbsp;oz.
- **Makes:** one bone dry cappuccino that fills that cup.

1. Pull a double espresso, 18&nbsp;g of coffee to 36&nbsp;g in the cup, into a 150 to 160&nbsp;ml cappuccino cup.
2. Pour cold fresh milk into the pitcher, a third to half full. Stretch it for longer than you would for a latte, adding air until the foam has grown, and finish the stretch before the milk feels warm.
3. Sink the steam tip and spin the milk until the jug is hot to touch, about 140–149°F&nbsp;(60–65°C).
4. Let the pitcher stand for a few seconds so the liquid milk drains from the foam, then tap it on the counter to pop any large bubbles.
5. Hold back the liquid milk with a large spoon and spoon the foam onto the espresso until the cup is full.
6. Serve it straight away.

**On the temperature:** 140–149°F&nbsp;(60–65°C) is the range Breville’s Bambino manual gives. Hoffmann says milk begins to degrade in flavor and texture above 154°F&nbsp;(68°C), and that good microfoam is impossible once milk has been heated to boiling point. Rao’s café target is 150–160°F&nbsp;(66–71°C). Our [milk steaming guide](/blog/how-to-steam-milk-for-lattes/) walks through the stretch and the spin.

**Using the spoon.** Rao describes the opposite move for a regular cappuccino: holding the frothiest milk back with a spoon at first and letting it in at the end. Here you hold back the liquid instead and take only the foam.

### Without an espresso machine

You can make a foam-topped coffee without a machine, though it won’t be a cappuccino in the strict sense, because that starts with espresso.

- **The coffee:** a moka pot gives you strong coffee on the stove; our [moka pot guide](/blog/moka-pot-brewing-guide/) has the method, and it isn’t espresso.
- **The foam:** Starbucks at Home heats milk in a saucepan over medium-low heat, stirring often, for 3 to 5 minutes until it’s steaming, then whisks it with a frother or hand blender until frothy. Let it stand for a moment so the liquid drains, then spoon off the foam. Our [milk frother guide](/blog/best-milk-frothers-home-baristas/) compares the tools.

## Bone dry, macchiato or cortado?

A bone dry cappuccino is easy to confuse with two smaller drinks. A [macchiato](/blog/what-is-a-macchiato/), in its Italian form, is espresso marked with just a little milk foam, so it’s much smaller. A [cortado](/blog/what-is-a-cortado/) is espresso cut with a little warm milk. A bone dry cappuccino fills a cappuccino cup with foam.
