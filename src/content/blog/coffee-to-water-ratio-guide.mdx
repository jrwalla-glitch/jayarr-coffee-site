A ratio is the simplest recipe in coffee: how much water for each gram of coffee, both weighed. Jessica Easto, author of *Craft Coffee: A Manual*, notes that most coffee professionals in the US start between 1:15 and 1:17, and that she uses anything from 1:12 to 1:17 depending on the brewer. Below are the starting ratios our Brew Guide uses for each brewer, the amounts for common cup sizes, and how to adjust.

## How to read a ratio

**For filter and immersion coffee, the ratio counts the water you pour in.** At 1:16, 22 g of coffee takes 22 × 16 = 352 g of water. Weigh both on a kitchen scale; for water, 1 g is 1 ml.

**Your cup holds less than the water you poured.** The wet grounds hold on to some of it. The Brew Guide reckons on about 2 g of water per gram of coffee, so those 22 g of coffee and 352 g of water give about 308 g of coffee, about 10.4 US fl oz (308 ml), in the cup (calculated: 352 − 22 × 2). That's why the Brew Guide works backwards from the cup you want: a 12 fl oz mug at 1:16 needs about 25 g of coffee and 406 g of water, not 355 g of water.

**For espresso, the ratio counts the drink that comes out.** An 18 g dose that makes a 36 g shot is 1:2. Scott Rao, in *Espresso Extraction: Measurement and Mastery*, defines his normale as a shot weighing about 1.5 to 2 times the dry dose, and calls shorter ratios ristretto and longer ones lungo. The Brew Guide offers 1:1.5, 1:2 and 1:2.5. Our guide to [ristretto and lungo shots](/blog/whats-different-about-ristretto-or-lungo-espresso-shots/) goes further.

**For cold brew, the ratio makes a concentrate.** You brew strong and dilute it to serve.

A few brewers need a note:

- **Pour-over.** The classic 1:16 matches the V60 recipe in our [pour-over guide](/blog/ultimate-pour-over-coffee-guide-and-best-v60-technique/), Jonathan Gagné's 22 g of coffee to 352 g of water.
- **Drip machine.** The Brew Guide starts a little lighter, at 1:17. The SCA's standard for certifying home brewers tests them at 55 g of coffee per kilogram of water, about 1:18 (calculated: 1,000 ÷ 55 = 18.2). Our [batch brew guide](/blog/batch-brew-drip-coffee-guide/) covers drip machines.
- **French press.** The Brew Guide adapts Easto's eight-minute French press. She brews it at 1:14, the Brew Guide's stronger setting; the Brew Guide starts at 1:15.
- **AeroPress.** The chamber holds about 230 g once the coffee is in, so for a bigger cup the Brew Guide has you top up with hot water after pressing. AeroPress's own Signature Brew measures differently, by its scoop (16 to 18 g of medium-ground coffee) and by filling the chamber to the 4 mark; if you prefer the maker's recipe, follow it as written.
- **Moka pot.** The pot sets both amounts: fill the basket level and the base with water to just below the valve. The Brew Guide's figure of about 1:10 is a description of that, not a ratio to choose; for a milder cup, it suggests adding hot water to the coffee.
- **Cold brew.** The Brew Guide uses Easto's French-press cold brew: 96 g of coffee to 600 g of cold water for 12 hours, then diluted one to one with water or milk to serve.
- **Turkish coffee** isn't in the Brew Guide; our [Turkish coffee guide](/blog/turkish-coffee-guide/) gives its method.

## How much coffee per cup

For pour-over, by the Brew Guide's arithmetic, at 1:15 (stronger), 1:16 (classic) and 1:17 (lighter). Each cell is grams of coffee + grams of water to pour; the cup size is what ends up in the mug.

| Cup | 1:15 | 1:16 | 1:17 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 fl oz (237 ml) | 18.2 + 273 | 16.9 + 270 | 15.8 + 268 |
| 10 fl oz (296 ml) | 22.7 + 341 | 21.1 + 338 | 19.7 + 335 |
| 12 fl oz (355 ml) | 27.3 + 409 | 25.3 + 406 | 23.7 + 402 |
| 16 fl oz (473 ml) | 36.4 + 546 | 33.8 + 541 | 31.5 + 536 |

The 16 fl oz row uses a Chemex, because the Brew Guide caps a V60 at about 32 g of coffee.

"Cups" on a coffee maker are usually smaller than a mug. Moccamaster, for example, rates its KBGV coffee maker at 1.25 L and 10 cups (as published by the maker), which works out to 125 ml, about 4 fl oz, per cup (calculated). Weigh the water your mug holds before you scale a recipe to it.

## Weigh it, don't scoop it

The National Coffee Association's "Golden Ratio" for drip coffee is 1 to 2 tablespoons of ground coffee for every 6 ounces of water. The Brew Guide reckons a tablespoon of ground coffee at about 5 g, so that rule covers anything from about 1:35 to about 1:18 (calculated: 6 fl oz is about 177 g of water). Two tablespoons land near the Brew Guide's lighter drip ratio, 1:18; one is very weak.

Spoons are approximate because the weight of a spoonful changes. Easto points out that beans vary in size, so a tablespoon of one coffee can weigh a gram or more different from another, and that a tablespoon of fine grounds weighs differently from one of coarse grounds. A scale removes the guesswork, and it lets you repeat a cup you liked.

## Adjusting by taste

The ratio sets strength: how concentrated the cup is. It does much less to extraction, which is what makes a cup sour or bitter. Our guide to [strength and extraction](/blog/coffee-strength-vs-extraction/) explains the difference. So use the ratio for a cup that tastes clean but too weak or too strong, and change the grind for one that tastes sour or bitter.

| What you taste | One change to try |
|---|---|
| Clean but thin and watery | One step stronger: more coffee for the same water, 1:16 to 1:15 |
| Clean but heavy and intense | One step lighter, 1:16 to 1:17, or add hot water to the cup |
| Sour and thin | Grind one step finer before touching the ratio |
| Bitter and drying | Grind one step coarser before touching the ratio |
| Bitter and heavy | Grind one step coarser; if it's still heavy, use more water for the same coffee |

For a 12 fl oz V60, one step stronger takes the coffee from 25.3 g to 27.3 g; one step lighter takes it to 23.7 g. Change one thing at a time, so you know which change helped. Our guides to [grind size](/blog/coffee-grind-size-guide-what-grind-for-every-brew-method/) and [extraction yield](/blog/coffee-extraction-yield-explained/) cover the other levers.
