Mexico grows more coffee than most drinkers would guess: USDA forecasts 4.1 million 60 kg bags for 2026/27, about 2 percent of the world's crop and the tenth-largest total on USDA's table (both calculated). Much of it goes to the United States, its main export market. Here's what it tends to taste like, where it grows and what to look for on a bag.

Our short video covers the same ground: the three regions it names, the nutty-chocolate profile, and two names worth knowing, Oaxaca Pluma and Altura Coatepec. It predates this revision, and it calls Chiapas a city; it's a state, in Mexico's far south.

## What Mexican coffee tastes like

As our video describes it, Mexican coffee generally has nutty notes, chocolatey undertones and hints of mild caramel, with light to medium acidity and a subtle white-wine finish. Those are tendencies, not a promise about any one bag, and the range is wide:

- **James Hoffmann**, in *The World Atlas of Coffee*, describes Mexican coffees as running from lighter-bodied, delicate cups to sweeter ones with caramel, toffee or chocolate.
- **The importer Café Imports** writes that Mexico long had a reputation for mild, nutty, low-grown coffee used in blends, but that high-grown Mexican coffee "has extremely interesting complex citric and malic acidity," with chocolate and toffee sweetness and a clean cup.
- **Roasters' notes:** across eight Mexican lots that roasters described from January to March 2026, our Coffee Origins sample found chocolate and lemon most often (three lots each), then apple, black tea, orange and lime.

So a bag from a named, high-grown farm can taste noticeably brighter than a supermarket "Mexican" blend. The region on the label helps too, as the next section shows.

## Where coffee grows in Mexico

Our video names three principal regions: Chiapas, Veracruz and Oaxaca. By volume, USDA's Mexico City office now lists Chiapas, Veracruz and Puebla as the three main producing states, together more than 80 percent of national production, with Chiapas the largest. Hoffmann's regional entries give the altitudes and harvest months for the three he covers:

| Region | Altitude; harvest (Hoffmann) | How our video describes it |
|---|---|---|
| Chiapas | 1,000–1,750 m (3,300–5,750 ft); Nov–Mar | Medium body, rich and delicate, more acidic than other Mexican coffees, with "an abrupt tang" |
| Oaxaca | 900–1,700 m (3,000–5,600 ft); Dec–Mar | Light body and light acidity |
| Veracruz | 800–1,700 m (2,600–5,600 ft); Dec–Mar | Rich and nutty, with hints of chocolate |

Hoffmann lists Bourbon, Typica, Caturra and Maragogype as the varieties in all three. The same months appear in our [coffee harvest calendar](/blog/coffee-harvest-calendar/), which also gives Café Imports' typical US arrivals for Mexico, May to June. Windows shift with altitude and the year's weather.

### Chiapas

Chiapas borders Guatemala, and Hoffmann notes that its Sierra Madre range gives it both the altitude and the volcanic soils that suit coffee. It's also where Mexico's robusta grows: USDA names Chiapas as the main robusta producer, ahead of Veracruz, Puebla and Oaxaca, so "Chiapas" on a label doesn't by itself mean high-grown arabica. Our video singles Chiapas out for some of Mexico's highest-quality coffee.

### Oaxaca and Pluma

Hoffmann writes that most farmers in Oaxaca own less than 2 hectares and that several large cooperatives work there. The coffee our video recommends from this state is Oaxaca Pluma. The name points to Pluma Hidalgo, a town in Oaxaca, and also to a variety: Coffee Review's September 2021 review of one lot gives its origin as "Pluma Hidalgo, Oaxaca State, Mexico" and the variety as Pluma Hidalgo.

### Veracruz and Coatepec

Veracruz runs along the Gulf of Mexico. Hoffmann says it has some of Mexico's lowest coffee production, but also some very high plantations around Coatepec that produce better coffee. That matches our video, which calls Veracruz coffee generally lower in quality than Chiapas but singles out the prized Altura Coatepec ("altura" is Spanish for "height"). Hoffmann cites reports of plantations around Veracruz as early as 1790.

### Puebla

Puebla isn't in Hoffmann's list or our video, but USDA now counts it among the three biggest producing states. It forecasts Puebla to have the highest yields in 2026/27, more than double the national average, which it credits to good soils, rust-resistant plantings and infrastructure near major cities.

## How much Mexico grows, and leaf rust

USDA forecasts 4.135 million 60 kg bags for 2026/27 (October to September): 3.575 million arabica and 560,000 robusta, so about 86 percent arabica (calculated; USDA's Mexico City office rounds it to 85 and 15). That's up 1 percent on the year, and USDA credits two years of high prices, better phytosanitary standards and more robusta. Mexico drinks a lot of its own coffee too: USDA forecasts about 3.2 million bags in 2026/27, with instant coffee around 60 percent of it.

Production hasn't been steady. USDA describes years of widespread coffee leaf rust in Mexico, and in its series Mexico's crop fell from 4.65 million bags in 2012/13 to 2.30 million in 2015/16, about half (calculated); it has recovered only partly. USDA reports that farmers have gradually replanted arabica with rust-resistant varieties, many not yet bearing fruit, and that robusta is expanding because it resists disease, grows at lower altitudes and is in demand from soluble-coffee makers. Our history of [coffee leaf rust](/blog/coffee-leaf-rust-hemileia-vastatrix-history/) covers the wider epidemic.

## Varieties

Our video names Mundo Novo, Caturra and Bourbon as popular Mexican varieties, and Hoffmann lists Bourbon, Typica, Caturra and Maragogype. Café Imports' list is longer: Bourbon, Typica, Caturra, Mundo Novo, Maragogype, Catimor, Catuai and Garnica. World Coffee Research (WCR) notes that Garnica, a variety similar to Catuai, was developed by Mexico's coffee institute, INMECAFE, in 1960–61.

WCR rates Typica and Bourbon "Very Good" for quality potential at high altitude, and Caturra, Mundo Novo and Catuai "Good", and it rates all of them susceptible to leaf rust. That's why the newer plantings USDA describes are rust-resistant varieties instead. Our [coffee varietal guide](/blog/coffee-varietal-guide/) explains how these varieties are related.

## Smallholders, processing and certification

Hoffmann writes that most Mexican coffee comes from smallholders rather than estates, a pattern that began with land redistribution around the end of the Mexican revolution in 1920. INMECAFE, the national coffee institute, was founded in 1958 and expanded its work with small producers in 1973, providing credit and technical help and organizing production and marketing, and production grew fast. Its withdrawal from those activities began in 1989; Hoffmann writes that credit then dried up, many farmers struggled to sell, and quality suffered. Producers in Oaxaca, Chiapas and Veracruz responded by forming collectives that took over milling, technical help and selling.

Café Imports lists Mexican coffee as washed or mechanically demucilaged, and our [processing guide](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/) explains what that does to the cup. Hoffmann notes that Fair Trade and organic certifications are quite common, and Café Imports says they are especially abundant in Oaxaca, Veracruz and Chiapas. Not every Mexican coffee is certified, though: if organic matters to you, look for the certification on the bag.

Hoffmann also points out that Mexico sells so much of its coffee to the United States that excellent Mexican coffee is relatively rare elsewhere in the world. Traceability, he writes, usually goes down to a producer group or cooperative, and occasionally to a single farm.

## Café de olla

Mexico's best-known coffee drink is café de olla, "pot coffee." USDA's report describes it as roasted coffee prepared with cinnamon and sweetened, sold at breakfast time from street tricycles in Mexico City and other cities. El Universal, citing the *Diccionario enciclopédico de la Gastronomía Mexicana*, gives the base as ground coffee, water, cinnamon and piloncillo (unrefined cane sugar). Its recipe, made in a clay pot, dissolves the piloncillo with cinnamon sticks and a couple of cloves, boils it for five minutes, then adds the coffee and keeps it hot, without boiling, for five more; you can strain it or let it settle. Our video's own description notes that cinnamon suits Mexican coffee well.

## How to brew it

A mild, chocolatey Mexican coffee suits a French press: Hoffmann explains that its metal mesh removes only the larger pieces of ground coffee, so the suspended grounds and oils add body. For a cleaner cup that shows off a high-grown lot's acidity, use a paper-filter brewer: our [V60 guide](/blog/ultimate-pour-over-coffee-guide-and-best-v60-technique/) walks through a full recipe.

## What to look for on a bag

- **A state, and ideally a place within it:** Chiapas, Oaxaca (Pluma Hidalgo), Veracruz (Coatepec) or Puebla, rather than just "Mexico."
- **A producer, cooperative or farm.** Hoffmann says traceability usually reaches a producer group or cooperative.
- **Arabica, if that's what you want.** About one bag in seven of Mexico's crop is robusta (calculated from USDA), with Chiapas the main robusta producer.
- **The certification itself,** if organic or Fair Trade matters to you.
- **A recent roast date.** Our guide to [coffee freshness](/blog/coffee-freshness-roast-to-stale/) explains how long a bag stays at its best.

Mexico is a good place to start with [single-origin coffee](/blog/what-is-single-origin-coffee/). For a neighbor to compare it with, our [Guatemalan coffee guide](/blog/guatemala-coffee-review-regional-differences-and-best-coffees/) covers the regions just across the border from Chiapas.
