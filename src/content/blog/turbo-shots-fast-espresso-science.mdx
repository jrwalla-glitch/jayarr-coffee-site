Most espresso advice aims for a shot of about 25 to 30 seconds. A turbo shot deliberately runs faster: the grind is coarser, so water passes through the puck in roughly half the time. The idea draws on two studies, and they're worth reading closely, because each tested one setup and each found trade-offs. If you want the standard method first, our [dial-in guide](/blog/how-to-dial-in-espresso/) covers it.

## What a turbo shot is

There's no single recipe. Smrke, Eiermann and Yeretzian describe "turbo" espresso as a colloquial name for shots of about 10 to 20 seconds instead of the traditional 25 to 30. What makes a shot fast is a coarser grind; some versions also use less coffee, more liquid per gram of coffee or lower pump pressure.

## The 2020 study: less coffee, coarser, faster

Michael Cameron, Christopher Hendon, Jamie Foster and colleagues built a mathematical model of espresso extraction and tested it in a café. Their test recipe was 20 g of coffee to 40 g of espresso on a commercial machine at 6 bar, with a Mahlkönig EK43 grinder, an automatic tamper and 198°F&nbsp;(92°C) water. They lowered the pressure from 9 bar because at 9 bar the finest grinds clogged.

- **Finer wasn't always better.** The model predicted that extraction would keep rising as the grind got finer. In the café, extraction peaked at a middle setting and then fell at finer settings, with more shot-to-shot variation. The authors read this as water flowing unevenly through partly clogged regions of the bed.
- **Less coffee, ground much coarser.** Their model showed that a barista could reach the same extraction as the 20 g shot with 15 g of coffee ground much coarser. They warned that this may give very fast shots, under 15 seconds, a less concentrated espresso and a different flavor profile.
- **The café trial.** A café in Eugene, Oregon, that adopted the lower dose used 25% less coffee per drink across 27,850 espresso drinks over a year, and its shots routinely ran 14 seconds.
- **Not the same taste.** The authors note that a 22% extraction from the partly clogged, finer regime tastes more "complex" than a fast 22% shot from their method. Blending two shots, they suggest, could approximate that complexity, though they expect few people to bother.

Their 15 g to 40 g shot works out to a ratio of 1:2.67 (calculated: 40 ÷ 15).

## The 2024 study: fines, flow and aroma

Samo Smrke and Chahan Yeretzian of the Zurich University of Applied Sciences, with André Eiermann, studied how the finest particles shape an espresso shot. They used a single Costa Rican pulped-natural coffee, a Bentwood Vertical 63 grinder at six settings, 20 g of coffee in a 20 g VST basket, and stopped every shot at 40 g, on a Victoria Arduino Black Eagle at 9 bar. To measure the effect of fines, they sieved them from finely ground coffee and added 1, 2 or 4 g in place of the same weight of grounds.

- **Fines set the flow.** In practical terms, adding fines mainly made the bed less permeable, which slowed the shot. The share of fines and the size of the main particles together predicted the shot time.
- **Fast shots still extracted a lot.** Shots of under 10 seconds and about 15 seconds reached 17 to 18% extraction, which the authors describe as more than 80% of the highest extraction they observed. They assume the maximum is reached in shots of over 40 seconds.
- **Some aroma compounds were higher in faster shots.** Measuring volatile compounds in the cup, they found a group of 18 signals, tentatively identified compounds, whose levels fell steadily as extraction yield rose. They suggest these are lost after extraction, more so in longer shots, and conclude that "fast extraction times may be preferred."
- **But the best-tasting shot wasn't a turbo shot.** One experienced taster scored the shots, which the authors say was guidance, not a formal blind test. The best flavor for this coffee came at about 30 seconds and 19 to 20% extraction, which they note is outside the turbo range, and they suggest the coffee's green quality and roast drive where that optimum falls.

## What the studies don't show

- **That turbo shots taste better.** Neither study ran a formal tasting panel. Cameron's team says the faster shot tastes different and less complex at the same extraction; Smrke's taster preferred a 30-second shot.
- **A universal recipe.** Each study used one grinder and one coffee, on commercial machines. Cameron's group ran at 6 bar, which many machines can't be set to.
- **Anything about your machine's ideal time.** A fast shot from a coarse grind is a different recipe from a fast shot that gushed because the grind slipped. Judge it by taste, not by the clock alone.

## Trying one at home

These are starting points we've adapted from the two studies, not recipes either study published. Start from a shot you've already dialed in, say 18 g in and 36 g out in 25 to 30 seconds, timed from pressing the button. Change one thing at a time and keep a [shot log](/blog/how-to-dial-in-espresso/).

**Same coffee, same yield, coarser grind (closest to Smrke's setup).** Keep 18 g in and 36 g out, and grind coarser one step at a time until the shot runs in about 15 seconds. Expect a lighter, less concentrated shot: with the same yield and less extracted, there's less dissolved coffee in the cup. At 17.5% extraction, an 18 g to 36 g shot is about 8.8% TDS, against 10% at 20% extraction (calculated: extraction × dose ÷ yield).

**Less coffee, much coarser (closest to Cameron's).** Cut the dose by about a quarter and grind much coarser for a fast shot of roughly the same yield; Cameron's version was 15 g to 40 g. Watch the basket: Rao recommends dosing within about 10% of what a basket is designed for, so a 15 g dose suits a smaller basket better than an 18 to 20 g one. If your machine has no pressure adjustment, leave the pressure alone.

In both cases, taste for sweetness and clarity as well as sourness. If a turbo shot is sour and thin, grind a step finer or let it run a little longer; if it tastes good, save the recipe and compare it with your usual shot on the same day.

## The grinder matters more here

Both studies point at the grinder. Cameron's group traced the fall in extraction at fine settings to the spread of particle sizes, and Smrke's team found that the share of fines set the shot time. A coarser grind changes both at once, so the same turbo recipe can behave differently on two grinders. Our guides to [grind size in microns](/blog/coffee-grind-size-micron-chart/) and [conical versus flat burrs](/blog/conical-vs-flat-burr-grinders/) cover what changes the spread, and [extraction yield explained](/blog/coffee-extraction-yield-explained/) covers how extraction is measured.
