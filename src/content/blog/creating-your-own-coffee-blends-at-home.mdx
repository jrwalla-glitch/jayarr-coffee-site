A blend is coffee from more than one origin in the same bag. In *Craft Coffee*, Jessica Easto writes that blends done well mix and match beans to make a balanced, consistent cup. The roaster and author Scott Rao lists the other reasons roasters blend: to keep a flavor profile the same all year, to hide an aging or disappointing lot, or to save money. He also calls blending perhaps the easiest and most effective way to improve a coffee's flavor after roasting, and says it can achieve an under-appreciated flavor synergy.

At home, you can do the same with two or three bags.

## What each coffee brings

The video's idea is to find coffees whose characteristics complement each other, and it traces those characteristics to geography, the coffee plant's variety, dry or wet processing, and the roast. It then sorts origins into rough groups:

| What the video says it adds | Origins the video names |
|---|---|
| Brightness, from strong acidity | Mexican Chiapas, Ethiopian Yirgacheffe and others |
| A rich body | Indonesian coffees such as Sumatra or Sulawesi Toraja |
| A thicker mouthfeel | Kenya, Papua New Guinea, Rwanda |
| Pronounced sweetness | Venezuela, Haiti, Panama, El Salvador |
| Distinctive flavors and aromas of their own | Kona, Guatemala, Colombia, Jamaica Blue Mountain |

Treat these as starting points, not rules. A country's coffees vary by region, lot and process; Easto warns that single origins from the same country can taste surprisingly different, and a natural Ethiopian and a washed one can be far apart. The words on each bag are a better guide to what that coffee will add than the country alone, and our guide to [reading a coffee bag label](/blog/how-to-read-coffee-bag-label/) explains them.

## A starting ratio: 40/40/20

The video notes that many blends use a base coffee as the biggest share, with others in smaller amounts, and suggests a three-coffee blend for a first attempt:

- **40% a sweet base,** the coffee the blend is built on.
- **40% a mid-palate coffee,** for example one with fruity notes and the body you want.
- **20% an accent,** for a distinctive flavor and aroma.

Two refinements, one ours and one Rao's:

- **Start with two coffees** (our suggestion). With two, you can tell what each one is doing; add a third once you can.
- **Keep every part at 15% or more.** Rao advises against any component under 15%. Even well mixed, the share of each coffee varies from one small dose to the next: in his example, a component that's 16% of a blend can be 10% of one 20&nbsp;g dose and 20% of another, and at 10% some doses would have just a few percent of it.

## Test the ratio with spoons, not beans

For choosing which coffees to blend and in what ratio, Rao's best hack, as he calls it, is the spoon method. It uses a [cupping](/blog/how-to-cup-coffee-at-home/), which you may already know how to set up:

1. Cup each coffee on its own.
2. Label the bottoms of a few empty cups with ratios, such as 1:1 and 2:1.
3. Make each ratio with spoonfuls from the cupped coffees: three spoonfuls of A, two of B and one of C make a 3:2:1 blend.
4. Taste the mixed cups side by side, and keep the ratio you like best.

You don't have to mix a whole bag to find out, and it shows what the coffees do together rather than what you'd guess from each one alone.

## A weighing worksheet

Once you've picked a ratio, weigh it. These amounts are calculated for a 20&nbsp;g dose and for a 12&nbsp;oz (340&nbsp;g) bag's worth of beans:

| Ratio | One 20 g dose | A 340 g batch |
|---|---|---|
| 40 / 40 / 20 | 8 g / 8 g / 4 g | 136 g / 136 g / 68 g |
| 60 / 40 | 12 g / 8 g | 204 g / 136 g |
| 50 / 25 / 25 | 10 g / 5 g / 5 g | 170 g / 85 g / 85 g |
| 70 / 15 / 15 | 14 g / 3 g / 3 g | 238 g / 51 g / 51 g |

For any other dose, multiply it by each percentage. Where a part is small, weighing it into every dose, rather than scooping from a mixed jar, keeps each cup at the ratio you chose: that's our way around the dose-to-dose variation Rao describes.

## The video's four blends

These are the video's own recipes. Its roast levels are given where it names them.

| Blend | Coffees |
|---|---|
| Filter blend | 40% Colombian, medium roast; 40% Kenyan, light roast; 20% Mexican, dark roast |
| Mocha-Java | 60% Sumatra; 40% Yemen Mocha |
| For chocolate lovers | 50% Brazilian; 25% Sumatra; 25% Yemen Mocha or Ethiopian Sidamo |
| More caffeine | 70% Brazilian arabica; 15% Central American arabica; 15% robusta |

**Mocha-Java** is, in Easto's words, one of the world's oldest blends; Mocha is Yemen's coffee, named after the port of Mokha. Hoffmann writes that coffees from Yemen were often blended with coffees from Java, but the name was never protected, so it became a stylistic term for a roaster's own blend rather than a statement of where the coffees came from. Easto adds that some blends sold as Mocha-Java contain coffee from neither Yemen nor Indonesia. The video's version uses Sumatra in place of Java. Our [Yemen guide](/blog/yemen-coffee-guide-mocha-origin/) and [Indonesian coffee guide](/blog/what-does-indonesian-coffee-taste-like/) cover both halves.

**The robusta blend** is the video's way to raise caffeine without losing flavor. Using our Caffeine Calculator's ranges for beans (0.9–1.3% caffeine by weight for arabica, 1.7–2.5% for robusta), 15% robusta gives the blend about 1.0–1.5% caffeine, roughly 13 to 14% more per gram than all-arabica (calculated). Easto notes that robusta is still used in Italian-style espresso blends, where it's thought to add a bold flavor, extra caffeine and crema. Its two 15% parts sit right at Rao's lower limit, so weigh them into each dose.

## Blend before or after roasting?

If you buy roasted coffee, you're blending after roasting, which is what Rao recommends. He writes: “The problem with blending before roasting is that beans of differing sizes and processes will develop at different rates.” Some can end up underdeveloped or a little too dark while others roast well. Roasting each coffee separately lets each one be roasted to suit it, and lets you adjust the ratio to the batches you actually have.

If you [roast at home](/blog/how-to-roast-coffee-at-home/), Rao says blending before roasting is generally a bad idea unless the coffees are of similar size and processing. He mentions an Italian belief that resting a green blend for a few days evens out its moisture, and says he can't verify it.

## Brewing your blend

Brew a blend as you would any coffee, and brew its parts the same way when you taste them alone. The filter changes what comes through: Hoffmann writes that paper gives the cleanest cup, while a metal filter, like a French press's, lets through fine particles and oils that add body.

## Why a roaster's blend can change

A roaster's blend isn't a fixed recipe. Easto notes that a blend is still seasonal and subject to some variation, and that keeping its flavor consistent month after month takes planning and skill. Hoffmann explains in his buying guide that green coffee from each origin arrives at its own time of year and ages after that, so the lots behind a blend change, and Rao points out that post-roast blending lets a roaster adjust the ratios to suit the roast batches on hand. Your own blend will drift the same way when you buy new bags, so retest the ratio with spoons each time.

## Tips

- **Taste the parts first.** Brew or cup each coffee on its own before you mix, so you know what each brings.
- **Keep notes:** coffees, roast dates, ratio, brew method and what you tasted, so you can repeat a blend you like.
- **Blend small batches.** Mix only what you'll drink while every part is fresh; our [freshness guide](/blog/coffee-freshness-roast-to-stale/) covers the windows.
- **Expect some misses.** The video's own advice: you'll fail a number of times before you get the blend you want.
