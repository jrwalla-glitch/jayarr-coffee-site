All three make cold coffee, but they spend coffee, water and time very differently. The fair way to compare them is glass for glass, counting every gram of water that ends up in the drink: the ice that melts into Japanese iced coffee, the water that dilutes cold brew concentrate, and the water the grounds keep back.

## Japanese iced coffee: the recipe by weight

Japanese iced coffee, also called flash brew, is an ordinary hot pour-over brewed onto ice. The trick is to count the ice as part of the water. Our example starts from the Brew Guide's 12 fl oz V60: 25.3 g of coffee and 406 g of water at 1:16. It pours 244 g of that water hot and puts the other 162 g, about 40%, in the server as ice. This split is our worked example, not a published recipe.

- **Coffee:** 25.3 g, ground medium-fine, like table salt
- **Water:** 244 g, just off the boil, plus 162 g of ice in the server
- **Temperature:** the Brew Guide's V60 kettle temperature: 201°F&nbsp;(94°C) for a medium roast, 205°F&nbsp;(96°C) for a light one. Hario's own iced-coffee instructions use boiling water.
- **Time:** about 3 minutes to set up; the brew itself is shorter than the hot recipe's 3 to 4 minutes, because less water goes through the coffee; up to about 7 minutes in all
- **Makes:** about 355 g, 12 US fl oz (355 ml), once the ice has melted. That's calculated: 244 g of hot water, less about 51 g held back by the grounds, gives about 193 g of coffee, plus 162 g of melted ice.

1. Rinse the paper filter in the V60 over a sink or a mug, and pour the rinse away.
2. Weigh 162 g of ice into the server. Set the V60 on top, add 25.3 g of coffee ground medium-fine, shake it level and tare the scale.
3. Start the timer and pour about 65 g of water to wet every ground. Wait until 0:45.
4. Pour slowly in small circles at the center until the scale reads 244 g.
5. Let it drain onto the ice, lift the V60 off, and swirl the server until the ice has melted.
6. Serve straight away, over fresh ice if you like.

**Why about 40% ice.** Melting ice soaks up a lot of heat, so the share of ice decides how cold the drink ends up. Assuming the coffee reaches the ice at 160–195°F&nbsp;(70–90°C), 40% ice melts completely and leaves the drink at about 35–55°F&nbsp;(2–13°C). With a third of the water as ice, the same coffee would end up near 67°F&nbsp;(19°C), cool rather than cold. With much more ice, some is left unmelted, and less hot water passes through the coffee. (Calculated from the standard heat values for water and melting ice; the arrival temperature is our assumption.)

**Taste and adjust.** Only 244 g of hot water passes through the coffee, against 406 g in the hot recipe. Writing about espresso, Scott Rao notes that running more water through the grounds raises extraction; by the same reasoning, less water through a pour-over tends to lower it. If the iced cup tastes sour or thin, grind a step finer next time; if it's simply weak, use the Brew Guide's stronger 1:15, 27.1 g of coffee for the same water (calculated: 406 ÷ 15). Hario suggests adjusting both the grind and the amount of coffee to taste.

**Hario's own method** for its V60 iced-coffee maker works by the marks on its server rather than by weight: about 150 g of ice in the ice chamber for four cups, a spoonful of coffee (about 12 g) per cup, a 30-second bloom with boiling water, brewing until the coffee reaches the 500 mark, then topping up with ice to the 700 mark.

## Cold brew, glass for glass

Cold brew has to be diluted, so its glass is made in two steps. The Brew Guide's cold brew, which is Jessica Easto's recipe, steeps coffee at 1:6.25 for 12 hours and mixes the concentrate one to one. For a 12 fl oz glass it takes 41.8 g of coffee and 261 g of cold water, which give about 177 g of concentrate, topped up with 177 g of cold water or milk.

That's about 65% more coffee than the iced pour-over for the same glass. The Brew Guide estimates the two at similar strength: about 1.2–1.6% total dissolved solids for the cold brew once mixed, and about 1.36–1.50% for a hot V60 at 1:16. It has no estimate for the iced version, which may extract a little less.

Ice affects the two differently. In Japanese iced coffee, the ice is part of the recipe, and the drink is at its intended strength once it has melted. Cold brew is diluted with water, and any ice in the glass dilutes it further. Easto points out that cold brew is already cold, so ice dilutes it less than it dilutes hot coffee poured over ice.

## Kyoto-style slow drip

A slow-drip tower holds cold water in a top bowl, lets it drip through a bed of coffee in the middle, and collects the coffee below. Hario's version is its Water Dripper; Hario USA lists the clear-frame model (WDCR-6) at 780 ml. Hario's manual gives the method:

- **Coffee:** one level spoon, about 12 g, per cup; the coffee bowl holds enough for 2 to 6 cups. Hario recommends a deep roast, finely ground.
- **Water:** fill the top bowl to the mark for your number of cups, about 140 ml per mark. That's a ratio of about 1:11.7 (calculated: 140 ÷ 12).
- **Prepare:** moisten the coffee evenly, stirring as you pour in a little water; Hario warns that coffee left dry can make a weak brew. Then lay a paper filter on top and wet it, which spreads the drips over the coffee.
- **Drip rate:** about 1 to 1.5 drops per second, set with the tap. The drip slows as the top bowl empties, so Hario suggests starting a little fast.
- **Time:** about 3 to 5 hours.
- **Keep:** stir before serving. Hario says the coffee keeps for about 3 days in its lidded pot in the fridge.

For four cups, that's 48 g of coffee and about 560 ml of water, which makes about 464 g of coffee, about 15¾ US fl oz (calculated, at 2 g kept per gram of coffee). It's ready to drink as it is. For a stronger cup, Hario suggests more coffee, a finer grind, less water and a slower drip; for a lighter one, the reverse.

## Acidity: what the studies measured

Cold brew is often called less acidic. Two studies measured it against hot coffee made from the same beans, and found that it depends on what you measure.

- **Total acid is lower.** Niny Rao and Megan Fuller brewed six coffees both ways at the same ratio, 35 g to 350 ml: cold for 7 hours at room temperature, and hot for 6 minutes in a French press with boiling water. Every cold brew had less titratable acidity, the total amount of acid, than its hot version: roughly 10 to 40 percent less, depending on the coffee and the endpoint (calculated from their tables). Cordoba and colleagues also found less titratable acidity in every cold brew they made, and their three trained tasters scored the cold brews' acidity lower, 1.0–1.7 against 3.0 on a 0-to-5 scale.
- **pH is a different matter.** pH measures how acidic the liquid is, not how much acid it holds. Rao and Fuller found it comparable: 4.96–5.13 for cold brew and 4.85–5.10 for hot. Cordoba measured a higher pH for cold brew, 4.81–4.92 against 4.36–4.44, so slightly less acidic.

Neither study tested Japanese iced coffee. It's brewed hot, so it starts with hot coffee's acids, but how it compares once chilled wasn't measured.

## Caffeine

Caffeine follows the coffee and the dilution. Per 100 ml, the Caffeine Calculator puts cold brew mixed one to one at 45–95 mg, about the same as drip coffee. A large glass of cold brew carries more mainly because it's a large glass. Our [caffeine guide](/blog/caffeine-in-coffee-guide/) has the table.

## Which to choose

- **For a glass now, from coffee you already brew hot:** Japanese iced coffee. It uses the least coffee per glass, and your usual pour-over gear.
- **For a week of glasses from one batch:** cold brew. It takes a few minutes of work and a night in the fridge, and Easto finds it forgiving; it uses the most coffee.
- **For ready-to-drink cold coffee in an afternoon:** slow drip, if you have a tower. It needs a drip rate you keep an eye on, and it keeps for about 3 days.

How each tastes depends on the coffee, so the best test is the same beans brewed two ways side by side. Easto suggests exactly that for cold brew and hot coffee.
