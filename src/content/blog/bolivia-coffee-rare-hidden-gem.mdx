If you've had a Bolivian coffee, you've had something genuinely scarce. James Hoffmann, in *The World Atlas of Coffee*, puts it bluntly: the country's entire production is smaller than that of one of Brazil's larger coffee farms. He also thinks Bolivia has the potential to produce truly great coffee, and already does in very small quantities. If you're new to [single-origin coffee](/blog/what-is-single-origin-coffee/), that guide explains what a label like "Caranavi" tells you.

## How much coffee Bolivia grows

USDA forecasts 80,000 60 kg bags for 2026/27 (Bolivia's coffee year runs April to March), all arabica. Only about 35,000 bags are expected to be exported; the other 45,000, more than half, are drunk in Bolivia (calculated from USDA's balance). For scale, neighboring Peru is forecast at 4.78 million bags, about 60 times as much (calculated).

The crop has shrunk. USDA's series had 140,000 bags as recently as 2011/12, so today's forecast is about 43 percent lower (calculated); Hoffmann gave 81,000 bags for 2016 and wrote that production was "shrinking year on year." The importer Café Imports reports a 1990s peak and a steep decline since, though it adds that production is likely rising again.

## Where coffee grows in Bolivia

Hoffmann writes that Bolivia's coffee regions "have never been strongly defined," so roasters use different names. About 95 percent of the coffee, he writes, comes from the Yungas: the forest that runs down the eastern side of the Andes, from Peru through Bolivia into Argentina. It's where coffee has been grown longest in Bolivia, and it includes some of the highest-altitude coffee in the world. Buyers reach it along the Yungas Road, the single-lane mountain road nicknamed the "Road of Death."

Because the Yungas is so large, roasters usually name a smaller area:

- **Caranavi.** Café Imports calls the La Paz department's Caranavi and La Asunta municipalities the country's most productive area, and the importer Royal Coffee says Caranavi usually supplies the greatest volume of high-quality coffee. Three of the five Bolivian Cup of Excellence winners listed by the Alliance for Coffee Excellence came from there.
- **Coroico and Inquisivi,** two more names Hoffmann says roasters use within the Yungas. Coffee Origins maps Coroico with the North Yungas.
- **Cochabamba and Teoponte,** which Café Imports also lists as growing areas.

Outside the Yungas, Hoffmann notes small amounts of coffee in Santa Cruz and Beni, but at only a few hundred meters above sea level they generally lack the altitude for high-quality coffee.

| Area | Altitude | Harvest | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yungas | 800–2,300 m (2,600–7,600 ft) | July–November | Hoffmann |
| Specialty highlands | 1,200–2,000 m | June–October | Coffee Origins (Mercanta's band; Café Imports', Apex's and Mercanta's calendars) |

The Cup of Excellence lots give a sense of where the best coffee grows: the winners from 2004 to 2009 were grown between 1,200 and 2,100 m.

Our [coffee harvest calendar](/blog/coffee-harvest-calendar/) doesn't include Bolivia yet. Café Imports lists typical US arrivals from January to April, so that's when fresh Bolivian lots are most likely to appear from roasters.

## Why Bolivian coffee is rare

- **Coca.** Hoffmann writes that farmers are increasingly switching from coffee to coca, whose price varies less. US- and Bolivian-funded anti-drug programs encouraged a move to coffee while prices were high in 2010 and 2011, but many farmers went back to coca when coffee prices fell.
- **Geography.** Bolivia is landlocked, which Hoffmann says adds time and cost to exports, and Café Imports adds the difficult mountain terrain.
- **Small farms.** Hoffmann counts about 23,000 families producing coffee on small farms, typically 1.2 to 8 hectares, with exports handled by about 30 private companies. Café Imports gives about 12,000 families, on farms of 1 to 8 hectares; the two count at different times and may count differently.
- **Reputation.** Hoffmann notes that Bolivian coffee once had a name for quality in Europe, less so recently, and that even quality-minded farmers are still giving up coffee.

## The Cup of Excellence and Taza Presidencial

The Alliance for Coffee Excellence lists Bolivian Cup of Excellence results for 2004, 2005 and 2007 to 2009. Every year's winner scored above 90 on the competition's 100-point scale, from 90.06 in 2004 to 93.10 in 2005, and in 2009 the winning Typica, grown at 2,100 m in the Yungas of La Paz, sold for 35.05 US dollars a pound at auction. Hoffmann credits competitions like these with shining a light on Bolivia's best coffees.

Bolivia has since run its own competition, the Taza Presidencial. Royal Coffee describes it as a government effort to promote Bolivian specialty coffee that began in 2015, says top lots have sold for record prices of over 150 US dollars a pound, and reports that the 2021 winner was a Catuai from Cochabamba, grown by Juan Calani Vargas.

## Varieties and processing

Hoffmann writes that most Bolivian coffee is old varieties such as Typica and Caturra, and Café Imports lists Typica and Red Catuai. The Cup of Excellence winners were mostly Typica and Caturra; the 2008 winner listed "Criollo" alongside Caturra. Our [coffee varietal guide](/blog/coffee-varietal-guide/) explains how these varieties are related.

Processing has changed. Hoffmann describes how producers used to pick and pulp their coffee, then carry the pulped coffee to a central processing station; on the way it could freeze, or keep fermenting, and quality suffered. More quality-minded producers now process on their own farms, and the US funded small washing stations as part of its anti-drug program. Café Imports lists both washed and natural coffees. Our [processing guide](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/) explains the difference in the cup.

## What Bolivian coffee tastes like

- **Hoffmann:** the best Bolivian coffees "tend to be very sweet and very clean, but relatively rarely are they particularly fruity."
- **Importers:** the Apex Coffee Imports description Coffee Origins uses lists fruit, chocolate and floral notes for specialty highland lots.
- **Roasters' notes:** across seven Bolivian lots that roasters described from January to March 2026, our Coffee Origins sample found peach and apricot, lemon, apple and mixed berries most often (two lots each).
- **The top end:** the 2009 Cup of Excellence jury described the winner with notes from jasmine, violet and honeysuckle to pineapple, orange, peach and black tea, with semi-sweet chocolate, vanilla and a brisk, citric acidity.

So expect sweetness and clarity first, with stone fruit or citrus in the brighter lots. These are what Bolivian coffee *can* taste like, not a promise about any one bag.

## How to buy and brew it

Bolivian coffee comes and goes from roasters' lists, so it pays to look for:

- **A specific area,** such as Caranavi or Coroico, and a producer or cooperative. Hoffmann says Bolivian coffee is typically traceable to a single farm or cooperative.
- **The variety,** if it's listed: Typica, Caturra or Catuai.
- **The process:** most is washed, but naturals exist.
- **A recent roast date.** Our guide to [coffee freshness](/blog/coffee-freshness-roast-to-stale/) explains how long a bag stays at its best.

A sweet, clean washed Bolivian suits a paper-filter pour-over, which Hoffmann says gives the cleanest cup. Much of it is grown high, and he advises grinding a little finer when you switch to a coffee grown much higher than you usually drink. Our [V60 guide](/blog/ultimate-pour-over-coffee-guide-and-best-v60-technique/) walks through a full recipe.
