Every burr grinder, conical or flat, makes a spread of particle sizes: a main peak near the size you dialed in, plus a smaller population of fines, the particles under about 100&nbsp;µm. What separates grinders is how much of each they make, and the particular burr set can matter more than its shape. This page covers how the two shapes work, what has actually been measured, and what to compare when you shop. For what D10 and D50 mean and how grinds are measured, see our [grind size micron chart](/blog/coffee-grind-size-micron-chart/).

## How the two shapes grind

Jonathan Gagné, an astrophysicist who studies brewing, describes both in *The Physics of Filter Coffee*:

- **Conical burrs** are a cone-shaped inner burr turning inside a ring-shaped outer one. Usually the outer burr is fixed and the inner one turns, by a crank or a motor. The beans are cut and crushed as they fall between the two, and gravity carries the pieces down until they're small enough to leave through the gap at the bottom.
- **Flat burrs** are two stacked disks with a hole in the middle. The beans go in at the center, and the spinning burr flings them outward between the two faces, so the disks' radius sets how far the beans travel while they're cut.

Each has its trade-offs. Gagné writes that conical burrs are usually cheaper to make, need less force and can give the beans a long path without as much engineering, but they grind slowly, about 1 to 5 g a second, which suits hand grinders. Flat burrs have to spin much faster for the beans to travel outward, typically 500 to 1,800 rpm, and can grind from 1 to 80 g a second. A long path and a high throughput need large flat burrs and a strong motor.

Within either shape, the teeth do most of the work. Gagné explains that large burrs often have three zones: a crushing stage that breaks the beans down fast and makes many fines, a cutting stage that brings the pieces to size, and a finishing stage that catches the remaining boulders. He also describes the basic compromise: more cuts make the main peak more uniform, but no cut is perfect, so each one crushes a little coffee into fines.

## Two ways a grind can be "even"

In his 2023 analysis, Gagné measures two separate things:

- **Unimodality** is how few fines a grinder makes for a given average size. He took the share of the coffee's volume finer than 100&nbsp;µm, compared at a median particle size of 340&nbsp;µm, inside the Grind Guide's medium-fine, fine-sand band.
- **Uniformity** is how narrow the main peak is. He measured the width of its coarse side, which grows with the number of boulders.

"Bimodal", meaning two-peaked, isn't a conical signature. Weighed by volume, good grinders commonly show a small fines peak beside the main one. Gagné writes that lower-quality grinders tend to make wide distributions, while higher-quality grinders make narrower or bimodal ones, and his book's example comes from a flat-burr Weber Workshops EG-1. Uman and colleagues, measuring a Mahlkönig EK43 by laser diffraction in 2016, found its grinds "distinctly bimodal" when plotted by surface area. Scott Rao's *Espresso Extraction* says the same of grinding at espresso fineness. So when Gagné calls a grinder more unimodal, he means it makes fewer fines, not that it has one peak.

## What the 24-grinder analysis found

The data came from the Kaffeemacher team, who compared 24 espresso grinders. Marco Wellinger at the Zurich University of Applied Sciences (ZHAW) measured the particle sizes with a Camsize X2 imaging instrument, using one coffee, an 82-point natural Brazilian, across a range of settings and at each grinder's dial-in for a ristretto, an espresso and an allongé. Gagné analyzed the 300 distributions and published his findings in 2023.

- **Shape:** on average, conical burrs were less unimodal and less uniform than flat burrs. The overlap was large, though, and Gagné stresses that this holds only on average: a specific flat burr can easily make more fines and more boulders than a specific conical one.
- **Fines and boulders went together.** Grinders that made fewer fines also tended to make fewer boulders, against the popular idea that one is traded for the other. He suspects burr geometry or alignment drives this.
- **Speed:** the burrs' rpm showed no clear link to either measure, though the conical grinders turned more slowly on average.
- **Size:** within this sample, larger flat burrs didn't grind more evenly. Tooth geometry and coating looked more important. Because a cone's diameter means something different from a flat disk's, he compares sizes only within each shape.
- **Taste:** the Kaffeemacher team blind-tasted a subset of the grinders. The favorites sat in the middle of both measures rather than at an extreme. The Niche Zero, a 63&nbsp;mm conical, and the Baratza Forté, a 54&nbsp;mm flat, both scored near the top, while a grinder with a similar spread rated poorly, which suggests something a particle-size chart can't show, such as clumping or heating, mattered too.

Gagné lists the limits himself. Each grinder was one unit, so alignment is unknown, and it could affect both measures. The data cover espresso settings only, and a grinder's ranking can change with grind size: in Lance Hedrick's laser measurements of three hand grinders, the Mazzer Omega made fines like the Kinu M47 at fine settings and behaved more like the Comandante C40 at coarser ones. He also warns that comparing a handful of distributions from different grinders, coffees or instruments is of very limited use; it takes many measurements per grinder, with one coffee and one instrument.

## Why "flat burrs are more uniform" isn't a settled rule

The average in that dataset is real, but it isn't a rule you can apply to two grinders on a shelf.

- **The evidence is thin.** In *The Physics of Filter Coffee*, published in 2021, Gagné wrote that the evidence on which shape grinds most evenly was still sparse, and that he knew of no systematic public study across a range of grinders and tooth geometries. The 2023 dataset is a large step, but it's one coffee, one unit of each grinder and espresso only.
- **Flat burrs differ widely among themselves.** In Gagné's own scans, his Baratza Forté, with 54&nbsp;mm flat burrs, made a lot more fines than his Weber EG-1 v2 with 80&nbsp;mm SSP "ultra-low-fines" flat burrs. To get similar V60 brew times without astringency, he had to grind the Forté with a coarser main peak. His scans couldn't detect particles under about 0.1&nbsp;mm.
- **Alignment matters, especially for flat burrs.** Gagné calls poor alignment a common problem with both commercial and home flat-burr grinders: burrs that aren't concentric and parallel widen the spread and can stop the grinder going very fine. Uman and colleagues note that EK43 burrs, like any grinder's, can become misaligned, and Rao writes that one or two grounds trapped under a reseated burr can tilt it enough to hurt the grind. Our [burr alignment guide](/blog/burr-alignment-guide/) covers how to check, and when not to.
- **Wear and seasoning change the result.** Rao writes that sharp burrs make narrower distributions, and that dulling burrs make more fines and boulders. New burrs improve at first: Gagné needed about 12&nbsp;lb (5.4&nbsp;kg) of beans to season his Forté and about 24&nbsp;lb (10.9&nbsp;kg) for the EG-1 with SSP burrs, and notes that makers usually don't quote a figure.

## What fewer fines does in the cup

Fines aren't simply bad. Gagné writes that classic espresso relies on them: they slow the water to a workable shot time and help create the drink's mouthfeel. Espresso with fewer fines tastes cleaner, with less mouthfeel, in his words with less body and more easily distinguishable origin character, and it needs a much finer grind, less pressure or some other way to restrict the flow. The 2023 data agree on the grind: the most unimodal grinders needed a much finer median size at dial-in.

In filter coffee, fines slow the drain and can clog the paper, as our [micron chart](/blog/coffee-grind-size-micron-chart/) explains. Whether they hurt the taste is another matter: Gagné writes that it "seems to be a matter of preference", though he argues the amount should be chosen deliberately. So a low-fines grinder is a style of cup, not an upgrade everyone will prefer, and it changes how you dial in.

## Heat, speed and cold beans

A common claim is that conical burrs keep the coffee cooler because they turn slowly. The sources don't support that as a rule. Gagné writes that grinding at a higher rotation rate makes less heat, because the beans pass through faster and have less time to warm, and that a slow rotation keeps coffee between the burrs longer. In his test of the EG-1, grinding at 700&nbsp;rpm and at 1,500&nbsp;rpm needed a 0.6 shift on the dial, after which he couldn't tell the two apart by taste or by extraction. The 2023 data showed no clear link between rpm and evenness.

Bean temperature has measured effects too. Uman and colleagues ground 20&nbsp;g doses at four temperatures, from liquid-nitrogen cold to room temperature, on an EK43, and colder beans gave a narrower distribution and a smaller average size. In Gagné's own scans, only frozen coffee ground noticeably differently, slightly finer. If you grind from the freezer, our [guide to freezing coffee](/blog/how-to-freeze-coffee-beans/) covers how; for static and clumping, see our [RDT guide](/blog/rdt-ross-droplet-technique-grinding/).

## What to compare when you choose

- **Measurements of the exact grinder.** Look for many particle-size measurements across settings, made with one coffee and one instrument, as in the 24-grinder set. A single chart comparing two grinders on different coffees tells you little.
- **What you brew.** Check the maker's own espresso and filter ranges, and whether a low-fines, clean style is what you want in the cup.
- **Adjustment.** Step size, or a stepless dial, affects how finely you can dial in espresso. Our guides to [electric grinders](/blog/top-5-best-electric-coffee-grinders/), [manual grinders](/blog/top-10-best-manual-coffee-grinders/) and [espresso grinders](/blog/best-espresso-grinders-2026/) compare makers' specs.
- **Build and alignment.** Misaligned burrs of either shape grind less evenly, so check what the maker says about alignment and whether you're allowed to adjust it.
