"Honey" doesn't describe the flavor. *Miel*, Spanish for honey, is the producers' word for the mucilage, James Hoffmann explains in *The World Atlas of Coffee*: the sticky layer of fruit that clings to the coffee seed once the skin is off. Many honey coffees do taste sweet, but the name comes from the fruit, not the cup.

## What the honey process is

The process sits between the two classic methods, which our [processing guide](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/) covers in full:

- **Washed:** the skin and all the fruit are removed, and the seeds are fermented and washed clean before drying.
- **Natural:** the whole cherry dries intact.
- **Honey:** a machine strips the skin and some of the flesh, and the seeds go straight out to dry with the rest of the mucilage still on.

Hoffmann describes honey as very similar to Brazil's pulped natural process and used in Central American countries including Costa Rica and El Salvador. Depulping machines can usually be set to leave a chosen share of the flesh on the beans, he writes, so a coffee might be called "one hundred per cent honey" or "twenty per cent honey." The importer Café Imports says honey processing originated in Costa Rica, and that producers in El Salvador and Honduras increasingly make honeys of different levels too. On its process page it adds that the coffee ferments all through drying, until the seeds reach about 11 percent moisture, and that drying takes 18 to 25 days on average.

Usually there's no tank fermentation and no washing, as Chris Kornman of the importer Royal Coffee points out. Any microbial breakdown of the mucilage happens early in drying, on the patio or raised bed.

## What the colors mean, and why they vary

The color names aren't a measure. Café Imports says so on its Costa Rica page: the term "honey" and its variations "will vary from mill to mill." At some mills, it explains, the color is set by removing a certain share of the mucilage before drying; other mills leave all of it on every honey coffee and change the drying technique instead.

Kornman, at Royal Coffee, goes further. Some producers color-code their honeys to show how much fruit is left, white or yellow for less and red or black for more, but "there are no standard definitions for these terms, and as such one producer's honey may be another's semi-washed." A Royal Coffee lot listing adds that the colors refer to the color the mucilage turns as it oxidizes in drying, and that machines able to strip mucilage to a set degree have produced "a veritable rainbow" of names: black, red, orange, yellow and white.

What four Costa Rican producers and mills actually do shows the range. The details come from the importers' own lot sheets, checked October 18, 2026:

| Mill and name | What the mill does | Drying |
|---|---|---|
| Las Lajas (Café Imports): white honey | Mucilage removed by machine, leaving a little (the mill calls it semi-washed); dried in a greenhouse | 3–4 days |
| Las Lajas: yellow honey | Depulped straight onto raised beds, turned 3 or 4 times the first day | 1 week |
| Las Lajas: red honey | All the mucilage left on; left still the first day, then turned twice a day | 7–15 days |
| Las Lajas: black honey | Left still for two days, then turned 3 or 4 times a day | 15–20 days |
| Puente Tarrazú (Royal): yellow honey | A demucilaging machine removes slightly more mucilage than a typical washed depulping | Not given |
| Finca Fátima (Royal): red honey | A short time on the patio, then dried by machine to 11% moisture | Not given |
| Beneficio La Eva (Royal): "Black Fire Honey" | Cherries macerated 24 hours before depulping, then the sticky seeds macerated again under plastic on the patio, which turns the mucilage black; then patio and raised beds | Not given |

Compare the two yellow honeys. At Las Lajas, run by Oscar and Francisca Chacón, yellow is a fast-drying honey; Café Imports' sheet says the couple make their range of honeys by changing the drying. At the Puente Tarrazú micro mill, yellow means slightly less mucilage than a washed coffee keeps after depulping. Same name, quite different coffees.

## What the colors tend to do in the cup

Café Imports' process page describes honey coffees as able to show fruity, pulpy or jammy flavors, caramel or burnt-sugar sweetness and nuttiness. It says honey keeps some of a natural's heavy body, sweet fruitiness and lower acidity. It ranks the common names from yellow or golden honey, "the mildest, perhaps closest to Washed coffees," through red to black, which it calls "arguably most comparable to the Brazilian Pulped Natural." Hoffmann writes that the fruit left on a pulped natural gives a noticeable increase in sweetness and body.

The general idea: more fruit left on, or slower drying, gives the microbes feeding on the mucilage's sugars more to work with. Some of what they make ends up in the bean, as our [processing guide](/blog/coffee-processing-methods-explained/) explains. That's the usual reasoning behind the darker names' heavier, sweeter reputation. But variety, altitude, the mill's skill and the roast all count, so a color sets an expectation, not a flavor.

Two red honeys from the lot sheets above show how much room the name leaves. Café Imports lists burnt sugar, panela and dried pear, with syrupy sweetness and winey acidity, for a Las Lajas red honey SL-28. Royal Coffee lists pineapple, raspberry, green mango, almond and dark chocolate for Finca Fátima's red honey Catuaí.

## The producer's side: risk, labor and water

Honey is a trade-off for the farm:

- **Risk.** With more flesh left on the beans, Hoffmann writes, there is a higher risk of fermentation and defects as the coffee dries. Café Imports notes the exposed fruit takes more labor in drying to keep taints from developing; the Las Lajas sheet describes turning the beds on a set schedule to bring the moisture down.
- **Speed.** Café Imports adds that honey coffees dry faster than full naturals, with less risk of spoilage and mold than whole cherries on the patio.
- **Water.** Hoffmann writes that honey processing uses even less water than pulped natural, and Royal Coffee's lot sheets note that drying with the mucilage on removes the need to wash the coffee.
- **Looks.** The green beans can look uneven, Café Imports warns, which some roasters find off-putting.

## Honey and pulped natural

The two are close cousins. Hoffmann credits Brazil's pulped natural to experiments by the equipment maker Pinhalense, aimed at high cup quality with less water than washing, and dates its introduction in Brazil to the early 1990s. Kornman writes that Pinhalense claims the first *cereja descascado*, Portuguese for "dehusked cherry," in 1991.

Costa Rica's color names came later, with its micro mills. Royal Coffee dates Costa Rica's "micro-mill revolution" to the early 2000s, and Café Imports writes that the Chacóns bought their own depulper in 2005 and were among the first Costa Rican farmers to make honey and natural specialty coffees. So Brazil's pulped natural is older, but by roughly a decade, not generations. As Kornman says of honey, pulped natural and semi-washed, "Sometimes they all mean exactly the same thing."

## Where honey coffees come from

Costa Rica is still the name most tied to honey coffees, and Café Imports' list of the country's processes runs washed, natural and honey. Our [Costa Rica guide](/blog/what-does-costa-rican-coffee-taste-like/) covers its regions and micro mills. Hoffmann also names El Salvador, and Café Imports adds Honduras.

## Reading a honey coffee's bag

- **The color is the mill's name, not a measurement.** Read it as "this mill's yellow," not as a fixed amount of mucilage.
- **The mill or farm tells you more.** A bag that names the producer lets you look up how they make their honeys, or ask the roaster.
- **Ask Kornman's questions.** How much fruit is left on the seed after pulping? How much water is used? What depulping machinery? A roaster who buys honey coffees should know.
- **No color at all?** Then the bag only tells you the coffee dried with some fruit on. Expect more sweetness and body than a washed coffee as a tendency, and judge by the cup.

## Brewing a honey coffee

Brew it as you would any coffee of the same roast. Our Brew Guide sets its starting kettle temperature and grind by brewer and roast; it doesn't ask how a coffee was processed. If the cup comes out sour or bitter, change the grind first, one step at a time.
