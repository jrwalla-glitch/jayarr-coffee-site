Our September 2019 video looked at ten of the world's most expensive coffees: where they come from, how they taste and whether they're worth the price. Prices like these move every year, so this page keeps the video's list, its order and its verdicts, and puts a dated figure from a seller or an auction beside each 2019 price.

Auction prices are for green, unroasted coffee, often sold by the box, and two of these auctions raised money for charity. Retail prices are for roasted coffee. So the two columns don't compare like for like; they show what each name has fetched.

## 1. Finca El Injerto, Guatemala

The video put El Injerto at 500 dollars a pound and described a sweet, fruity, earthy cup with a hint of chocolate, a smooth but heavy body and a buttery, milky texture.

**What we found.** El Injerto is a family farm in Huehuetenango; its website says Jesús Aguirre Panamá acquired the land in 1874 and began growing coffee around 1900, and that the third and fourth generations of the Aguirre family run it now. It sells its rarest lots through its own private auction; the Alliance for Coffee Excellence (ACE) presented the latest one it lists, held on July 23, 2024, where the top price was 100 dollars a pound, for a 50 lb lot of dry-processed Mocca; its Geisha lots sold for about 26 to 69 dollars a pound, and its Pacamaras for about 14 to 39. We found no record of a sale at the video's 500 dollars a pound, so treat that figure as unconfirmed.

El Injerto's lots also reach ordinary roasters: Stumptown, for one, says its Direct Trade buying began in 2003 with the Aguirre family.

## 2. Kopi luwak, Indonesia

The video described kopi luwak as coffee from beans eaten and excreted by civets, with reduced bitterness and a mellow, floral cup reminiscent of black tea, and put it at 500 to 700 dollars a kilogram.

**What we found.** The price is the least of the problems:

- **Welfare.** Two peer-reviewed surveys of civet coffee tourism plantations in Bali found wild-caught civets kept in cages in poor conditions. Carder and colleagues (2016) assessed 48 civets at 16 plantations; in Lewis-Whelan and colleagues' 2024 survey of 29 plantations and 99 civets, "no civet met all of the five freedoms" of animal welfare.
- **No way to check "wild".** National Geographic reported in 2016 that "there's now no way to tell whether a bag of kopi luwak was made from wild or caged civets." Lab tests can show whether beans passed through a civet, not whether it was caged.
- **Fakes.** James Hoffmann writes in *The World Atlas of Coffee* that "the forgery of this coffee is quite commonplace. Several times more is sold than produced."

Hoffmann's advice is to avoid it and spend the money on a stunning coffee instead. Our [kopi luwak guide](/blog/kopi-luwak-truth-ethical-fraud/) has the studies in full, including how labs test for fakes. We haven't linked a seller, and an earlier link to an Amazon search for kopi luwak is gone.

## 3. Jamaica Blue Mountain

The video described Jamaica Blue Mountain as mild, slightly fruity and not very bitter, at around 60 to 80 dollars a pound, and said over 80 percent of it went to Japan.

**What we found.** Hoffmann writes that only coffee grown between 900 and 1,500 m (3,000 and 4,900 ft) in the parishes of Saint Andrew, Saint Thomas, Portland and Saint Mary can be called Jamaica Blue Mountain, and that for a long time most of it was sold to Japan. We couldn't find a current source for the video's 80 percent. It's a tiny crop: USDA forecasts 15,000 60 kg bags for all of Jamaica in 2026/27, under 0.01 percent of the world total (calculated).

Prices have risen. Volcanica Coffee's single-estate beans, which it describes as 100 percent certified Blue Mountain coffee from Jamaica's regulator, JACRA, were listed at $119.99 to $149.99 for 16 oz when checked October 18, 2026, depending on the estate. The same seller listed a "Jamaica Blue Mountain Blend" at $54.99 for 16 oz when checked the same day, and says it contains 30 percent Blue Mountain beans, which is exactly why the label matters (see below).

Hoffmann's verdict on the cup is cool. Jamaican coffees, he writes, are clean, sweet and very mild, but lack the complexity expected of specialty coffee and can't really compete with the very best from Central and South America or East Africa; their reputation owes a lot to producing, and cleverly marketing, clean, sweet coffees before others did. He also warns that a fair amount of coffee is fraudulently mislabeled as Blue Mountain.

## 4. Los Planes, El Salvador

The video described Los Planes as an award-winning single-estate coffee: sweet butterscotch with a tangerine twist, a medium body with lemon-like acidity, a hint of milk chocolate and nuttiness, and a shortbread finish with baking spice. At around 40 dollars a pound, it was "one coffee we highly recommend trying." That verdict stands: of the ten, it's the coffee the video recommended.

**What we found.** Finca Los Planes is in Chalatenango, farmed by Sergio and Isabel Ticas. Its Pacamara placed second in El Salvador's 2006 Cup of Excellence with 93.52 points, by ACE's farm directory. PT's Coffee, which says the Ticases became its first Direct Trade partners nearly 20 years ago, lists a Los Planes Bourbon Honey, a light roast with notes of mandarin, hazelnut cream and black tea, at $19.50 for 10 oz and $54.25 for 2 lb, but shown as sold out when checked October 18, 2026. That's about 27 to 31 dollars a pound (calculated), less than the video's figure.

## 5. Black Ivory Coffee, Thailand

The video described Black Ivory as coffee cherries passed through elephants, with fruity notes and hints of butter, leather, malt and earth, a chocolate-like aroma as it cools, and a long caramel-and-chocolate finish. It put the price at over 1,000 dollars a pound and the output at 150 kilograms a year, and its verdict was that Black Ivory "ranks top among the most distinctive cups of coffee you'll ever taste."

**What we found.** Black Ivory Coffee says elephants in Surin province eat Thai arabica cherries, that digestion takes 12 to 72 hours, and that about 33 kg of cherries are needed for 1 kg of finished coffee; its home page says each harvest yields only a few hundred kilograms. When checked October 18, 2026, it listed its 1 lb box at $1,700 and 1 kg at $3,500, beside a cheaper "Mahout's Blend" line. It lists the notes of its own coffee as chocolate, tamarind and plum.

The maker says its elephants "enjoy life in the beautiful Thai landscape" and that each purchase supports the mahouts who tend them; we found no independent welfare study of its operation like those for civet coffee. Hoffmann's broader advice is that people "should avoid all animal-processed coffees," which takes in elephants as well as civets.

If you want to try it anyway, the maker sells direct, and Amazon lists its 40 g box: <a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Black+Ivory+Coffee&tag=jayarrcoffee-20" rel="sponsored noopener">Black Ivory Coffee on Amazon</a>.

## 6. HR-61, Hacienda El Roble, Colombia

The video described HR-61 as a single-estate coffee named after its lot and farm, with floral notes, a clean finish, a creamy body, lemon-lime acidity and undertones of milk chocolate and caramel. It said only 22 kilos were produced a year, that one roaster sold it, through cupping sessions at its café, and that it cost 100 dollars for a 100 g bag or 30 dollars a cup.

**What we found.** Daily Coffee News described HR-61 as a certified organic and Bird Friendly cultivar that Hacienda El Roble, in Santander, says is exclusive to its estate. In 2018, the charity Grounds for Health's auction website said earlier HR-61 crops had only been sold roasted, by Australia's Proud Mary. Donated HR-61 lots set that charity auction's record twice: 120.05 dollars a pound in 2018, and 151.10 dollars a pound for 26.5 lb in 2019. We found no source for the 22 kg a year.

## 7. Ospina Dynasty Grand Café, Colombia

The video described Ospina's Dynasty Grand Café Premier Grand Cru as Colombian Typica grown at 7,500 feet in Antioquia, hand-picked, fermented and sun-dried, with a creamy, bold body, notes of chocolate, coconut, berries and macadamia, and an aroma of peach, jasmine, almond, caramel and orange, at 150 to 750 dollars a pound.

**What we found.** Ospina Coffee's website says it produces and roasts only "rare and scarce Arabica Typica beans," grown on the mountain slopes of the Andes, and calls itself the work of five generations; it quotes the *Robb Report* naming it the world's finest coffee. When checked October 18, 2026, the site listed no products or prices, and we found no current seller. We couldn't confirm the video's altitude or price.

## 8. Hacienda La Esmeralda, Panama

The video described Esmeralda's Geisha as a fresh, citrusy, tea-like coffee whose floral sweetness gives way to peach, pomegranate and strawberry, priced at about 100 dollars a pound because the harvest is so small. Its verdict: many experts call it the most unique and distinctive coffee ever grown.

**What we found.** This is where the record prices are. At the 2004 Best of Panama, Esmeralda's Geisha drew a then-record bid of 21 dollars a pound, Hoffmann writes, and the record kept climbing. The Specialty Coffee Association of Panama reported that at the 2025 auction, a 20 kg lot of Esmeralda's washed Geisha sold for 30,204 dollars a kilogram, about 13,700 dollars a pound (calculated). In 2026 the top lot, Lamastus Family Estate's Elida Geisha Torre, sold for 18,004 dollars a kilogram. Hoffmann credits Esmeralda's prices mainly to the coffee's quality, while granting that demand and great marketing played a part, and describes the record-breaking coffee as extremely floral and citrusy with a light, tea-like body.

Esmeralda also sells roasted coffee directly. Its shop listed a set of two 100 g bags, one washed and one natural Geisha, at $220 including worldwide shipping when checked October 18, 2026, shown as sold out. That's about 500 dollars a pound (calculated). Our [Panama guide](/blog/panama-coffee-guide-geisha-boquete/) covers the auction year by year, and our [Gesha guide](/blog/gesha-coffee-worth-the-price/) asks what the premium buys.

## 9. El Morito, Guatemala

The video named this one El Morito, Finca de Fátima, at 44 dollars a pound, and said it was hard to buy in most countries because Taiwanese and Japanese buyers usually took it at auction. It described a clean, heavy body with layered flavor: soft citrus, balanced acidity, and hints of vanilla, walnut, caramel, jam, butterscotch, baker's chocolate and cherry.

**What we found.** The closest match we found is Finca El Morito, in Mataquescuintla, Jalapa, run by Roberto Monterroso; we couldn't confirm the "Fátima" in the video's name or its claim about the buyers. ACE's auction page says Monterroso won first place in Guatemala's 2022 Cup of Excellence with a washed Gesha, and its farm directory lists that lot, ranked 1B, at 91.75 points. When checked October 18, 2026, ACE's results page for its Legends of Excellence charity auction showed a 22.05 lb box of El Morito's washed Geisha sold for 190 dollars a pound.

## 10. St Helena

The video said St Helena's coffee, grown on a small island in the middle of the South Atlantic, was known from Starbucks' shelves, came from plants originally from Yemen, and was complex, fruity and winey with hints of spice, chocolate and caramel, at around 145 dollars a pound or more.

**What we found.** Daily Coffee News reported in August 2016 that Starbucks was releasing 204 bags of 8.8 oz at 80 dollars each, which is about 145 dollars a pound (calculated), and that its 220 lb was, Starbucks said, a third of the island's output for that crop year. The coffee is widely considered a green-tipped Bourbon that reached the island in the early 1700s, through the East India Company, by way of the port of Mocha in Yemen. (Yemen is on the Arabian Peninsula, not in Africa, as the video put it.) Today the UK seller Sea Island Coffee sells several St Helena estates; when checked October 18, 2026, it listed Bamboo Hedge Estate at £72 and Wranghams Estate at £77 for 125 g, roughly £260 to £280 a pound (calculated).

## Read the label before you pay

The higher the price, the more a label is worth checking:

- **"Blend" is a different product.** A "Jamaica Blue Mountain Blend" contains some Blue Mountain coffee, but it isn't 100 percent. Hawaii writes the rule into law. Under Hawaii Revised Statutes §486-120.6, "100% Kona Coffee" or "Kona Coffee" means all of the coffee is from Kona; a blend that uses the name must print the Kona percentage, as in "10% Kona Coffee Blend," and needs at least 10 percent Kona coffee by weight until June 30, 2027, and 51 percent from July 1, 2027. The same applies to Hawaii's other named origins, such as Ka'u; our [Ka'u guide](/blog/ka-u-hawaii-specialty-coffee-region/) explains the rule.
- **A famous name can be faked.** Hoffmann warns of mislabeled Blue Mountain and of kopi luwak forgery.
- **A variety name isn't a guarantee.** World Coffee Research warns that several genetically distinct coffees have been called Geisha, so one sold under the name may not share Panamanian Geisha's genetics; see our [Gesha guide](/blog/gesha-coffee-worth-the-price/).
- **Auction lots rarely reach a shelf.** A retail bag from a famous farm is usually not the lot that set the record.

## What makes a coffee expensive

The ten show the main reasons, and they often stack:

- **Scarcity.** Black Ivory needs about 33 kg of cherries for 1 kg of coffee, by the maker's count; by Starbucks' figures, St Helena's whole 2016 crop came to about 660 lb (calculated).
- **Competition.** A high score at the Best of Panama or a Cup of Excellence sends a lot to auction, where buyers bid against each other.
- **The cost of growing.** Hoffmann writes that land prices and Panama's more protective labor laws make it an expensive place to grow coffee, and Jamaica's Blue Mountain region is small and tightly defined.
- **Reputation and story.** Hoffmann credits marketing alongside quality at Esmeralda, and calls Blue Mountain the subject of one of the most successful pieces of marketing in coffee's history.

Price tells you what buyers paid, not how a coffee will taste to you. Hoffmann's point about kopi luwak applies more widely: a quarter of what you'd spend on a bag "could instead buy you a stunning coffee from one of the very best producers in the world."

## Brewing an expensive bag

If you do buy one, brew it with care. A delicate, light-roasted lot such as a Geisha suits a paper-filter pour-over; Hoffmann notes that paper filters give the cleanest cup. For a light roast in a V60, the Brew Guide starts at 1:16 with the kettle at 205°F&nbsp;(96°C). Taste, then adjust one thing at a time: if it's sour, grind a step finer; if it's bitter or drying, a step coarser.
