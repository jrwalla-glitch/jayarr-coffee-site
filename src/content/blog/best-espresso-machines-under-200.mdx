A machine listed under $200 buys you an espresso maker, not espresso mastery, and the machine is only half of it. With pre-ground coffee you're limited to a pressurized basket, and a blade grinder has no grind setting to adjust. Paired with a grinder that can grind fine and evenly, the same machine is a real way to learn espresso and decide whether it's worth spending more later.

This guide uses the makers' own US list prices and specifications, checked on October 15, 2026, and the data behind our Espresso Machine Chooser. It doesn't use sale, marketplace or refurbished prices, which change daily. It's a research comparison, not a test of each machine.

## Pump machines, lever presses and steam brewers

Three kinds of machine call themselves espresso makers at this price, and they make pressure in different ways.

- **Pump machines.** An electric pump pushes hot water through the coffee. The Stilosa and the Dedica Duo both list a 15-bar pump. That's the pump's rating, not the pressure on the coffee: Scott Rao explains that flow through an espresso puck peaks somewhere between 7 and 9 bar at the group, and Breville and Gaggia both describe extraction at 9 bar on their machines.
- **Manual lever presses.** You make the pressure yourself by pressing a lever, and you heat the water in a kettle. The Flair Classic is one.
- **Steam brewers.** Some of the cheapest "espresso makers" have no pump: they heat water in a sealed tank, and the steam pressure pushes it through the coffee. James Hoffmann notes that the first espresso machines worked this way, and that the pressure you can get from steam alone, without danger, is relatively low. The breakthrough, he writes, was Achille Gaggia's lever, whose sudden jump in pressure allowed a much finer grind. A steam brewer makes strong coffee on the same principle as a [moka pot](/blog/moka-pot-brewing-guide/), not espresso.

So before you buy, check the listing for a pump, usually given in bar. If there's no pump and no lever, it's a steam brewer.

## The grinder decides more than the machine

Scott Rao calls the grinder "the most important piece of equipment in an espresso bar." A good espresso grinder, he writes, produces the particle sizes that give the water enough resistance, and limits fines. Espresso also needs a grind you can change in small steps; a blade grinder has no grind setting at all, only a run time. When the bed resists unevenly, water finds the easy paths, which our guide to [channeling](/blog/fix-channeling-uneven-extraction/) explains.

Our Espresso Machine Chooser counts the grinder in the budget for any machine that needs one. In its data, the lowest-priced espresso grinders, the <a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Baratza+Encore+ESP+grinder&tag=jayarrcoffee-20" rel="sponsored noopener">Baratza Encore ESP</a> and the Breville Smart Grinder Pro, were each listed at about $200 when checked October 15, 2026. Baratza says the Encore ESP gives "the grind resolution you need to brew creamy, syrupy shots of espresso." With either one, a machine listed at about $150 is about a $350 setup (calculated).

A hand grinder can cost less, but check that its maker rates it for espresso. 1Zpresso's own page for the Q2, which this guide used to suggest, doesn't list espresso among its uses. ## Two features to understand before you buy

**Pressurized baskets.** Some machines come with a dual-wall, or pressurized, basket; Breville's Bambino includes both kinds, and its manual explains the idea: use dual-wall baskets with pre-ground coffee, because they regulate the pressure and help the extraction whatever the grind, dose, tamp pressure or freshness, and use single-wall baskets with fresh-ground beans so you can "experiment with grind, dose and tamp." The forgiving basket also mutes the effect of changing the grind, so once you have an espresso grinder, the single-wall basket is the one that shows you what's happening.

**Frothing wands and steam wands.** A frothing wand (a panarello) draws air into the milk for you; it's easier but less precise. A bare manual steam wand needs practice, but it gives you the most control over the texture. Our [milk-steaming guide](/blog/how-to-steam-milk-for-lattes/) covers the technique.
